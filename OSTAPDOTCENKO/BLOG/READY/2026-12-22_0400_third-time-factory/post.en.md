> Third time lucky. The factory agrees, and invoices each time separately. — From the author’s notebook, section “Accounting and Theology”

Nine times in this column I’ve written about other people’s restarts. Luo Yonghao and his last startup that refuses to be the last. The teacher who went into retail. Coffee with a side of fraud. A man who has been coming home “next week” for years. Yellow bikes, a pig the wind stopped holding up, a console maker that loaded its last save, a phone that burned out on the job, and a ride-hailing company that reached the stock exchange and promptly turned around.

Writing about other people’s failures is easy and pleasant. A bit like giving renovation advice while sitting in someone else’s flat.

Time to clean up my own room. This last piece is about what I’ve seen over years of working with Chinese factories. No names or dates: the names don’t matter, and the dates are always the same — “again.”

## A question shaped like a box

Let’s start with the first time. In Chinese it’s called dayang (打样), “making a sample.”

When I was younger, I honestly believed a sample was the product, just one of it. You send the spec, they send a box, you open the box, and inside is what you wanted. As logical as the times table.

The very first box taught me that factories have their own times table. Inside was not what I wanted but what they understood from my spec. Those are two different things, and the distance between them is measured not in millimeters but in adjectives. “Blue.” Which blue? “Durable.” How durable? “Like the photo.” Which of the four photos?

A first sample isn’t a product. It’s a question shaped like a box: “Is this what you meant?”

The right answer to that question is almost never “yes.” That’s not failure. That’s the start of the conversation.

## Mr. Chabuduo

Speaking of adjectives. Chinese has the word chabuduo (差不多): “roughly,” “close enough,” “that’ll do.” In the early twentieth century, the philosopher Hu Shi wrote a satire called “The Biography of Mr. Chabuduo,” about a man for whom everything was “more or less the same,” including, at the end, life and death.

I used to think this was a purely Chinese problem. Then I reread my early specs. Mr. Chabuduo had co-written every page. “Roughly this size.” “A similar shade.” “Good quality.” The factory honestly made it roughly, similar and good. By its own standards.

If you don’t want “roughly,” don’t write “roughly.” A simple thought. It took me years to put it into words and a couple more to start following it.

## A sample is made by a craftsman, a run by a process

The second time is shichan (试产), the pilot run.

Hardware engineers slice this path even finer and give the stages acronyms. EVT checks whether the design works at all, usually a hundred to a thousand units. DVT checks whether it holds up when built on real production tooling. PVT checks whether it holds up at real line speed, and that’s thousands of units meant for actual customers. Only then comes MP, mass production.

Note that none of these clever people expect to get it right the first time. They put three attempts into the plan from the start. That isn’t pessimism. It’s experience, translated into a schedule.

Why? Because a sample is made by a craftsman. A production run is made by a process. A craftsman can file, bend and glue until the thing works. A machine doesn’t improvise. A machine only remembers what it was told.

One good unit proves the craftsman is talented. A thousand identical ones prove the process is.

## A box with two signatures

Between the second and third time, there’s a ritual I love most. It’s called fengyang (封样), the “sealed sample.”

When everything finally works, the best unit doesn’t go into production or onto a shelf for visitors. It gets sealed. Both sides sign it. One stays with the customer, one at the factory. From then on, any dispute is settled not with words but with a line-up: here’s the batch, here’s the sample, compare.

I’ve signed plenty of contracts. A contract can be interpreted. A sealed sample can’t: it just sits there and silently compares.

If you ask me what the most valuable document is when working with a Chinese factory, I’ll say: a box with two signatures on it. It states what things should be, not what they seemed to be on the day you signed.

The third time is liangchan (量产), mass production.

You’d think that’s it. But even here, the first batch is a bit of a sample. You need to see it yourself, with your own eyes, not in photos. Not because the factory will cheat you. Because between “we made two hundred good ones” and “we made twenty thousand good ones” lies one more distance, and this one is measured not in adjectives but in patience.

## “Let’s start over” is a production term

Now the main point. Why all this is normal.

Colleagues who are just starting out with China often ask me: can’t we do it right the first time? Find a good factory, explain everything properly and get it right on the first try? I answer honestly: you can. Sometimes. The way you can sometimes win the lottery. I just wouldn’t build a business plan on it.

In my experience, in China a redo isn’t a disgrace. It’s a production step. It’s in the plan. It has a name, a deadline and a budget. The shameful thing isn’t redoing. It’s pretending you won’t need to.

Come to think of it, that’s what this whole column has been about. Every one of its heroes redid something: companies, products, business models, themselves. Some succeeded, some didn’t, for some it’s too early to say. But none of them got it right the first time.

“Let’s start over” isn’t a cry of despair. It’s a production term. At a factory, people say it calmly, without an exclamation mark.

Allow me one last intermission. A toast. In China, a business dinner without toasts isn’t a dinner, it’s a meeting with chopsticks. I wrote this one myself. I say it in my head, and out loud depending on how much ganbei has happened.

*“Friends. To the first sample, which was wrong. Without it, we’d never have learned what we wanted.*

*To the second, which was almost right. Without it, we’d never have learned what the machine wanted.*

*To the third, which was exactly right. We’ll seal it, sign it and never open it again.*

*And a separate toast to Mr. Chabuduo. May he live a long and happy life, preferably in some other industry.*

*Ganbei!“*

That’s about it. No, not quite.

When I started this column, I thought I was writing about failures. It turned out to be about something else. About people and companies who open the box for the third, fifth, sixth time, see it’s not right, sigh, and sit down to write a new spec. A more precise one than last time.

The box is what went wrong. Starting over is what people do.

This column ends here. But I suspect this was only the sample.
