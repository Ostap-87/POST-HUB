> “This is the last time” — the most frequently repeated sentence in human history. — Anyone who has ever quit smoking

Like any grown-up, I’ve had plenty of last times. The last time I go to a trade show in uncomfortable shoes. The last time I sign a contract without reading Appendix 3. The last time I believe a supplier who says “no problem.” All of these last times are alive and well and recur on schedule. So I feel real tenderness for a Chinese founder who announced “this is my last startup” and apparently believed it.

His name is Luo Yonghao. Everyone in China knows him. Elsewhere, people know his phones — beautiful, odd, and not quite profitable.

## A failure counted down to the day

Let’s start with a list. Think of it as an obituary in reverse: everyone’s still alive.

In 2006, Luo launched a blogging platform, Bullog. Closed. In 2008, an English school, Laoluo English. Lost money for years, closed. In 2012, Smartisan: phones and an operating system with real design ambitions. In 2018, Smartisan released the TNT, a voice-controlled desktop priced in the tens of thousands of yuan, and Bullet Message, a chat app meant to take on WeChat. The messaging team was disbanded after 198 days. In 2019 came an e-cigarette. It was dropped in under six months — faster than most smokers drop the habit.

Note the number: 198. Not “about six months.” One hundred and ninety-eight. A failure counted down to the day is already a small success. It shows good bookkeeping.

## The legend of really paying back

Speaking of bookkeeping. Smartisan was shut down at the end of 2018. Chinese media put its debts at around 600 million yuan, and Luo had personally guaranteed the company. He landed on China’s “high consumption” blacklist: no flights, no first-class seats on high-speed trains, no nice hotels. The man who built phones for aesthetes took the slow train. Literally, and a bit figuratively too.

In 2019, ByteDance bought Smartisan’s patents. In spring 2020, Luo went live on Douyin and started selling everything. His very first stream reportedly charged brands 15 million yuan for a slot.

Chinese netizens nicknamed his saga “The Legend of Zhen Huan” — except “Zhen Huan” was punned into “really paying back.” The original palace drama is about concubines and intrigue. The concubines never repaid anyone.

In January 2022, the court enforcement records against Luo disappeared from public databases. His team was quick to add that this didn’t mean everything was repaid: he was “still working and paying according to plan.” That’s honesty. Usually it’s the other way round: nothing’s paid off, but the party has already happened.

Debt is your past taking out a loan against your future.

## The last startup with the most sequels

And here, in 2022, comes the main act. Luo quits social media and founds Thin Red Line to build AR glasses. And he says the sentence this whole essay is about: “This will probably be my last venture. I’m putting everything into it.”

Investors believed him. The angel round was about $50 million at a valuation of about $200 million, with Meituan, DJI, top funds and big-name founders writing personal checks.

Then the familiar plot. The prototype was too heavy to mass-produce. He planned a thousand employees; the team peaked at about 150. In early 2024, according to press reports, about half were laid off. The company quietly pivoted from AR to AI. In January 2025 it released a beta of J1 Assistant — not even listed in app stores. The response was, let’s say, polite.

The last startup turned out to have the most sequels.

An intermission. A short list, inspired by this story and a dozen similar ones you hear over tea in Shenzhen and Shanghai.

Six signs your startup is your last: 1. You told the press. 2. You told your wife. 3. The press believed you. Your wife did not. 4. You’ve invested everything, including money you haven’t made yet. 5. Investors no longer ask “when’s the product?” but “how are you feeling?” 6. You’ve already named the next one.

## Laughing with him, not at him

And yet I’m not laughing at Luo. I’m laughing with him.

Because in June 2025, a livestream on Baidu ran for over six hours hosted not by Luo but by his AI avatar. It drew over 13 million views and about 55 million yuan in sales, beating the real Luo’s May stream in several categories. Baidu says the AI produced 97,000 words of product descriptions in one session.

For years he dreamed of building a product that sells better than he does. He finally did. The product is him.

Then, in September 2025, Luo picked a fight with the restaurant chain Xibei, saying nearly everything on its pricey menu was pre-made, and offered 100,000 yuan for proof. Xibei threatened to sue and opened its kitchens to the public. The public found, among other things, frozen broccoli with a two-year shelf life. Within days Xibei apologized and promised to cook eight dishes fresh on site. Meanwhile, work on a national standard requiring restaurants to disclose pre-made food picked up speed.

I’ve written a lot about central kitchens, and I know that thawing isn’t a crime. What struck me was something else. A man who has been frozen and reheated by life so many times can tell fresh from microwaved better than anyone.

## Failure is a comma, not a full stop

Some will say: what kind of role model is this? Failure after failure. I’d say that in China, failure is a comma, not a full stop. Falling isn’t the shameful part. Staying down is.

Luo is now in his fifties. His last startup is still looking for its product. His avatar sells without lunch breaks. As for the debt saga, I confess I’ve stopped keeping track. I hope it gets a happy ending.

And I look at my own last times. They’re smaller. None of them cost 600 million. None of them started a national conversation about broccoli.

Maybe that’s for the best. Or maybe my last time just hasn’t come yet.
