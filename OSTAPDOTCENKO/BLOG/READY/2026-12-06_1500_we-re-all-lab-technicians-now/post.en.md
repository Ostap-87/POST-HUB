> Research is what I’m doing when I don’t know what I’m doing. — Attributed to Wernher von Braun. The gray market took it literally

Let me say this up front, as an honest man and as a marketer who’s occasionally embarrassed by his trade. This essay contains no addresses, no shop names and no doses. If that’s what you came for, you’re in the wrong place. This is about how a small line on a label became the most effective slogan of the decade, even though lawyers wrote it, not copywriters.

The line reads: “For research use only. Not for human consumption.”

I’ve written labels, banners and taglines for twenty years. I know how to make people want a product: promise a result. How to make them want it by promising it’s not for them at all — that I never learned. China’s peptide exporters did.

The scheme has been described in the US press and in regulators’ documents. Chemical plants in China produce peptides, short chains of amino acids, including copies of fashionable weight-loss drugs and molecules that aren’t approved anywhere yet. They ship them as reagents, lab supplies or “research chemicals.” On the other side of the ocean, resellers put the powder into vials, attach a certificate of analysis from a private lab and sell through websites, messaging apps and private groups. On the vial: those four words.

As a result, America now has more researchers than at any point in the history of science. Many of their labs have a shower curtain.

## Who brought what

The numbers, because otherwise it reads like satire.

According to US customs data cited by The New York Times, imports of hormone and peptide compounds from China nearly doubled in the first nine months of 2025: $328 million versus $164 million a year earlier. That’s only what was declared. Customs, for obvious reasons, doesn’t count what travels under another name. Though sometimes it finds it: between December 2025 and March 2026, according to CBP, officers in Cincinnati intercepted more than 5,000 units of unapproved peptides from a single Chinese exporter, in mis-manifested cartons.

Chainalysis, cited by Asia Matters for America, saw a sharp jump in crypto payments to peptide vendors in early 2026. Credit cards, apparently, are too public for this kind of science.

In January 2026 The New York Times described peptides spreading through Silicon Valley startups and hacker houses, all the way to parties sponsored by suppliers. Valley parties used to be about who raised what. Now they’re about who brought what.

## Nobody plans to sniff it

Speaking of regulators: they don’t like the label, and they have an argument no slogan can beat.

On March 31, 2026, the FDA sent a warning letter to an online seller that offered, among other things, retatrutide, Eli Lilly’s experimental molecule, which isn’t approved anywhere. The label said “research use only,” naturally. The FDA’s answer, in essence: the label changes nothing. If your website promises appetite suppression and weight loss, the product is intended for humans, which makes it a drug, which makes it an unapproved one. The bacteriostatic water sold alongside got its own paragraph. The logic is simple: if the powder comes with water for injection, nobody plans to sniff it.

That’s the kind of legal literalism I love. The words on the label mean what they say. The seller’s intent means what the website says.

Congress joined in. In January 2026 Representative Raja Krishnamoorthi sent letters to three Chinese biotech companies with ten questions on manufacturing, “research use only” labeling and exports of GLP-1 drugs and their ingredients. In August 2026 Eli Lilly sued six US vendors selling its retatrutide as a research chemical. Lilly says it plans to file the molecule with the FDA by the end of 2026. So the drug hit the gray market before the real one. Possibly the first time a knockoff beat the original to the shelf. Certainly not the last.

## The product is something

Now a document this story needs. It’s a pastiche: I’ve never seen a real agreement with this exact text. But I think it captures the genre.

“The buyer confirms that he or she: 1. is a qualified researcher; 2. will not use the product on humans; 3. will not use the product on animals; 4. understands the product is not a drug; 5. understands the product is not a supplement; 6. understands the product is something.”

The last line is mine. I think it’s the most honest one.

## Politics zigzags

A word on politics, which zigzags here. In February 2026, US Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. told Joe Rogan’s podcast he was a “big fan of peptides.” In April he announced that twelve peptides would come off the list that had effectively barred them from pharmacy compounding and be re-reviewed. In late July an FDA advisory committee voted to recommend six of them for compounding, although the agency’s own scientists didn’t back it. It isn’t final, and turning a recommendation into a rule can take months or years.

So one hand of government writes warning letters while the other opens the pharmacy door. Meanwhile the Chinese plant just keeps shipping. It doesn’t care which hand in Washington is in charge this month. It works with both.

## I still have my own stomach

Why this market exists needs no explanation from me: huge demand, expensive official drugs, and the trendiest molecules not on sale at all. The fashion for “skinny shots” has outrun medicine, regulation and common sense. Why it’s dangerous is equally plain: nobody but the seller knows what’s in the vial, where it was made or how. A certificate of analysis, doctors and journalists warn, can be printed. So can a label.

I’m old school. When I was young and a bottle said “not for internal use,” I believed it. Naive, perhaps. But I still have my own stomach, and I’m attached to it.

We’re all lab technicians now. Except a real technician has a lab head, a protocol and a logbook. This one has a label.
