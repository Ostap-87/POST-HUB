In Vietnam you don’t cross the street. You live through it.

I learned this on my first evening in Hanoi. I stood at the kerb and waited for the traffic to end. It didn’t. Traffic in Vietnam never ends. It just occasionally turns into slightly different traffic.

I stood there about ten minutes. Then an older woman with a shopping bag came up behind me, looked at me with the sympathy you’d show someone waiting for a tram in a city without trams, and walked off. Straight into the traffic. Not fast, not slow. The stream flowed around her like a river around a rock that was there before the river.

I followed her. It was the best Vietnamese lesson I’ve ever had. Not a single word.

## Eight motorbikes for every ten people

Some numbers, for scale.

According to the Ministry of Public Security, by mid-2026 there were more than 84 million motorbikes on Vietnam’s roads. The population is about 102 million. That’s more than eight motorbikes for every ten people, counting babies, monks and those who are no longer in a hurry.

Hanoi alone has nearly seven million.

Vietnamese often just call a motorbike a “Honda,” the way people once called every copier a Xerox. Fair enough: Honda holds about 83% of the petrol motorbike market.

A brand that becomes a noun is marketing’s highest achievement. The only thing higher is becoming a verb.

## A traffic light isn’t a law, it’s an opinion

So, the guide. Compiled from my own experience and other people’s advice. I got none of it right the first time.

Don’t stop. The traffic sees you and has already factored you in. If you stop, you ruin the calculation. A thousand riders have already steered around you in their heads, and you go and freeze. That’s rude.

Don’t run. A running pedestrian is unpredictable, and unpredictable people aren’t popular here. Popular are those who move like the hour hand: slowly, but always.

Don’t look at the bikes. Look at the riders. Bikes don’t negotiate. People do.

And don’t wait for the lights. Hanoi has traffic lights. But a traffic light here isn’t a law. It’s an opinion. It’s heard out with respect, and then everyone does what the situation requires.

Let me list some things I’ve seen riding on a motorbike. Personal observations, but you’ll find a thousand photos of each online:

A family of four, the youngest asleep between the grown-ups. A refrigerator. A pane of glass the size of a door, held by a passenger facing it. Forty potted plants. A bunch of balloons so big you couldn’t see the rider. A ladder. A young man in a suit in that heat, because he was on his way to propose. I made that last part up. But his face suggested it.

A motorbike here isn’t transport. It’s a front hall rolled out into the street. People live in it, eat in it, haul things, court, sleep and take calls, sometimes all at once.

## An entire country put on a helmet

Speaking of helmets.

Until the end of 2007, helmets in Vietnam were required only on certain roads, and researchers estimate about three riders in ten wore one. From December 15, 2007, helmets became mandatory for all riders and passengers on all roads. In spring 2008, researchers stood on the streets of four cities and counted helmets on 90 to 99 percent of adults.

Within months. An entire country put on a helmet.

I like this more than any GDP figure. There’s nothing harder than getting people to put something on their heads. Especially in the heat. Especially if they’ve managed fine without it. The Vietnamese did it.

Children fared worse: far fewer young passengers wore helmets back then. The adults protected their own heads before their kids’. Sadly, that isn’t a Vietnamese trait. It’s a human one.

## Changing its engine, not its character

Now the traffic is being talked into something new.

In summer 2025, the government told Hanoi to clear petrol motorbikes out of the area inside Ring Road 1 by July 2026. The city counted how many people live and ride there, how many bikes they own and what an electric one costs. Then it did what any sensible city does with an impossible order: it broke it into phases.

Since July 1, 2026, it has started small: on Friday, Saturday and Sunday evenings, no vehicles at all are allowed into the walking zone around Hoan Kiem Lake. The zone is meant to grow, and from 2028 bikes that fail the emissions standard will be barred from everywhere inside Ring Road 1.

Meanwhile electric bikes are selling fast. VinFast says it sold more than 406,000 electric motorbikes in Vietnam in 2025, almost six times as many as the year before.

The traffic is changing its engine, not its character. I’m sure an electric bike in Hanoi will negotiate the same way. Just more quietly. The horn will stay. A horn here isn’t a threat. It means “I’m here, I see you, I’ll go around you.” The politest word on a Vietnamese street sounds like “beep.”

## Not miracles, attention

Now, why it all works.

As someone used to lane markings, rules and fines, I long thought the Vietnamese street ran on miracles. Then I realised: not miracles. Attention. Everyone watches everyone. Nobody is sure they’ll be given way, so everyone is ready to give way.

Where there are few rules, you have to think. Where there are many, you don’t have to. It’s not obvious which is more dangerous.

I’m not saying it’s safe. It isn’t, and nobody knows that better than the people riding every day. I’m saying something else: a million people can pass through one junction every day with no light, no traffic cop and no contract, and still reach an agreement.

Evening. Ho Chi Minh City. I’m on the pavement again, no longer afraid, not yet brave.

A motorbike goes by. A man in front, a woman behind, a child between them, asleep, cheek against his father’s back. The helmet’s a little too big for him. The bike moves smoothly, no jerks, because it’s carrying the most precious thing these people have.

The strongest structures aren’t held together by bolts. They’re held together by nobody braking hard.

The bike melts into the stream. The stream doesn’t end. I finally take a step.
