A disclaimer first: I’m not a doctor, a pharmacist or a chemist. I’m a marketer. So there won’t be a single word here about what, who or how much. Only what I actually understand: who makes it, who buys it and why nobody likes to talk about it.

In a good restaurant, the chef never comes out. Diners see a white tablecloth, a beautiful plate and the chef’s name on the menu. They don’t see the kitchen. Fair enough: kitchens are loud, hot, and someone is always shouting in Mandarin.

Global pharma works the same way with peptides. The dining room is in Copenhagen, Indianapolis and Hyderabad. Much of the kitchen is in China.

## A necklace of a few dozen beads

What’s a peptide? Here’s how I understand it: a short chain of amino acids, a necklace of a few dozen beads. Several drugs everyone is talking about, from gossip columns to market reports, are exactly such necklaces. You can grow them with cells, by fermentation, or assemble them chemically, bead by bead, on a solid support — solid-phase synthesis. For a chain of 30-plus links, it takes more than two weeks.

Two weeks per chain. It sometimes takes me two weeks to finish one slide deck, and it still has typos.

## A swimming pool filled with orders

Now the scale. The head chef is WuXi AppTec, specifically its WuXi TIDES unit for oligonucleotides and peptides. In early 2024 its solid-phase reactors totaled about 32,000 liters. By the end of 2025: over 100,000. The Taixing build-out was finished in September 2025, ahead of schedule. TIDES revenue for 2025: RMB 11.37 billion, nearly double the year before.

A hundred thousand liters isn’t a saucepan. It’s a swimming pool filled with orders.

Goldman Sachs put it plainly in mid-2026: China already leads in synthetic peptide API supply. It estimates the world will need around 99,000 kg of peptide APIs a year by 2030, and notes that China already has significant solid-phase capacity, though actual utilization, yields and output are hard to see.

That’s a kitchen for you: everything’s boiling, nobody tells you how much. Not secrecy. The chef is just busy.

## Customers prefer private rooms

As for who eats — this is where it gets interesting, because the customers of the Chinese kitchen prefer private rooms.

Take Shenzhen-based Hybio. In 2023 its subsidiary signed a $30 million GLP-1 API order with a US customer it didn’t name. In December 2025 it signed a non-binding term sheet with India’s Dr. Reddy’s on peptide APIs and finished forms. In the first half of 2026 it reported framework GLP-1 API orders worth RMB 180 million and RMB 327 million — again with unnamed customers, and with the honest caveat that actual amounts depend on what clients really order.

As a marketer, I know this genre. A framework agreement is “let’s grab lunch sometime.” Lunch may or may not happen, but it’s already in the quarterly report.

## Who’s in the dining room

Who’s in the dining room is fairly clear. First, the inventors of the drugs, who need contract manufacturers when demand outruns their own plants. Second, Indian generic makers: as patent protection on some peptide molecules lapses in various markets, Goldman expects most new entrants to choose chemical synthesis, which is cheaper to start than fermentation. And chemical synthesis is China’s specialty.

So: a Western molecule, Indian packaging, a Chinese API. We call it a global supply chain. Where I grew up, there was a simpler word for it: “I got hold of it.”

Allow me a menu.

1.  Starter: an API cooked in Changzhou or Taixing.
2.  Main: the same, in Indian packaging.
3.  Drinks: US import control. In September 2025 the FDA launched a “green list” for GLP-1 API sources; shipments from anyone not on it can be detained at the border without examination.
4.  Dessert: the stock ticker.
5.  Tip: not included. The chef is on a framework agreement.

## Just with no logo on the box

What moves me about this story: we think of China as the maker of cheap mass goods — lighters, umbrellas, phones. Here it makes one of the subtlest products of modern chemistry, and also at scale. Just with no logo on the box. You can’t recognize a peptide API by its brand like a pair of sneakers. Only by paperwork, inspections, and the people in Taixing and Changzhou whose names nobody will ever print on the menu.

A good chef is praised for the dish. A great one, for going unnoticed.

The chef never comes out. He stands at the stove somewhere between Shanghai and the Yangtze, stringing his beads.

And in the dining room, as always, they discuss the taste.
