“This feature is unavailable during the gaokao period.”

That is the most honest sentence I have ever heard from an AI. Normally it answers everything: the meaning of life, a borscht recipe, why the client hasn’t paid. Confidently, at length, with bullet points. And then suddenly, like a strict hall monitor: not allowed. No bullet points.

I respected it instantly. I have never learned to say “not allowed.” A marketer who says “not allowed” is called a lawyer.

## A European country sent to its desks

First, the exam itself. The gaokao is China’s national university entrance exam, restored in 1977, and the most important few days in the life of a Chinese family. About 13.4 million students sat it in 2025. In 2026, the Ministry of Education reported 12.9 million registrations; the exam began on June 7.

12.9 million people. That’s not a cohort, it’s a mid-sized European country sent to its desks for four days. And outside every school gate, parents whose nervous energy could heat a small city.

Naturally, nobody wants a phone solving the problems.

## The chatbots closed their eyes

So the chatbots closed their eyes. In June 2025, Tencent’s Yuanbao and Moonshot’s Kimi turned off image recognition during exam hours. Alibaba said Qwen’s photo feature was frozen from June 7 to 10. ByteDance’s Doubao still accepted photos, but when shown a test paper, it said the picture was “not in compliance with rules.” Asked why, the bots answered almost in unison: to ensure fairness, this function cannot be used during the exam.

In 2026, Doubao, Qianwen, Quark and Kimi all went into “temporary lock” mode. Qianwen announced it was pausing photo problem-solving. Baidu said Wenxin would work as usual, though some gaokao-related requests might be restricted. Doubao had explained its policy back in May: before the exam, I’ll help you study; after, I’ll go through your mistakes; during — no.

Before and after, sure. During — please leave the hall.

I have read many corporate policies. This is the first that fits on one line without lying.

## Learn from it, don’t copy from it

Speaking of eyes. While chatbots squeezed theirs shut, the ministry upgraded the smart security gates at exam centers to catch not only phones but smart glasses. A year ago those glasses were sold as “AI on your face.” Now they’re searched for at the door, like a crib sheet up a sleeve.

Every invention in China gets sold first and confiscated at the gaokao second.

The ministry handles AI the way a strict father handles a gifted son. In May 2025 it said students shouldn’t hand in AI-generated text as homework or test answers, while schools should nurture AI talent from an early age. In April 2026 an “AI + Education” plan added mechanisms against AI-assisted cheating.

Learn from it. Don’t copy from it. And never show it the exam paper.

The ministry also warned parents about vendors selling “AI exam predictions,” saying the chance of AI or so-called experts guessing the questions is extremely small. I’d frame that line in every marketing agency on earth, including the ones I’ve worked for.

## The models at the same desk

Now the best part. The exam ends, the locks come off, and journalists immediately put the models at the same desk.

Beijing News gave six models the 2026 math paper (reconstructed from students’ recollections) and had two teachers grade them. iFlytek Spark scored 148 out of 150, Kimi 145, DeepSeek 144, Zhipu 143, MiniMax 142, ChatGPT 137. Tech outlet Zhidongxi tested Yuanbao: 150 in math, 142 in Chinese. A former head essay grader called one of its essays full-mark material — but asked for more personal insight.

Personal insight. From a model. That’s when I realized the gaokao still tests a human, even when a machine writes it.

For four days the machine is kept out so it can’t help a child. On day five it’s called to the blackboard to show off. That’s not a contradiction. That’s China’s relationship with AI: be proud of it, but don’t let it sit the exam.

## Wait in the hallway, there’s a person in there

I could probably pass gaokao math today at about the level of “this feature is unavailable.” Still, I like this story. A country pouring enormous effort into AI tells it once a year: wait in the hallway. There’s a person in there. They’re eighteen, their palms are sweaty, their mother is outside the gate with a thermos, and these are their four days.

AI, please leave the exam hall.

You’ll be back. They’ll need you at university, at work, for the rest of their lives.

But these four days are theirs.
