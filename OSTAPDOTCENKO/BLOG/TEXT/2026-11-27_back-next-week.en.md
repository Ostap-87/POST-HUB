> Never put off till tomorrow what you can put off till next week. — Folk wisdom, revised in Los Angeles

I keep a “next week” to-do list. I’ve kept it for years, and honestly it’s the most stable document in my life. Contracts change, partners change, exchange rates change, but “clear the inbox,” “call the lawyer” and “finally learn to use the calendar” stand firm like monuments. Next week is a remarkable stretch of time. It never arrives, yet it’s always fully booked.

So I have some sympathy for Jia Yueting. He has a “next week” item too. It’s just a little bigger than mine.

Let’s start at the top. It makes the fall easier to see.

Jia founded LeTV, a video streaming service, in 2004. In 2010 it listed on Shenzhen’s ChiNext board. The streaming service grew into an empire called LeEco: smartphones, TVs, sports, film, cloud, and of course electric cars. It all came with a signature buzzword, the “ecosystem chemical reaction”: put the businesses together and you get more than the sum of the parts.

On May 12, 2015, LeTV shares hit 179 yuan and the company was worth close to 170 billion yuan.

The chemical reaction did happen. Like many chemical reactions, it turned out to be exothermic. In plain English: it blew up.

In November 2016, LeEco ran out of cash. In January 2017, property developer Sunac bought into the empire for 15 billion yuan; Sunac’s chairman Sun Hongbin later acknowledged the investment had been written down to zero. That same January, at CES in Las Vegas, Jia unveiled the FF 91 electric car from his US company Faraday Future.

And on July 4, 2017, Jia flew to the United States, saying he’d be back next week.

In China, “下周回国” — “back in the country next week” — became a meme. People post it under vacation photos, under their bosses’ promises, under roadwork notices. In December 2017, the Beijing bureau of the China Securities Regulatory Commission ordered Jia to return by December 31 to meet his obligations as LeTV’s controlling shareholder. He didn’t. That same month, a court put him on its list of dishonest debtors.

The week had begun.

A digression. In my line of work there’s a genre called “the roadmap.” It’s a handsome slide with an arrow going left to right and quarters written above it: Q1, Q2, Q3. I’ve drawn plenty myself. Any seasoned reader of roadmaps knows the arrow measures mood, not time.

Jia is the best roadmap artist I know. And, it seems, the only one who has managed to live inside his roadmap for nine years.

From here the story ran on several tracks at once, and I’ll list them dryly, like a court record. This part is less funny.

In 2018, Evergrande put $800 million into Faraday Future; within months the two sides were in court. In 2019, Jia filed for personal bankruptcy in the US; in 2020, a Los Angeles court approved his plan: his assets went into a trust for creditors, who were to be repaid from his stake in Faraday Future.

On July 20, 2020, LeTV was delisted. Its final price: 0.18 yuan; about 99.9% of its peak market value was gone. Behind those numbers are ordinary retail investors who had no US court, no trusts and no roadmaps.

In April 2021, the CSRC fined Jia 241 million yuan and banned him from China’s securities market for life. According to the regulator, LeTV had inflated its revenue by 1.87 billion yuan between 2007 and 2016.

Sometimes “next week” isn’t a promise. It’s a way of life.

In July 2021, Faraday Future went public on Nasdaq through a SPAC merger. The FF 91, unveiled in 2017, entered production in spring 2023 — six years later. In 2023 the company handed over 10 cars: four sold, six leased. According to its filings, by spring 2025 the total delivered was around sixteen. Price: about $300,000 apiece.

Six years to launch a car that has found fewer owners than a modest wedding has guests. A very modest wedding.

Then came the FX brand and a cheaper minivan, the FX Super One. In January 2026, the company laid out a plan: US deliveries from the second quarter. In May 2026, the original version was paused, to be reworked on an 800-volt architecture or as a hybrid, with mass production depending on whether investors turn up. Meanwhile the car company had shipped 68 robots by the end of April.

A car company that sells robots. That, in a sense, is the ecosystem chemical reaction at last. Just nine years late and in a different lab.

An intermission. Four lines I couldn’t help writing.

*“I’ll be back,” he said, “in just a week — Once my car is ready for the street.”* *Nine years on, the weeks have all gone by;* *The calendar’s still waiting for that “Hi.”*

(My own verse. I’m about as much of a poet as the FF 91 is a production car: technically yes, numerically no.)

These days Jia’s title is founder and global co-CEO of Faraday Future. Every week he publishes an update for investors. Every. Single. Week.

There’s an unintended honesty in that. A man who once promised “next week” now reports in every week. As if he’s trying to work off one sentence spoken in the summer of 2017.

I don’t know whether he’ll return to China. I don’t know whether the FX Super One will ever drive on American roads, or what the stock will be worth by the time you read this. Predicting anything about Jia Yueting is a game that people far smarter than me, with far more money, have lost.

This series of mine is about people who fell and got back up. Jia is a special case. He doesn’t fall and he doesn’t get up. He just keeps starting. Every time: a new product, a new brand, a new slide, a new week.

A restart means admitting that the old thing is over. If you never admit it, it isn’t a restart. It’s a very long teaser.

I close my laptop and look at my own “next week” list. It’s shorter than Jia’s and a lot cheaper. But I think I’ll do something on it today.

I’ll call the lawyer. The inbox, fine, can wait till next week.
