> Grandpa stood in formation, Dad stood in line, The son lay down — and frankly, that seems fine. If forty years of standing got you nowhere new, Lying down’s a faster way to get there too. — My own verse. Not yet shown to anyone in China

I’ve worked with China for years, and the whole time China has been running. The factories ran, the cities ran, and don’t get me started on the trains — 350 kilometers an hour. People ran so hard that next to them I felt less like a business partner and more like a pedestrian who wandered onto a track mid-race and is pretending he meant to.

I’ve never lain down myself, if I’m honest. The only time I lie flat is on a beach, and even then with a phone, receiving photos of a sample from a factory. We were raised not to stand still. So we didn’t. We ran. Where to — that became clear later, and not for everyone.

And then, in China, one young man lay down.

In the spring of 2021 a post appeared on the Baidu Tieba forum titled “Lying Flat Is Justice.” Its author, Luo Huazhong, explained that he’d quit his job, lived on very little — The New York Times put it at about sixty dollars a month — and had stopped running. According to the Hong Kong press, he picked up occasional work at a film studio in Dongyang. Playing corpses.

Think about that. A man decides to lie down and finds a job that consists of lying down. Most people spend their whole lives looking for a true vocation. He found his in a week.

The post was deleted. The copies were deleted. The word survived. “Tang ping” spread across the country faster than any ad campaign I’ve ever run — and I’ve run a lot of them. None of mine ever told people to lie down.

Naturally, the newspapers woke up.

Nanfang Daily ran a commentary saying, in essence, that lying flat is shameful. At the end of 2021 Guangming Daily put “Lying Flat Is Not Advisable” on its front page. Merchandise printed with “tang ping” vanished from online stores.

You don’t kick a man when he’s down. You write an editorial at him.

It’s a remarkable country. It built the world’s longest high-speed rail network and was rattled by one horizontal young man. Perhaps because horizontal is contagious. Vertical isn’t. Vertical just gets tired.

To understand why people lay down, you have to understand how they ran.

China has a word for it: “neijuan,” involution. The usual explanation goes like this. A theater. Everyone is seated. One person stands up to see better. Then a second, then a third, and soon the whole house is watching on its feet. Nobody sees any better. Everybody is exhausted. But sitting down is now out of the question: sit, and you’ll see nothing.

Tang ping isn’t the man who walks out of the theater. It’s the man who sits down in a theater where everyone is standing. And stretches his legs.

Running in place is the most exhausting sport there is. No medals, and you can’t drop out of the race, because there’s no finish line.

Then, as genres do, it evolved.

After tang ping came “bai lan,” roughly “let it rot.” If tang ping means lying down, bai lan means lying down and no longer fluffing the pillow. Things are going bad, and instead of rescuing them you watch with mild curiosity: let’s see how bad.

As a marketing man, I see a great slogan in this. “It’s going to rot anyway, so let it rot with style.” No agency would ever pitch that. The agency would be afraid of the client.

The numbers weren’t funny. In mid-2023 the unemployment rate for urban youth aged 16 to 24 hit a record 21.3%. Publication was then suspended, the methodology changed, students were taken out of the count — and in 2025 the figure still crept toward 19% in some months. Back in 2023, Peking University economist Zhang Dandan estimated that if you included the young people who had lain down and stopped looking, the rate for one month could have been as high as 46.5%.

Forty-six and a half percent. Nearly one in two. Half the young city, horizontal.

Now the parents. Here the laughter runs out sooner.

The parents ran so their children wouldn’t have to.

Their generation worked the assembly lines, lived in dormitories, sent money home, bought apartments for the whole extended family and saw their kids at New Year. They ran their whole lives for one reason: so the child wouldn’t have to.

The child doesn’t have to. The child is lying down.

The parents ran so the children wouldn’t have to. The children don’t. The parents are not pleased.

Around 2022 and 2023 Chinese social media discovered “full-time children”: adult sons and daughters paid a salary by their parents to live at home, shop, cook and look after the elderly. A Douban group for them gathered several thousand members. A young woman from Luoyang told CNN her parents paid her 6,000 yuan a month; she did the shopping and cared for her grandmother, who has dementia. Her peers with office jobs were making three or four thousand.

The full-time child out-earns the employed one. Economists call this an anomaly. Grandma calls it her granddaughter.

Grandma probably doesn’t know anyone is being paid. But she knows someone is there. Which of us would say that isn’t a job?

Parents want their children to stand on their own two feet. The children reply: we tried, there’s a queue.

I’m not here to judge; I’m watching from the side. I see one generation that spent its life standing — in formation, in line, at the conveyor belt — and the next one lying down. And it looks to me like the same gesture filmed from two angles. The father stood so there would be something to lie on. The son lay down because he saw what that sofa cost his father.

Lying flat isn’t falling down. It’s refusing to fall down mid-stride.

I picture it like this. Evening. A father comes home from a job he’s stood at for thirty years. His son is lying on the sofa. The father looks at him for a long time and takes a breath for the Great Generational Talk.

Then he sits down next to him.

Not because he agrees. Just his legs.

They sit there. One has finally sat down, the other hasn’t got up yet. Between them is a sofa that, it turns out, has room for both.

You can write the ending yourselves. I’m going to lie down for a bit. Not long.
