> In China you don’t wait for food. You greet it. — Observed at the entrance to an office tower

I’m not a fast man. I’m on time for meetings only because I leave an hour early. I eat slowly, walk slowly, and, as this essay will show, think slowly too.

But there’s a man in China who does everything faster than me. Faster than anyone. He’s the fastest man in town. Yellow jacket, a helmet — sometimes with little ears on top — a phone on the handlebars, a box on the back, and in the box somebody’s lunch, still hot.

Order accepted. He’s on his way.

## Not a street but a beehive

Have you seen a big office tower in Shanghai or Shenzhen at noon? It’s not a street. It’s a beehive. Yellow jackets, blue jackets, and since 2025 red ones too. Scooters parked in rows like horses at a hitching rail. Riders sprint to the pickup racks; the racks are stuffed with bags; the bags are labeled with phone numbers. Nobody talks. Everybody knows what to do.

If government ministries worked like this, the country would have taken off long ago. Well — further off.

How many are there? According to Meituan itself, about 7.45 million riders earned income on its platform in 2023. Seven and a half million people. That’s the population of Hong Kong putting on yellow jackets and going out to deliver lunch.

The company paid them about 80 billion yuan for the year. TechNode did the division and got roughly 3.65 yuan per delivery. Less than the coffee he just brought you.

That’s China in a nutshell. Enormous numbers that turn very small once you get up close.

## Trapped in the system

Speaking of speed.

In September 2020, the Chinese magazine Renwu (“People”) published a long investigation whose headline the whole country quoted afterwards: “Delivery Riders, Trapped in the System.” Reporters spent half a year talking to dozens of riders. One idea stood out, frightening in its simplicity: the algorithm learns how fast you made it, and next time gives you less time. You made it — great, tomorrow you’ll make it faster. You didn’t — a fine, a lower rating, an argument with an app that doesn’t listen.

Meituan’s dispatch system is called “Super Brain.” Ele.me’s is called “Ark.” A brain and an ark — names from science fiction and the Bible. And underneath the brain and the ark, a man on a scooter running an amber light.

The very next day, Meituan responded: the system would add eight minutes of flexibility per order, so riders could wait for a slow elevator and ease off at intersections. And it wrote a line I liked: problems with the system ultimately have to be solved by the people behind the system.

Eight minutes. Eight whole minutes. A man was granted eight extra minutes of life per order, and it made national news.

It made me wonder how many minutes of flexibility I’ve been given. How many times has anyone told me: it’s fine, wait for the elevator, slow down at the crossing? Not many. At least the rider got it in writing.

## The shortest relationship of my life

A short scene. It’s invented, but invented from things I’ve watched at building entrances.

— Where are you? — Downstairs. — I can’t see you. — I’m in a yellow jacket. — Everybody’s in a yellow jacket. — I’m the one looking at his phone. — Everybody’s looking at their phone. — I’m the one who’s already leaving. Your order’s on the rack. Five stars, please.

And he’s gone. I stand there holding the bag, thinking: that was the shortest relationship of my life. Fifteen seconds, three lines, and a request for a rating.

## The riders held up, the servers didn’t

Now, the war.

In 2025, JD.com entered food delivery, Alibaba answered with tens of billions in subsidies, Meituan answered with discounts, and the price war got so fierce that coffee cost less than its cup. On July 5, 2025, Meituan handled more than 120 million orders in a single day — the highest in its history. The servers buckled; in some areas the app briefly went down.

A hundred and twenty million orders in a day. The riders held up. The servers didn’t.

As a summary of the balance of power between people and machines in 2025, I can’t think of a better one.

That same year, the big platforms announced they would gradually provide social insurance for riders: JD for full-time riders starting March 2025, Meituan starting with its most regular riders. Slowly, in stages, with pilots. But they announced it. The fastest man in town gets his protection at the slowest possible pace. Apparently that’s how balance works.

## Speed isn’t a technology, it’s a person

The fastest man in town isn’t the happiest. I understand that. But he isn’t the unhappiest either. Many riders come from small towns and villages. This is their first money in a big city, their first Shanghai, seen at thirty kilometers an hour between traffic lights.

He knows the city better than any of us. Every entrance, every back door, every security guard who lets him in and every one who doesn’t. He knows the city by heart, the way cab drivers used to — and hates the traffic just as much.

We foreigners look at China and see speed. Trains, factories, delivery in half an hour. But speed in China isn’t a technology. It’s a person in a great hurry so that everything arrives on time for you.

Evening. I’m in my hotel room ordering dinner. On the screen a little yellow figure moves across the map. Turns. Stops. Moves again.

I watch him the way I used to watch from a window as a kid, waiting for someone. Except now I know exactly where he is, how many meters are left and when he’ll arrive.

He arrives four minutes early. Says, “Your order.” Leaves.

I never saw his face. Helmet, mask, jacket. The fastest man in town. Somewhere right now he’s probably having dinner too. If he’s found the time.
