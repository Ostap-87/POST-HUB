> People used to be judged by their wallets. Now it’s by their battery level. — Observed in a queue for steamed buns

I’m old school. There was always something that rustled in my pocket. Just in case. The cases varied; the rustling stayed the same and always ran out before the case did.

I brought the habit to China. I brought yuan. Cash. Handsome red notes with a portrait. I put them in my pocket and went into town like a proper foreigner.

A week later the yuan were still in my pocket. Every one. Untouched. Like a museum piece I carry around for reasons I can’t explain.

My wallet stayed at the hotel. It used to stay there before, too, but back then it was forgetfulness. Now it’s adaptation.

## Who scans whom

China pays by phone. All of it. Not “many people,” not “in big cities.” All of it — from the Beijing restaurant to the roasted-chestnut cart, where the vendor looks a bit like a chestnut himself and the QR code is laminated, frayed at the corners, like a family heirloom.

China has almost 1.1 billion internet users. That billion-plus stopped asking “cash or card?” long ago. The question now is: “Do you scan me, or do I scan you?”

That’s the great philosophical question of modern China. Not “to be or not to be.” Who scans whom.

## A two-yuan bottle and a QR code

About cards. A plastic card in China is like a paper business card at a startup party: technically it exists, but handing one over feels awkward. The cashier looks at it like a floppy disk. There may be a terminal somewhere. In the back room. Under a box. If the owner remembers where he put it last year.

For years, foreigners were second-class citizens here — not because of their passports, but because of their payments. A traveler arrives with a credit card, a bank and full faith in the global financial system, and can’t buy a bottle of water. Water! In front of him: a fridge, a two-yuan bottle and a QR code. He stares at the code. The code stares back. Nobody blinks.

China noticed. Since 2023–2024, foreign cards have become easier to link to Alipay and WeChat Pay, limits have gone up, procedures have been simplified. And the numbers took off: in the first half of 2024 alone, more than five million foreign visitors used mobile payments in China — about four times as many as a year earlier. They made over 90 million transactions, seven times more than the year before.

Ninety million times a foreigner paid by phone in China. And I can picture how many times before that he stood in front of a fridge, staring at a code.

An intermission. A short list of things I’ve seen paid for by QR code in China and nearby (compiled from personal observation; nothing verified except my own phone):

1.  Lunch.
2.  A taxi.
3.  A bike.
4.  A rented power bank, so your phone doesn’t die while you pay with your phone.
5.  A street musician.
6.  A second lunch, because the first power bank didn’t help.
7.  The feeling that you belong.

The last one isn’t for sale, actually. It turns up by itself on about day three, the first time you pay without fumbling — casually, like a local.

## A dead phone, a temporary loss of citizenship

Now for the unnerving part.

It’s unnerving when your phone dies. Anywhere else, a dead phone is an inconvenience. In China, it’s a temporary loss of citizenship. You exist, but you don’t. You’re hungry, but you can’t pay. You’re standing in a city of millions with yuan in your pocket, and here yuan are like Latin: everybody technically understands them, but speaking them out loud feels odd.

## Somebody goes back for grandma

In fairness, cash is legal tender in China and refusing it is not allowed. The central bank fines businesses for it, and means it. In 2020 it fined sixteen organizations, some of them up to 500,000 yuan. In 2024 the targets included a KFC franchise in Wuxi. The country that built one of the world’s most cashless economies also makes sure nobody bullies cash.

That’s very Chinese. First everyone races ahead. Then somebody goes back for grandma.

Because grandma has cash. So does grandpa. So does whoever never learned to scan. Speed is wonderful as long as there’s room in it for the ones who can’t keep up.

## Money you can’t see

I walk around Shanghai without a wallet. Convenient. Fast. Shop, taxi, coffee, metro — all with one gesture. Life becomes smooth as a screen.

Then, somewhere in the second week, I notice something strange. I’ve stopped noticing that I spend. Money doesn’t leave my hands — it was never in my hands. It just quietly disappears from a number I haven’t checked in a while.

Money you can’t see is spent like time: quietly and for good.

When you handed over a banknote, you said goodbye, if only for a second. You looked at the portrait. The portrait looked at you. Now there’s nobody to say goodbye to. A beep, and you’ve moved on.

I fly home from China and bring back those same yuan. Red, intact, with the portrait. I put them in a drawer until the next trip.

Next time I’ll take them along again. Not because I’ll need them. Because there should be something in my pocket that doesn’t depend on a battery.

Everything else, it seems, already does.
