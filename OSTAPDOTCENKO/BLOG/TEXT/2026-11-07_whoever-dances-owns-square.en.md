> By day the square belongs to the state. By night it belongs to whoever has the louder speaker. — Observed from a hotel balcony

I come to China to work. Meetings, factories, negotiations, tea, more tea. In the evening I step out of the hotel for some air — and walk straight into a dance.

I don’t dance. I stopped a long time ago, and to be fair I never really started. But nobody asks you in China after dark. After dark you either dance or you stand at the edge and watch. I watch. For the next half hour, that’s my job.

## The speaker obeys only her

Eight p.m. The square.

First the speaker arrives. A speaker on wheels with a pull-up handle, like a suitcase packed not for a holiday but for a campaign. Then comes the woman who wheeled it in. Not the oldest, not the most glamorous. The boss. You can tell at once: the speaker obeys only her.

Then everyone else. Twenty people, forty, sometimes a hundred. They form rows. The rows are straight, like a military parade — except this parade happens every night and nobody reviews it.

And off they go.

Chinese media once estimated that up to 100 million people dance like this. A hundred million. That’s not a hobby, that’s a mid-sized country that walks out to the square every night and demands nothing. Except to be left alone.

Here’s what gets me. In a world where everyone wants change, there are a hundred million people who want exactly the same thing every night at eight. And get it.

## Forty thousand dollars for silence

Speaking of being left alone — the neighbors.

The neighbors also want the same thing every night at eight. Silence. They don’t get it.

The chronicle of this war reads like an action film. In Beijing, a man fired a shotgun into the air and set his dogs on the dancers. Elsewhere, residents dropped bags from their windows whose contents I won’t describe on a professional blog. Somewhere else, neighbors pooled money for their own sound system to drown out the dancers’ — reportedly more than $40,000. Forty thousand dollars for silence. In China, quiet is a premium product.

And the women kept dancing. The gun went off, the dogs ran around, the rival speaker blared. They kept dancing.

You can’t defeat someone who came to dance, not to fight. They don’t even know there’s a war on.

## Twelve routines and “Little Apple”

The government noticed too, of course. Governments always notice when a hundred million people do something without them.

In 2015, the General Administration of Sport and the Ministry of Culture rolled out twelve “scientific” and “healthy” routines designed by experts. One was set to the pop hit “Little Apple.” Instructors were to fan out across the country.

People heard one thing: a national standard. They were not pleased. Officials quickly clarified: no, no, it’s optional, just an alternative.

I picture the conversation like this:

— Comrades, here are twelve approved routines. — We dance our own. — This routine is scientifically designed. — We dance our own. — It’s set to “Little Apple”! — We already dance to “Little Apple.” Our own way. — Then consider it a recommendation. — Considered.

It’s the only case I know of where grandmothers edited government policy overnight. No petition, no protest. They just didn’t stop dancing.

## Negotiating the decibels

Time passes. The country grows up. The grandmothers don’t.

In June 2022, China’s new noise pollution law took effect. It has a special provision for dancing and fitness in streets, squares and parks: follow the venue manager’s rules on area, time and volume. Don’t crank the sound.

Look at the wording. Not “ban.” Not “disperse.” “Follow the venue’s rules.” It’s a law written by someone who understands you can’t break up the dance. You can only negotiate the decibels.

That’s the unwritten deal. The square belongs to the dancers until nine and to the sleepers after nine. Volume up to here. Speaker over there, away from the windows. Some watch, some cheat, some complain, but the square runs two shifts like a well-managed factory.

That’s China in general: the law says “volume,” and life replies, “Understood. Just one more song.”

## The evening is theirs by right

Now, seriously, as far as I’m capable of it.

I stand at the edge of the square and watch. The women are sixty, seventy. Many lived lives with no squares, no speakers and no evenings of their own. There was work, there were queues, there was always someone deciding for them. The children grew up and moved to other cities. The grandchildren live inside a phone.

And here is the square. Eight p.m. Theirs.

And I — a man here on business, with a laptop and a meeting schedule — catch myself envying them. Not the dancing; I’d be hopeless. Their certainty that the evening is theirs by right. That you can walk out, step into a row and, for half an hour, be nobody’s mother and nobody’s former employee. Just part of the row.

Old age is when you have nowhere to hurry. Old age in China is when you have nowhere to hurry, but somewhere to go.

Nine p.m. The boss switches off the speaker. The rows dissolve into people. The people go home. The square becomes just a square again — lamps, benches, and one foreigner standing in the middle for no clear reason.

I walk back to the hotel with “Little Apple” stuck in my head. I don’t know the words. I’ll never forget the tune.

Tomorrow at eight they’ll be back. I may already be in another city. There’ll be a square there, too. And a speaker. And someone dancing because nobody else is going to ask them to.
