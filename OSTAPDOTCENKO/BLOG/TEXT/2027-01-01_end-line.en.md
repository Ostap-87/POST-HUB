> The driver knows where to go. The GPS knows how. Whether you’re allowed to — you find out on the way. — Notes from the back seat

In ride-hailing apps there’s a cheerful little phrase: “You have arrived.” I’ve always found it oddly philosophical. Sometimes you arrive where you wanted. Sometimes you arrive somewhere you never planned to be, and the app still congratulates you. The gap between those two can be about two days.

This story is about those two days.

It started beautifully. In June 2012, in Beijing, Cheng Wei launched a taxi-hailing app called Didi Dache. In 2015 it merged with its main rival, Kuaidi. On August 1, 2016, Didi announced it was buying Uber’s China business. The Americans who taught the world to summon a car with a button sold that button in China.

Anyone who has been to China on business knows the feeling: you stand outside the hotel, tap the screen, and a few minutes later a car pulls up whose plate number you’ve already memorized. Didi became part of China’s air. Its IPO prospectus cited 377 million annual active users. Its share of China’s ride-hailing market was estimated at around 90%.

With ninety percent of the market, it feels like the road belongs to you. That is the most dangerous thought a driver can have.

On June 10, 2021, Didi filed for a US listing. On June 30, its shares began trading on the New York Stock Exchange. Offer price $14, about $4.4 billion raised, a valuation of around $70 billion. According to press reports, Chinese regulators had advised it not to rush. The company didn’t brake.

On July 2, two days later, the Cyberspace Administration of China announced a cybersecurity review of Didi. Until the review was done, Didi couldn’t register new users. On July 4, the regulator ordered the app removed from app stores. On July 9, 25 more Didi apps followed. When US trading reopened on July 6, the stock fell more than 20%.

The opening bell was still ringing when the app came off the shelves.

As a marketer, I have to admit professional envy. We spend years telling clients that timing is everything. Didi proved it better than any textbook. By counterexample.

Allow me an intermission. The voice of the GPS. The words are mine; the dates are real.

*“Route calculated. Destination: New York Stock Exchange. Estimated travel time: nine years.*

*You have arrived. June 30.*

*Caution. Inspection ahead. July 2.*

*Recalculating. New passengers cannot be picked up at this time.*

*Recalculating. This app is not available in your store.*

*Recalculating. Destination: Hong Kong. Arrival time: to be confirmed.*

*Recalculating. Destination: over-the-counter market.*

*Continue straight for eighteen months.*

*In 200 meters, fine.*

*You have arrived. Please rate your trip.”*

After that, things moved slowly, and I’ll list them dryly, like a logbook.

On December 3, 2021, Didi announced it would leave the NYSE and prepare to list in Hong Kong. In spring 2022, according to press reports, the Hong Kong plan was halted because regulators said the company still hadn’t met data security requirements. On May 23, shareholders voted to delist. On June 10, 2022, the shares traded in New York for the last time and closed at $2.29, 84% below the offer price. From June 13: the over-the-counter market, under the new ticker DIDIY.

A company that drove people around got out of the car itself. Before reaching the destination.

On July 21, 2022, the Cyberspace Administration fined Didi 8.026 billion yuan, about $1.2 billion and more than 4% of its 2021 revenue. Cheng Wei and president Jean Liu were each fined 1 million yuan personally. The regulator cited illegal data collection going back to 2015, facial recognition data and photos from passengers’ phones collected without consent, and data on more than 57 million drivers stored insecurely.

On some routes, what matters isn’t where you’re going, but who said you could go.

Let me stop here and say this without irony. I don’t read this as a fairy tale about an evil regulator and a poor company, or the other way round. I read it as a story about how big business in China has not one road but two: the one on the map, and the one in the heads of the people who approve the map. If you only drive the first, you get there fast. Just not always to the right place.

Speed is a virtue in a driver. A sense of the road is a virtue in a passenger who’d like to arrive alive.

On January 16, 2023, Didi was allowed to register new users again. A day later the app was back in Android stores. For a year and a half, the country’s biggest ride-hailing service had worked with its doors closed: carrying old passengers, taking no new ones. I can’t think of another company that spent so long in “boarding closed” mode without closing itself.

Then came what this column exists for. Quietly, without a bell.

In Q4 2024, Didi’s China business averaged a record 35.3 million rides a day. The company went abroad in earnest: 99, the Brazilian service it bought in 2018, relaunched its 99Food delivery brand in more than seventy cities in 2025. In Q2 2026, Didi reported a net profit of 869 million yuan, though overseas spending pushed the first half into a loss. The shares still trade over the counter, several times below that $14. Hong Kong has been discussed for years. There’s no date yet.

A regulator can cancel an IPO. Nobody can cancel the morning ride to work.

That’s the whole trip. Well, almost.

I often think a stock exchange and a taxi work the same way. You get in, give an address and hand someone the wheel. Except in a taxi you know who’s driving. On an exchange it turns out there are several wheels, and every one of them thinks it’s in charge.

Didi never reached the address it gave in 2021. But it keeps driving people, every day, tens of millions of times. And every time a car stops at the door, the app says the same thing.

You have arrived.

And this time it’s the good kind.
