“Irasshaimase!”

It’s the first thing you hear when the door slides open. “Welcome.” Said loudly, cheerfully and, crucially, without looking at you. You’re not being greeted as a person but as an event. A human has entered; rejoice.

My Japanese consists of three words: arigato, sumimasen and irasshaimase. I say the first two. The third I only ever hear. In my whole life I’ve never once had the chance to welcome a Japanese person anywhere. They always get there first.

## Several institutions folded into one room

A konbini, the dictionary says, is a convenience store. Dictionaries say a lot of things. In reality it’s several institutions folded into a room the size of a generous kitchen. Let’s take a tour, department by department.

**The Kitchen**

Onigiri in a triangular wrapper that opens in three moves — one, two, three — and the seaweed lands on the rice without a crease. It took me a year. Japanese people are born knowing how.

Bento, soup, oden simmering by the register, fried chicken under a heat lamp. At the counter they ask you: “Atatamemasu ka?” — “Shall I heat it up?”

Remember that question. It’ll be back.

According to the industry association, Japan had more than 56,000 konbini at the end of 2025, and the average purchase was about 740 yen. Seven hundred and forty yen. That’s lunch. Sometimes dinner. Sometimes, to be honest, an entire evening’s social life.

**The Post Office**

Send a parcel. Pick up the parcel you ordered online because you’re never home. Return the parcel that didn’t fit. The konbini is open 24 hours, which is its main advantage over the post office, which is open while you’re at work.

**The Bank**

An ATM in the corner. Electricity, gas and phone bills. Some taxes. Concert tickets. In Japan you can watch a man in a suit pay a tax in the same place he bought a beer a minute earlier. Nothing happens. Nobody fainted. The tax didn’t get smaller, and the beer didn’t get worse.

**The Records Office**

Here I take my hat off. With a My Number card — Japan’s ID number card — you can print an official certificate of residence on the store’s multifunction machine. A certificate! From city hall! Between the milk and the umbrellas!

I always thought getting an official document required a campaign: early morning, a folder, a reserve of patience. In Japan you pop in for one the way you pop in for bread. And you come out, typically, with both.

A state that issues certificates in a corner shop is a state that once stopped to think about where its citizens actually spend their time.

**The Shelter**

The biggest chains — Seven-Eleven, Lawson, FamilyMart — are on the Japanese government’s list of “designated public institutions” for disasters, alongside utilities, railways and broadcasters. When the ground shakes, the konbini isn’t just expected to stay open but to help: water, food, toilets, information, a way home.

Think about that. There’s a country where the corner shop is officially part of the rescue system. In much of the world the corner shop is officially part of the “closed for stocktaking” system.

## It doesn’t know your name, it knows your hour

**The File on You**

Now about that headline.

In accounts of Japanese konbini from the early 2000s there’s a detail that floored me. The registers had keys for the customer’s gender and age group. You buy an onigiri, and the clerk, without blinking, taps: male, forties. And the system learns: at 11:40 p.m., men over forty buy salmon onigiri and a can of coffee.

They say a spouse knows everything about you. Maybe. A spouse knows what you like. The konbini knows what you buy when you’re feeling low. Between us, those are two different lists.

The store doesn’t know your name. It knows your hour.

## The department that isn’t on the floor plan

**The Confidant**

This department isn’t on the floor plan. But it’s there.

In 2016 the writer Sayaka Murata won the Akutagawa Prize for “Convenience Store Woman.” Its heroine has worked in the same corner store for eighteen years. The store gives her what the world never did: a manual for being a person. What to say, when to smile, how to stand. “Irasshaimase” — and you’re exactly where you belong.

The novel is funny. And very sad. Like everything true.

In 2021 Japan appointed a minister for loneliness. Since April 2024 it has had a law on measures against loneliness and isolation. And a 2024 government survey found that 39.3% of people in Japan feel lonely to some degree.

Thirty-nine percent. More than one in three. Among those walking into a konbini at 11:40 p.m., I suspect, rather more.

So there you are at the register. The clerk is a student, maybe from another country, working the night shift between exams. He doesn’t know you; you don’t know him. He sets your bento on the counter and asks:

“Atatamemasu ka?”

Shall I heat it up?

And you realize that in the whole day nobody else has asked you anything. Not in the meeting, not on the train, not in your messages. Only him. Only this.

Yes, you say. Please. Heat it up.

## Only the konbini glows

Three in the morning. The city is asleep. Windows dark, signs off, taxis cruising empty. Only the konbini glows, like an aquarium where someone left the light on for the fish.

I walk out with water, an onigiri and something else I hadn’t meant to buy. The door slides shut behind me. From inside, no longer meant for me but for whoever walks in next, comes:

“Irasshaimase!”

He doesn’t know who’s coming. He’s glad in advance. At three in the morning that’s not nothing. It may be more than many of us get all day.
