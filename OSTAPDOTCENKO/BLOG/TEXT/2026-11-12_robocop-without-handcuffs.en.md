> Dead or alive, you’re coming with me. — RoboCop, 1987. The 2026 Chinese version says: “Please put on your helmet.”

Generations of Soviet children knew the same policeman. Uncle Styopa, from a children’s poem: enormously tall, endlessly kind, always rescuing someone, and above all, visible from a mile away. He was basically the country’s first traffic light with a human face.

Hollywood’s version of the giant policeman was RoboCop. Armored, armed, and a little grim.

China has built something in between. Height: 1.88 meters. Weight: 98 kilograms. Seven joints in each arm. Polite. Unarmed. On wheels.

No handcuffs included.

## Kind by firmware

Let’s begin with the one that actually works. Since May 1, 2026, a “robot police squadron” of 15 traffic robots has been on duty at downtown intersections in Hangzhou. Reuters later described them in detail: the T2 model from local firm SUPCON Information, 1.88 meters tall, in a vest, with arms that perform traffic-police gestures in sync with the lights.

What it can do: cameras and radar spot e-bike riders without helmets, riders carrying passengers, cars over the stop line and pedestrians crossing on red. It issues a spoken warning. It tells you how to get somewhere and where to park. At West Lake, tourists press an “I want to speak” button and a large language model plans their route.

What it can’t do: arrest anyone, fine anyone, or take anything “on the spot.” The goal, the company says, is to remind people of the rules before a violation becomes a habit, not to punish.

Uncle Styopa was kind by nature. T2 is kind by firmware.

## A hundred and seventy thousand reminders

Now the company’s own numbers. Since May, it says, its Hangzhou robots have issued more than 170,000 warnings, and monthly cases of helmetless riding and stop-line violations have dropped by over 40%. Reuters notes it couldn’t verify the figures, and Hangzhou traffic police didn’t respond.

A hundred and seventy thousand reminders. That’s one very patient mother-in-law for an entire district. Except mothers-in-law get tired.

“It doesn’t get tired,” says Cao Hui, who heads SUPCON’s unmanned systems center. It works from 7 a.m. to 6 p.m., moves between posts on its own, and a central platform lets police monitor and reassign it. A Hangzhou traffic officer told Xinhua such robots can work 8 to 9 hours a day and handle routine tasks.

Eight to nine hours. Exactly a human shift. The robot that never gets tired works the schedule of those who do. There’s a kind of justice in that.

A word about weaknesses, or this would read like an ad. Strong sunlight, rain and extreme temperatures interfere with the cameras and radar. In other words, the robot traffic cop has the same problems as a human one: bad in the heat, bad in the rain, worse in the cold.

The only difference is that a human grumbles in bad weather, while a robot quietly gets it wrong. The company does claim recognition accuracy above 95%.

## A traffic cop also needs a work permit

Hangzhou isn’t alone. In Wuhu, a humanoid with the badge “Intelligent Police Unit R001” stands on a traffic island. It’s made by AiMOGA, the robotics business linked to carmaker Chery. During the May 2026 holidays, robot traffic officers also worked in Kashgar and Ordos. By August, Reuters counted nearly 50 such robots across eight cities in three provinces, and SUPCON expected around 200 by year-end.

AiMOGA’s Zhang Guibing made an interesting point: exporting these robots is harder than exporting a car. They have to plug into local law-enforcement IT systems, get certified and comply with data protection rules. He sees demand where it’s hot, in the Middle East, and where it rains, in Southeast Asia.

You can sell a car. A traffic cop also needs a work permit.

## RoboCop in elementary school

Patrols are a different story.

In February 2025, in Shenzhen’s Nanshan district outside the MixC World mall, an EngineAI PM01 humanoid in police gear walked the beat with officers. It stands 1.38 meters tall and weighs 40 kilograms. Around New Year 2026, a video of a bigger robot marching beside two officers at the Window of the World park went viral, and Western media immediately reached for “Terminator.” Officially it wasn’t described as a regular deployment. It looked like a demo.

At 1.38 meters, it’s RoboCop in elementary school.

Then there’s Wenzhou, where police are assisted by a robot that doesn’t look human at all. Logon Technology’s spherical RT-G: a ball of about 125 kilograms that rolls over land and water at up to 35 km/h and can fire nets, release smoke and tear gas, and blast a siren. The video went around the world. The same video shows the ball wobbling on turns, and reporters suggested an ordinary staircase might end the chase.

The best defense against high-tech pursuit is a building with stairs. I always suspected as much. Now there’s footage.

A scene that never happened but practically writes itself. An intersection. A man on an e-bike, no helmet.

“Sir, please put on your helmet.” “Who are you?” “Traffic control.” “Show me some ID.” “Badge number R001.” “Are you going to fine me?” “No.” “Arrest me?” “No.” “So what are you going to do?” “Remember you.”

He puts on the helmet. Not out of fear. Out of awkwardness.

## Showpiece or worker

So: showpiece or worker? I’ll answer like a marketer, which means honestly and with caveats.

The humanoid walking beside police is, for now, a showpiece. It works for the image of the city, the company, the industry. The net-firing ball is a viral showpiece. But the quiet robot at the Hangzhou intersection, reminding cyclists from seven to six, already looks like a worker. Not a hero. Not a Terminator. More like a very polite sergeant who never takes a day off and never takes anything else either.

A worker with a very narrow job. It was given not authority but the task of reminding. And that may be the smartest part of the whole idea. An intersection is the perfect first job for a robot: the rules are known, the gestures are standard, and the worst that can happen is that nobody listens.

Order doesn’t rest on handcuffs. It rests on being seen.

In the later poems, Uncle Styopa is a retired veteran, and children still look up to him. Not because he arrested anyone, but because he always stood where everyone could see him and helped.

His Chinese successor stands where everyone can see him and helps. He isn’t allowed to arrest anyone, and I think that’s right.

No handcuffs included.

People put on their helmets anyway.
