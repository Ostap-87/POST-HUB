> It’s not the winning, it’s the taking part. Especially if you take part as the supplier. — The Olympic creed, as revised by procurement

I don’t really understand cricket. I know a match can last five days and end in a draw, and everyone considers that normal. I understand table tennis slightly better: points are over in seconds, and China usually wins. So when I set out to describe the India–China match for the cheap GLP-1 market, my first question was which rules we were playing by. Turns out: both.

The whistle blew on March 20, 2026. That day, Novo Nordisk’s core patent on semaglutide, the molecule behind Ozempic and Wegovy, expired in both India and China. One date, two countries, roughly 1.4 billion people on each side of the net. A level playing field, you’d think.

Level playing fields exist only in economics textbooks. And not in every chapter.

India came out like a cricket team: everybody at once, bats raised. In March, Dr. Reddy’s launched the first generic semaglutide injection and Torrent launched tablets. Sun Pharma, Lupin, Glenmark and others followed. The business press counted more than fifty brands on the way. Prices were a fraction of the original: according to Outlook Business, a month of Sun’s version cost around ₹3,400 against Novo’s ₹8,800–10,000. Novo didn’t wait around either; it licensed local firm Emcure to market its own semaglutide.

Fifty brands of one molecule. That’s not a market. That’s a wedding where every guest showed up in the same dress.

India has long been the world’s pharmacy, supplying around a fifth of global generics by volume. On April 28, 2026, Health Canada approved Dr. Reddy’s semaglutide, the first generic of the molecule anywhere in the G7. India is on the board.

Then the ball hit the stumps. In July, Dr. Reddy’s paused commercial production after an impurity turned up in some API batches during scale-up. It cut its half-year forecast from 12 million pens to 6–7 million and did not expect to resume supply before late October or early November. Around the same time, Torrent recalled some batches of its pens. Chemistry World reported that some big players had stayed out of the first wave altogether, calling the Indian market a “bloodbath.”

That’s peptides for you. Thirty-one amino acids, a fatty acid on the side, and one stray impurity is enough to stop a plant. This isn’t aspirin. It’s a clockwork mechanism assembled from beads.

Now China. Same day, same patent and… silence. By March 23, according to Beijing News, ten Chinese companies, including Huadong, Livzon, Qilu, United Laboratories and Jiuyuan, had filed for approval, and eleven more were in Phase III. Approvals so far: zero.

The reason has a bureaucratic poetry to it. Ozempic’s marketing authorisation in China is held by Novo’s Swiss subsidiary, and under the China–Switzerland free trade agreement, undisclosed clinical trial data is protected for at least six years from approval. Approval came in April 2021. So: April 2027. In May 2026, Novo said publicly that it expected Chinese copies to be delayed until next year. Livzon has pencilled in the second quarter of 2027.

The patent expired, but the protection stayed. The lock’s gone, but the door is still nailed shut.

Chinese players aren’t idle, and prices are falling anyway. In December 2025 Novo halved Wegovy prices in Yunnan and Sichuan, and Lilly’s tirzepatide is playing at the same table. Chinese consumers got a price war before a single Chinese generic took the field. It’s like getting your change before you’ve paid.

And here’s where the real ping-pong begins. China isn’t playing on its own court yet, but it’s very active on everyone else’s. Chemistry World lists China’s Sinopep among the API suppliers, alongside Indian firms. Reuters found that in 2024 eight Chinese companies shipped enough semaglutide to the US for over a billion starter doses for compounding pharmacies. When the US shut that market down, Chinese suppliers reportedly pivoted to Canada and Brazil, where patents are expiring too.

So India runs out with the bat, and at least some of the balls are made in China. In cricket, that would be a conflict of interest. In pharma, it’s called a supply chain.

I’m a marketer, so I always ask who pays and who earns. The answer here is simple and slightly insulting to the spectators. The winner isn’t whoever takes the field first. It’s whoever sells kit to both teams.

So who becomes the GLP-1 generics factory? My honest forecast: both, in different ways. India is the packer, the distributor and the daredevil who reaches the market first and gets the first bruises. China is the chemist in the back room with the reactors, who will probably also enter its own market next year with prices that make everyone wince.

A cricket match lasts five days. A ping-pong rally lasts four seconds. The India–China GLP-1 match has been going for six months, and the rules are still being written mid-game.

And the spectators it’s all supposedly for are sitting in the stands, waiting for the medicine to become just a medicine. No hype, no drama, no scoreboard.
