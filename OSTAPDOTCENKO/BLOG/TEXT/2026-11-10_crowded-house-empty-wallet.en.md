> The more, the merrier. — Old proverb. Not written by a venture capitalist

I’m a marketer, which means I’m a professional at one sentence: “We’re not like the others.” I’ve written it for clients more times than I can count. About services, software, ad platforms. And every time I meant it. Briefly, but sincerely.

So when more than 150 Chinese companies all say “we’re not like the others” about the same two-legged robot, I don’t feel irony. I feel professional sympathy. Like an old wedding host at a party with more toasts than guests.

On November 27, 2025, Li Chao, spokesperson for China’s National Development and Reform Commission, said out loud what people at robotics expos had been whispering. More than 150 Chinese companies are building humanoid robots, and over half are startups or firms that switched in from other sectors. Frontier industries, he said, always struggle to balance the speed of growth against the risk of bubbles. The market must not drown in “highly similar” products.

The regulator promised to improve the mechanisms for entry and exit.

Entry has never been the problem. Entry is free. It’s the exit that charges by the hour.

So how many are there really? Depends on who’s counting.

The planning commission said 150-plus. Research firm GGII said 320-plus in early 2026, up from around 120 three years earlier. A venture investor quoted in the Chinese press in September 2026 put the number of embodied AI companies at 200 to 300.

150, 200, 320. Like three witnesses to the same fender-bender: everyone saw it, everyone’s sure, and everyone blames a different car.

Whatever the number, they all want to sleep in the same bed.

Why a bed? Walk any Chinese robotics expo and you’d think humanity’s deepest wish is for someone else to fold its towels. Humanoids fold laundry, make beds, pour tea, stack boxes. Booth after booth, all in the same pose, like a graduating class photo: different faces, one rented suit.

Now the numbers behind the metaphor. According to IDC, about 18,000 humanoids shipped worldwide in 2025. Nearly 60% came from two leaders: Unitree with over 5,500 and AgiBot with about 5,200. Hundreds of other companies split the rest.

Split it fairly and you get a few dozen robots per company. A few dozen isn’t production. It’s a family reunion.

A hundred and fifty humanoids can make the bed. There is one bed.

Now the money, because a bubble without money is just soap.

GGII counts more than 58 billion yuan invested over three years, about 38 billion of it in 2025 alone. In the first half of 2026, Chinese business media report that about 93.4 billion yuan went into embodied AI. More than the whole previous year.

So in November the regulator said “careful, bubble,” and over the next six months investors poured in more than in the entire year before the warning.

I’m not mocking. I’m impressed. The warning was heard. It was just read the way investors read everything: if it’s a bubble, get in before it pops.

China has seen this movie before. In 2023 came the “battle of a hundred models”: more than 200 large language models, each with its own launch event and its own “world first.” A couple of years later, fewer than ten companies were still building their own foundation models.

Call this one the battle of a hundred robots. Investment firm Xinding Capital estimates that of the hundred-plus robotics companies that raised money in 2025, perhaps 10 to 20 will survive 2026. Jiang Lei of China’s national humanoid robotics innovation center says about 80% of complete-robot makers are still building their platforms. Foundations, that is. The house comes later. If it comes.

When everyone is first, the winner is whoever is last to leave.

A short list: “Six signs your humanoid belongs to the surplus hundred.”

1.  It folds a towel in a video sped up four times.
2.  Its main customer is the next trade show.
3.  The pitch deck says “first” more often than “revenue.”
4.  It looks like the robot at the next booth. That robot looks like it. Both look like a Unitree.
5.  A year ago its founder was building something else entirely, and was also first.
6.  There is a sixth sign, but it’s charging.

The grim part is no longer forecasts. It’s court filings.

On October 10, 2025, a Beijing court accepted a bankruptcy liquidation case against the Beijing robotics unit of CloudMinds, once one of China’s service-robot unicorns. Over its life the company raised about 5.4 billion yuan, at a peak valuation above 20 billion. In May 2026, a Shanghai court accepted the bankruptcy of DeepBlue Robotics, where more than a hundred employees are reportedly owed over 23 million yuan in wages and severance. The parent group says it is not bankrupt itself and its core business continues.

Twenty billion in valuation. Twenty million in unpaid wages. Between those two numbers lies the whole distance between a dream and a payroll.

People will say this is normal. It’s how China did solar panels, electric cars and bike sharing, which left behind whole graveyards of brightly colored frames. True. China knows how to overheat an industry and then cool it down. Out of the boiling water comes steel, and out of a hundred companies come five or ten the rest of the world will have to reckon with.

The bed will get made. By whoever is still standing in the morning.

But I’m not thinking about the survivors. I’m thinking about the engineers who taught someone else’s robot to fold towels and are now filing claims with a liquidator. You can switch off a humanoid and put it in a corner. People don’t work that way.

The more, the merrier. That’s true of people.

For robots, so far, it’s just “the more.”
