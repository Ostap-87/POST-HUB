> Beauty is pain. — Folk wisdom. In Japan, the pain is usually the chicken’s.

My blog has a whole series on the drinks of Asia. I’ve had tea on its seventh steeping, Korean soju from the green bottle and things in China better left out of a Tech column. But one Asian drink I kept skipping, because people drink it not for the mood but for the face: collagen in a glass.

Full disclosure: my face is a working marketer’s face, and nothing will help it now except good lighting at a conference. So no advice here on what to drink or how much. Just history, advertising and a scientific argument, which interests me more than wrinkles. Wrinkles aren’t going anywhere. The argument might actually end.

Shiseido launched its first collagen supplements in 1996. By the company’s count, its The Collagen line shipped about 350 million bottles of the drink between March 2009 and April 2025, and ranks first in Japan’s collagen drink segment. Meiji has sold Amino Collagen long enough to call it “a favorite for 23 years,” and also calls itself Japan’s number one collagen supplement.

Two number ones in one market. Perfectly normal; I’ve seen it many times. Number one isn’t a position. It’s a mood.

Japan didn’t stop at bottles. By 2010 Japan was in a collagen-hotpot craze. It started when one restaurant introduced a “beauty nabe,” a hotpot built on chicken-bone broth rich in collagen; others followed, it went nationwide, and manufacturers bottled collagen stock for home cooking. Earlier still came suppon, soft-shell turtle. In 2008 Reuters reported that about 70% of diners at Tokyo suppon restaurants were women, many in their thirties, and that the turtle’s blood was mixed with sake or plum wine. One diner told reporters frankly that no effects had been confirmed, but she felt warm and energetic.

That’s the best review of any beauty product I’ve ever heard. Effect unconfirmed, but warm.

Now the science. The skeptics’ argument was always simple: collagen is a protein, and the stomach breaks protein down into amino acids. Drinking collagen for your skin is like eating bricks to grow taller.

In 2005, researchers from Kyoto Prefectural University and the R&D center of meat company Nippon Meat Packers published a study the industry still cites. After volunteers took gelatin hydrolysate, their blood contained not just amino acids but short peptides, chiefly the dipeptide prolyl-hydroxyproline, peaking within one to two hours. The brick reaches the bloodstream not as sand but as small chunks. Note the co-author: a meat company. In Japan, beauty science eats in the same kitchen as beauty.

Then, in 2025, The American Journal of Medicine published a meta-analysis of 23 randomized trials with 1,474 participants. Pooled, collagen looked decent. Split by funding, trials without industry money showed no benefit; the positive results came largely from industry-funded work. The authors concluded there is currently no clinical evidence that collagen supplements prevent or treat skin aging. The industry pushed back, calling the method flawed and “collagen” too broad a term.

So the debate goes: Scientists: it reaches the blood. Other scientists: not proven to reach the face. Industry: you counted the wrong studies. Customer: I feel warm.

Japan’s government plays referee here, with a whistle but no stopwatch. Since 2015, under the Foods with Function Claims system, a company assembles its own scientific evidence, notifies the Consumer Affairs Agency and prints a function on the pack, such as “helps maintain skin moisture and elasticity.” Fancl’s new collagen jelly, launched in August 2026 with tripeptides it says are easier to absorb, is one such product. After the 2024 Kobayashi red yeast rice scandal, the rules tightened: companies must report health hazards, and from September 1, 2026, GMP is mandatory for supplements in tablet, capsule and powder form.

China was watching. It regulated the category its own way, through a national food safety standard for collagen peptides, GB 31645, in force since 2018. The debate there is the same, only louder: Chinese media run headlines asking whether drinking collagen peptides is a “tax on the gullible,” and answer honestly that absorption happens but a direct link to collagen synthesis in skin isn’t proven. I’d add: it’s the most voluntary tax in the world. People pay it gladly and ask for a receipt.

I won’t judge whether collagen in a glass works. I’ll judge something else: it’s one of Asia’s most beautiful marketing stories. A product scientists have debated for twenty years sells by the hundreds of millions, not because everyone is fooled, but because people aren’t buying a molecule. They’re buying a morning ritual, a small glass promise to themselves.

You can’t drink youth. But you can drink to youth, and that, Japan discovered, is a big market too.

I still side with the woman at the turtle restaurant. Effect unconfirmed. But warm.
