> Game over. Continue? — A question computers ask more honestly than life does

Let me confess right away: I’m not a gamer. My relationship with video games stopped where it did for most people my age — at falling blocks. Since then I’ve watched the games industry the way people who don’t understand ballet watch ballet: beautiful, expensive, unclear why everyone’s so excited.

But games have one thing I’ve envied my whole business life. The “Load Last Save” button. You fail, fall into a pit, get eaten — no problem. You go back to the moment when everything was still fine and try again, now knowing where the pit is.

Business has no such button. But one Japanese company seems to have found it anyway.

First there was the Wii. A console played by grandmothers, kids and whole departments at office parties. More than 101 million sold. Nintendo back then was like a guest who turns up to a party in a strange costume and becomes the most popular person in the room.

In November 2012 its successor arrived: the Wii U. The big idea was a controller with its own screen. Play on the TV, or on that screen while someone else in the family watches a show.

The idea, as it turned out, was good. The name was not.

In spring 2013, Nintendo president Satoru Iwata said something few CEOs would say in public: “We feel deeply responsible for not having tried hard enough to have consumers understand the product.” He added that some people thought the Wii U GamePad was an accessory for the old Wii.

As a marketer I know this mistake by heart. You invent something new but are so afraid of losing the old that you name the new thing almost like the old one. You add one letter. The customer looks at the letter, shrugs, and decides he already has one.

One letter cost Nintendo several years.

Now the dry numbers, the kind read out to shareholders with a straight face. In January 2014 Nintendo cut its Wii U forecast for the fiscal year from 9 million to 2.8 million and swapped a projected profit for a loss. It ended up selling 2.72 million that year. Operating loss: 46.4 billion yen. The third year in a row.

Lifetime Wii U sales: 13.56 million. Seven and a half times fewer than the Wii. One of the biggest flops in the company’s history.

People compare failure to a fall. This wasn’t a fall. It was a very polite, very Japanese slide down the stairs. One step at a time, with a bow on each.

And now the reason I sat down to write this.

In this situation, every corporation in the world has a standard recipe: cut people. Analysts applaud, the stock ticks up, the press release says “optimization.” At the 2013 shareholders’ meeting, Iwata was asked why Nintendo wasn’t cutting staff. He answered that cutting employees for better short-term results would hurt morale. “I sincerely doubt employees who fear that they may be laid off will be able to develop software titles that could impress people around the world.”

He started the cuts with himself. In January 2014 Iwata cut his own pay by 50%, Shigeru Miyamoto’s by 30%, and other directors’ by 20%. He had already halved his salary once before, in 2011, when Nintendo had to slash the price of the 3DS.

Laying people off is a reboot. Keeping them is loading the last save. The difference is that in the second case, the experience isn’t erased.

Allow me an interlude. A classified ad nobody ever posted. I made it up.

*For sale: Wii U.*

*Excellent condition, barely used. Not a Wii accessory. A separate console. No, really, separate.*

*GamePad with screen included. Play while your relatives watch TV. Relatives not included.*

*Reason for sale: a new one came out. Also has a screen, but now the screen IS the console.*

*Price negotiable. No Switch trades, please — I want one myself.*

In March 2015 Iwata announced that Nintendo was working on a new platform codenamed NX. On July 11, 2015, he died. He was 55.

He never saw how the story ended. That still bothers me a little, though of course I never knew him. At the 2005 Game Developers Conference he described himself this way: “On my business card, I am a corporate president. In my mind, I am a game developer. But in my heart, I am a gamer.” A good order of things. Many executives I’ve met keep it the other way round: in their hearts, they carry a business card.

On March 3, 2017, the Nintendo Switch came out. Look at what’s in it. A screen you can take away from the TV — the very idea of the Wii U, finally carried all the way: now the console is the screen. A name you can’t confuse with anything. And games.

The same day saw the release of The Legend of Zelda: Breath of the Wild, on two platforms at once: the new Switch and the old Wii U. Failure and success launched on the same day with the same game.

Mario Kart 8, released on the Wii U in 2014, was reissued for Switch as Mario Kart 8 Deluxe. By mid-2026 it had sold 71.5 million copies. That’s more than five times the number of Wii U consoles ever sold. A game that once lacked an audience simply waited for a bigger stage.

By summer 2026 the Switch had sold 156.59 million units. By the end of 2025 it had overtaken the Nintendo DS to become the best-selling console in the company’s history. Its successor, Switch 2, launched in June 2025, sold more than 23 million in just over a year.

The Switch blueprint was drawn on the wreckage of the Wii U. Almost nothing was thrown away — not the ideas, not the games, not the people who made them.

I work a lot with Asian companies, and I notice that failure there is often handled differently than at home. Not “tear it all down and start over,” but “keep everything that works and redo what doesn’t.” It’s slower. It’s duller for the press. But it seems to be the only way not to lose along the way the thing it all started for.

Business has no “Load Last Save” button. But you can build one by hand. All it takes is not deleting the people who remember where the pit is.

Iwata saved the game. Others got to load it.
