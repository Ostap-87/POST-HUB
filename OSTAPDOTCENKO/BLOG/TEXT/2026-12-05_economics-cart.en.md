> Big business starts with a small cart. Small business starts with a big business plan. — From a lecture I never got around to giving

For years I’ve been explaining to companies how to sell. Funnels, leads, touchpoints, customer journeys. I say “touchpoint” with a straight face, even though in all those years I’ve never actually touched a customer. It isn’t done.

Then one evening I walk into a night market and the smoke touches me. Instantly. No cookie banner, no consent form. The smoke takes me by the nose and leads me three rows over to a cart where a woman in an apron is flipping something in a wok without looking at the wok. She’s looking at me. She knows I’ve already bought. I don’t know it yet.

That is a funnel. Twenty meters downwind. Conversion rate: one hundred percent.

Let me show you what this business would look like if someone pitched it to me. I’ve sat through a lot of pitch decks. This one would be the best.

**Investment Memorandum. Project “Cart”**

*CapEx.* One cart. One wok. One gas cylinder. One light bulb. The bulb is essential: without it you have a product but not a business.

*OpEx.* Oil, flour, scallions. A neighbor who keeps an eye on things while you run out for more scallions.

*Headcount.* Husband and wife. The board of directors meets at the same wok. Daily. No minutes are kept, but everyone can hear the decisions.

*Product range.* One SKU. Occasionally one and a half.

*Marketing.* Smell. Reach: three alleys, four with a tailwind.

*Competitive advantage.* Twenty years of the same flick of the wrist.

*Scaling plan.* A second wok.

*Risks.* Rain. City inspectors. The neighbor selling the exact same pancake for five yuan less under a brighter bulb.

*Exit strategy.* 6 a.m. Every day.

I’d invest. They wouldn’t take my money, of course: no shareholders, no openings, and a queue longer than the one at any trade show booth I’ve ever staffed.

A one-item menu isn’t poverty. It’s strategy.

Corporations pay consultants a fortune to be told one word: “Focus.” The stinky tofu lady focused for free some thirty years ago and hasn’t been distracted since. She doesn’t have a product line. She has tofu. And a queue.

A Michelin star is for restaurants. For a cart, the honor is a queue. A queue is more honest: you can’t buy one, you can only stand in it.

To be fair, Michelin did come to the carts. Taipei’s 2019 Bib Gourmand list included 24 street food stalls from eight night markets: stinky tofu, black pepper buns at Raohe, herbal pork rib soup, scallion pancakes. The inspector came to the cart. The cart did not go to the inspector. It had an evening to get through.

In Taiwan a night market isn’t a market so much as an institution. According to Taiwan’s Tourism Administration, more than 80% of international visitors put night markets on their itinerary. Museums are optional; Shilin is mandatory. I get it. A museum shows you how people lived. A night market shows you how they live.

On the mainland, the cart has a more complicated biography. China alternately loves its carts and chases them off, and, as with everything in China, does both at scale.

In the spring of 2020, as the economy came out of lockdown and people needed to earn something, the premier publicly stood up for street vending. The “street stall economy” — ditan jingji — became the phrase of the season. He held up Chengdu as an example: the city allowed 36,000 stalls and, he said, gained 100,000 jobs. The stock market reacted at once: shares in companies making catering equipment and vending vans hit their daily trading limit several days running.

The cart went public. No IPO. It just rolled out.

A few days later the Beijing press clarified that stalls did not suit the image of the capital. The provinces sell, the capital observes. Fair enough: in any country you can push a cart anywhere except where it’s clearly visible.

The cart is the only business with regulatory evasion built into the design. Wheels.

I stand at a fried-noodle cart somewhere in China and run the numbers. Occupational hazard.

Order cycle: under a minute. Warehouse: under the cart. Logistics: a hundred meters from home. Payments: a QR code taped on, one corner already peeling. No office. Barely any rent. Marketing: smell. Customer feedback: the face of the person who just took a bite — the fastest analytics I have ever seen. No dashboard will tell you in a second and a half whether the product works. A face will.

In my industry we test products for months. Here it takes one bite. And, annoyingly, it’s more accurate.

The cart also keeps books that never make it into any report.

It liquidates every morning and re-registers every evening. No lawyer, no notary, no articles of association. Just one unwritten article: however tired you are, you come back tomorrow.

I’ve seen how it ends. By midnight the market thins out. The bulbs go off one by one, like a theater after the show. An elderly man wipes down his wok, stacks the stools, ties down the gas cylinder. Slowly, carefully, with the same flick of the wrist he’s used on noodles for twenty years. He pushes the cart home. One wheel squeaks. Nobody has replaced that in twenty years either.

And I, a man who draws business models for a living, suddenly realize that the most resilient business model I’ve seen in Asia doesn’t rest on investors or algorithms. It rests on one man’s back.

In the morning this spot will be a parking space. In the evening, smoke again.
