> Genius is simple. Especially when you close the loop. — Folk wisdom, peer-reviewed by chemists

As a kid I tried to spin a hula hoop. It always hit the floor before I could locate my waist. Ever since, I’ve treated rings with respect and suspicion: wedding rings, onion rings, boxing rings. Hiroaki Suga, a professor at the University of Tokyo, has a different relationship with rings. He bends medicines into them. And unlike mine, his don’t fall.

We have to start with the guitar, because Suga did. He told Bloomberg he wanted to be a guitarist when he was young. When that didn’t work out, he went looking for the origin of life instead. He hasn’t found it yet, but along the way he found a new way to make drugs, and a company worth billions grew around it.

Now that’s a career pivot. Most people who give up the guitar end up in accounting. Suga gave it up and went to the ribosome.

They say an electric guitar still stands in his office. Science is science, but you always keep a backup plan.

The science, briefly and without formulas. A peptide is a chain of amino acids. An ordinary peptide behaves like a strand of cooked pasta: floppy, soft and digested by enzymes before it can do much. Suga and his colleagues found a way to make ring-shaped peptides. Bloomberg put it neatly: the old ones were spaghetti; the new ones are hula hoops. A ring is stiffer, more stable and better at wedging itself between two proteins that should never have met.

The key tool is called a flexizyme, an artificial ribozyme that lets the ribosome, the cell’s natural assembly line, build chains with amino acids that don’t exist in the human body. Nature gave the ribosome a menu of twenty dishes. Suga brought it a full buffet.

Then comes selection. One test tube holds a library of roughly a trillion different peptides, and the RaPID method quickly fishes out the ones that bind to the target. Together this is PDPS, the Peptide Discovery Platform System. According to the company’s chief science officer, finding “hits” went from about three days to about four hours.

A trillion candidates in one tube. We get forty CVs for a marketing job and spend a week unable to pick one.

PeptiDream was founded in 2006. Suga invented the technology; co-founder Kiichi Kubota built the business. The University of Tokyo’s venture fund invested in 2008, and its partner recalls cleaning their first university office and carrying in chairs herself. Sceptics said the model couldn’t work: which pharma company would share its secret targets with a tiny start-up?

They did. PeptiDream listed on the Tokyo Mothers market in June 2013, and its roster of big-pharma partners grew past a dozen. Back in 2016 Suga gave Bloomberg a line I’d hang over the door: “Everybody comes to PeptiDream.”

That’s how Japanese science works. It doesn’t chase you with a pitch deck. It sits in its lab, and sooner or later you come to it, with paperwork and a chequebook.

Speaking of chequebooks: in 2024 Novartis expanded its long-running partnership with PeptiDream, paying $180 million upfront with up to $2.71 billion more in milestones. The focus is macrocyclic peptides, including for radioligand therapy, where the peptide ring acts as a courier delivering a radioactive payload to the right address. A year earlier, Genentech signed a similar deal worth up to $1 billion.

A courier who never loses a parcel and always knows the address. Logistics hasn’t managed that yet. Chemistry has.

In 2023 Suga received the Wolf Prize in Chemistry “for developing RNA-based catalysts that revolutionized the discovery of bioactive peptides.” His co-founder once said he hoped Suga would win a Nobel. I’m not the Nobel committee, but I’ll say this: guitarists rarely get that far.

Now the sad part, because rings have a downside. To stop living on partner payments alone, PeptiDream bought Fujifilm Toyama Chemical’s radiopharmaceutical business in 2022. That brought its own products to market, but also amortisation and, according to analysts, a revenue dip and an operating loss in 2025. Platform science is wonderful while someone else pays for it. When you start paying yourself, the ring can also turn into a vicious circle.

The rings themselves, meanwhile, have proved the genre is alive. In July 2026 the US FDA approved Merck’s enlicitide, the first oral PCSK9 inhibitor for lowering cholesterol. It is a macrocyclic peptide, exactly the class of molecule the Japanese school has spent years bending into hoops. A peptide in a pill. Not long ago that sounded like fried ice.

And where does China come in, a regular reader might ask. Rings have to be made of something. If the Japanese school is the jeweller’s workshop where the shape is invented, China is the foundry that melts the metal and draws the wire: amino acids, reagents, resins. More on that next time.

For now, just this. Science sometimes goes in circles, and not because it’s lost. Sometimes a ring isn’t a dead end. It’s the most stable shape there is.

I never did master the hula hoop. But now I know it wasn’t my waist. I just have the wrong amino acids.
