All my life I pictured world heritage like this: pyramids, cathedrals, the Great Wall. Something big and made of stone that you look up at and must not touch.

In Singapore, world heritage comes on a plastic plate. You can touch it. In fact you have to touch it, or it gets cold.

On December 16, 2020, UNESCO added Singapore’s hawker culture to the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity. A hawker is a street-food seller. A hawker centre is the roof he was moved under, years ago. Under the roof: tables, stools, ceiling fans and dozens of stalls, each doing one or two dishes. Chicken rice, laksa, fried noodles, fish soup, and milk tea poured from a height, as if to test its nerve.

UNESCO’s evaluators described hawker centres in two words I’d put over every entrance: “community dining rooms.” Not restaurants. Dining rooms. Shared ones. The banker and the cab driver eat at the same table because there isn’t another free one.

Equality is when there’s no free table for anybody.

In a hawker centre I’m useless. Forty stalls, forty signs, forty queues. By the time I’ve chosen, a local has eaten, cleared up and gone back to work. I stand there like a tourist in a museum where every exhibit smells wonderful and there’s no guide.

Why do noodles need protection? It took me a while. Then I read the history: they’re not protected from enemies. They’re protected from disappearing.

In 1966 Singapore started licensing street hawkers; it counted about 24,000 of them. In 1971 a committee for building hawker centres was set up. They built, moved people, persuaded. The last street hawkers, about eighty of them, went indoors in early 1986. The streets emptied. There were more than a hundred centres by then.

Fifteen years of moving. Cart by cart, wok by wok. Other states have relocated entire peoples. Singapore relocated lunch.

Today hawker centres fall under the National Environment Agency. Its list for August 2026 has 123 of them. In Singapore, noodles are an environmental matter. Fair enough. For a Singaporean, that’s what the environment is.

The state protects the noodles.

Speaking of property rights.

If there’s a packet of tissues on an empty table, the table is taken. It’s called “chope.” The owner of the tissues is in the chicken-rice queue and can stay there as long as he likes. His claim is secure.

Not by law. By something stronger. You can challenge a law in court. You can’t challenge a packet of tissues.

I once watched a grown man holding a full tray stand over a table with nothing on it but tissues, looking at them as if they were a minefield. He stood there. Sighed. Moved on.

The most reliable lock in the world costs less than any lock. And nobody ever picks it.

Now, the tray.

For years other people cleared up after diners. From June 1, 2021, you have to return your own tray and dishes, and from September 1 it’s enforced. First offence: a written warning. Second: a 300-dollar fine.

Let’s do the maths. In 2022–2023, researchers from the Institute of Policy Studies surveyed more than 800 eateries: hawker centres, food courts, coffee shops. Breakfast there cost S$4.81 on average. There are my five dollars.

So breakfast is five and a tray left behind for the second time is three hundred. The tray is worth sixty breakfasts.

Food prices are kept low here. Bad manners are priced high. As far as I can tell, that’s the entire economic policy.

The state protects the noodles. And the crockery from the diners.

There was a Michelin star, too. In 2016, Singapore’s first Michelin guide gave one star to a stall in Chinatown selling soy sauce chicken rice for about two Singapore dollars. The cheapest star on the planet. The queue stretched for hours.

Then the investors arrived. A chain, outlets abroad, a new name, Hawker Chan, a bigger menu. In the 2021 guide, the star was gone.

The star came to the stall. The stall went off after the star. Somewhere along the way they missed each other.

I’m not judging. Everyone has a right to a franchise. It’s just that heritage, it turns out, doesn’t scale. You can’t open it in a mall. It doesn’t relocate. It stands at the stove.

And now the main thing, which isn’t funny.

In 2021 the median hawker was sixty years old, and the number keeps rising. In a 2020 survey, 82.5% of residents said they bought from hawker centres at least once a week, and 87.3% said they had no wish to become hawkers. Long days, heat, up before dawn.

Everyone wants to eat. Nobody wants to cook. Maybe that’s what heritage means: something everybody loves and nobody wants to do.

The state can see it. In 2020 it launched a training programme for new hawkers, and in 2021 a succession scheme: if a veteran has no one in the family to take over, he can hand the stall and the recipe to a stranger. Even a minister, speaking about the programme, admitted it wouldn’t make the trade thrive overnight.

That’s more honest than any advertising. You can’t save noodles by decree. You can only cook them. Tomorrow. At six a.m.

Evening. The hawker centre empties. The fans turn for no one. An old man behind a counter washes his wok in slow circles, the same motion someone else probably used on that wok forty years ago.

On the next table lies a packet of tissues. Somebody claimed the seat and never came back.

UNESCO protects the culture. The culture is protected by a sixty-year-old man with a ladle.

And the table stays taken. Until morning. Just in case.
