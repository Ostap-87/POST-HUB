> Every safety rule is somebody’s bad day, written down neatly. — The author, from a safe distance

My profession loves fire. Hot deals, burning deadlines, “a product that will set the market alight,” “explosive growth.” I’ve written those words in decks for years and never once had to call the fire brigade. That’s the beauty of a metaphor: it doesn’t smoke.

Then one day the market took it literally. Ever since, whenever I type “hot offer” to a client, I pause for a second.

On August 2, 2016, Samsung unveiled the Galaxy Note 7 in New York. It went on sale on August 19. A big screen, a stylus, an iris scanner and a 3,500 mAh battery with the highest energy density of any Samsung device at the time. Reviewers loved it.

Energy density is a wonderful thing. Especially when the energy stays inside.

Within days, reports came in of phones overheating and catching fire. In the US alone, Samsung received 92 such reports: 26 involving burns, 55 involving property damage. On September 2, the company announced a recall of 2.5 million phones in ten markets. Owners got replacements. New, checked, safe.

The safe replacements started burning too.

In early October, a Southwest flight was evacuated before takeoff after a powered-off Note 7 started smoking. On October 9, Samsung stopped exchanges and US carriers stopped sales. On October 11, Samsung announced that Note 7 production was ending for good.

On October 14, the US Department of Transportation issued an order few consumer gadgets have ever earned. From noon on October 15, a Note 7 could not travel on your person, in carry-on, in checked bags or as cargo. Passengers carrying one were denied boarding. Sneaking one aboard meant fines and possible prosecution.

A phone that didn’t want to fly. Nobody made it.

Allow me an intermission. I made up this announcement, but airlines really were warning passengers about the Note 7 before every flight back then, in roughly these words.

*“Ladies and gentlemen, before takeoff, please return your seatbacks to the upright position, fasten your seatbelts and inform a crew member if you have a Samsung Galaxy Note 7. Inform a crew member. Not your followers. Emergency exits are located on both sides of the aircraft. Passengers with a Galaxy Note 7 are kindly asked to use the exit now, while we’re still at the gate. Your crew wishes you a pleasant flight. And your phone a pleasant stay on the ground.”*

I fly around Asia a lot. I’ve never seen a product get this much airtime: it was announced on every flight. Marketers dream of reach like that. Just with a different script.

Now some dry numbers, the kind companies read aloud with very straight backs. In Q3 2016, Samsung’s mobile division made 100 billion won in operating profit. That was down 95% and the lowest in nearly eight years. The total cost of the disaster was estimated at no less than $5.3 billion.

The expensive part of a recall isn’t the phones. You recall trust along with the product, and trust is always out of stock.

Here’s what I actually sat down to write about. Not the fire. What came after.

Samsung could have quietly buried the model, changed the name and moved on. Instead it ran an investigation with about 700 researchers and engineers. They tested more than 200,000 assembled phones and over 30,000 batteries. UL and Exponent in the US examined the batteries separately; Germany’s TÜV Rheinland looked at the supply chain. On January 23, 2017, in Seoul, mobile chief Koh Dong-jin faced the press, apologized and explained what they’d found.

Two different defects from two different suppliers.

In the first battery, the casing was too tight at one corner for what was packed into it. The electrodes bent, opening the door to a short circuit. In the second, from a supplier the press identified as China’s ATL, the welding was, in Exponent’s words, “poorly controlled,” leaving sharp burrs that wore through the insulation between layers. Some cells were missing insulation tape altogether.

One was too small for what it carried. The other was sloppy at the seams. Come to think of it, that describes half the people I know, me included.

Koh called the episode a chance to strengthen lithium-ion battery safety for the whole industry. Coming from a man whose flagship had just gone up in smoke, it sounds odd. But it’s honest: it’s better to learn from other people’s fires than from your own, and somebody had to burn first.

The main outcome was the 8-Point Battery Safety Check. I’ll list it in full because I consider it the finest poem a corporation has ever written.

Durability testing: overcharging, nail puncture, extreme temperatures. Visual inspection against standard criteria. X-ray. Large-scale charge and discharge. A test for leaking volatile organic compounds. Disassembly, checking tab welds and insulation tape. Accelerated usage, as if the phone had been used for a long time without mercy. And voltage checks all the way from component to finished device.

Look at point six. Disassembly, welds, insulation tape. Exactly what burned.

Every line in a safety manual is somebody’s fire. You just can’t always tell whose.

And now the epilogue, without which this column would have a different name.

By January 2017, about 96% of sold Note 7s had come back. That summer, South Korea got the Galaxy Note FE, the “Fan Edition,” built from unused Note 7 parts. About 400,000 units, roughly 30% cheaper. The battery: 3,200 mAh instead of 3,500.

To come back, it had to get a little weaker. I know that feeling too.

And in September 2017, the Note 8 collected about 850,000 pre-orders in Korea in eight days. The Note 7 had managed 380,000 in thirteen. Samsung didn’t rename the line. It didn’t hide it. It started checking it.

I’ve worked with Chinese and Asian factories for years, and I noticed long ago that the best engineers there aren’t the ones who never had a fire. They’re the ones who can tell you from memory where it burned and why. They carry their own eight-point check, written not in a manual but on their hands.

So my decks will keep their hot deals and explosive growth. Metaphors don’t smoke.

But next to them, in my head, there’s now a small footnote: “X-rayed.”
