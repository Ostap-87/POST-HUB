> A peptide is a protein that dropped out of school. — The author’s own definition. Chemists, please don’t write in.

I read labels. It comes with the job: a marketer reads a label the way a pickpocket reads a crowd, spotting the valuables at once. On a Chinese face cream, the valuable used to be the word “France.” Or “Switzerland.” Or at least “Japan,” in small print on the back. Science in Chinese cosmetics was imported for a long time, like good cognac: you have your own, but you pour the foreign one for guests.

Now the jar says “acetyl tyrosyl cyclo-D-glutamyl tripeptide-110 amide,” and it’s Chinese companies writing it about Chinese chemistry. It sounds like a flight number I’ve already missed.

## Even beauty starts with paperwork

Even beauty in China starts with paperwork. Since 2021, an ingredient not on the official inventory can’t simply be stirred in. High-risk ones (preservatives, sunscreen filters, colorants, whitening agents, hair dyes) need full registration with the NMPA, the National Medical Products Administration. Everything else goes through filing, which roughly translates as “notify and wait.” Then the ingredient spends three years under safety monitoring, like a new hire on probation, except this probation is real.

The numbers: 6 new ingredients in 2021, 42 in 2022, 69 in 2023, 90 in 2024 and 169 in 2025. By mid-July 2026, another 124 had been filed, bringing the total past 500. Foreign companies account for about one filing in seven.

Six a year is a high-school chemistry club. A hundred and sixty-nine is a faculty.

## Like “artisanal” on a menu

Peptides were the hottest category of 2024: eight filings from seven companies. In October 2025 alone, Shenzhen-based Weiqi, Proya and Harbin’s Fuerjia each added peptides of their own; 16 of that month’s 17 filings were Chinese.

Nobody can say “acetyl tyrosyl cyclo” in a TV spot, so the ads sell the single word “peptide.” It’s short, sounds scientific and promises nothing in particular. Marketing gold.

“Peptide” on a jar is like “artisanal” on a menu: nobody knows what it means, but you instantly know the price.

## 1,400 jars, one sea snail

Now the story I actually came to tell. In April 2023, Weiqi filed an ingredient known in China as the “cone snail peptide,” after the sea mollusk whose venom inspired the original molecule. Filing number 20230010; maximum concentration in a finished product: 0.0025%. That isn’t a drop. That’s the memory of a drop.

Trade outlet Qingyan then counted at least 1,400 cosmetics with “cone snail peptide” in their names. Six products from three companies had permission to use the newly filed ingredient. Many of the rest, reporters found, contained a different, long-approved arginine/lysine polypeptide. The snail was on the box.

1,400 jars, one sea snail. Nobody asked the snail.

I’m not laughing at my Chinese colleagues. I recognize myself. Half of global marketing runs on names traveling faster than contents. The difference is that China now has a registry where the contents can be checked by number, which is worse news for the industry than any fine.

## The evolution of the face-cream jar

The evolution of the Chinese face-cream jar, as I’ve observed it:

1.  “Made with French technology.”
2.  “With a French ingredient.”
3.  “With our own ingredient, filed with the NMPA.”
4.  “With an ingredient whose supplier is listed on the stock exchange.”
5.  “Cone snail peptide,” on the box. Item five coexists happily with all the others.

About that stock exchange. Weiqi, founded in 2011 by Ding Wenfeng, author of a book on skin-active peptides, listed in Beijing on July 27, 2026, billed as the first “peptide raw material” stock in cosmetics. It opened at more than double its IPO price. Proya and Giant Biogene came in as strategic investors: customers buying into their supplier. 2025 revenue: 275 million yuan, with a 79% gross margin on raw materials. Its 23 filed ingredients tie it with Botanee, owner of Winona, for first place in the country.

A 79% margin. In advertising, we only see numbers like that in dreams. Apparently science is where margin comes not from thin air but from a test tube. Then again, who says thin air doesn’t sell? I’ve sold plenty. We called it “reach.”

## Science in a jar is a habit of checking

I watch all this with the kind of envy polite people call respect. Not long ago, Chinese beauty rented its science abroad, the way you rent a suit for a wedding: expensive, one night only, not quite your size. Now it tailors its own. Sometimes crooked, sometimes with an extra snail on the pocket. But its own, and with a registry number.

Science in a jar isn’t a molecule. It’s the habit of checking what’s written on the jar.

I do feel for the snail, though. Fourteen hundred creams named after it, and not one free sample.
