This is the last piece in the column. I’m no good at endings. In advertising there are no endings — just the next brief.

Nine times I’ve sat on the sidelines and watched. The square where the grannies dance. The city without wallets. The courier in the yellow jacket. The young people who lay down flat. The cart with one frying pan. The Japanese store that never turns off the lights. Singapore’s noodles under state protection. The Vietnamese traffic. The thermos.

I looked ahead, sideways, out of train windows. Not once did I look down.

And down there — socks.

Sock, old friend.

You were with me at every meeting. In every factory where they hand you shoe covers. Everywhere in Asia where shoes come off at the door and suddenly you’re the most visible part of me. I never once asked where you came from.

I’m asking now. Honestly, I don’t know. But statistically, the odds that you’re from Datang are better than one in three.

Datang is a district of Zhuji, in Zhejiang province. According to trade publications, it produces around 25 billion pairs of socks a year — roughly 70% of China’s output and more than a third of the world’s. There are about 13,000 sock and sock-related businesses and up to 200,000 workers. Xinhua put it more simply: three pairs for every person on Earth. Every year.

I checked my drawer. I’m short. Someone must be wearing mine.

According to China News Service, it began in the late 1970s. By day, farmers worked in the collective production team. By night, they knitted socks on the quiet. A pair of socks earned one yuan. A day in the production team paid four jiao. So one pair knitted at night was worth two and a half days of work in daylight.

There’s China’s whole economic reform on a single page. By day you work for the plan. By night you work for yourself. And by morning it turns out the night shift pays better.

The machines were hand-cranked. One pair, turn after turn, until a sock came out. By the mid-1980s around 70% of Datang’s residents were in the sock trade — buying yarn, knitting, selling, fixing machines. In 1991 the first market opened, with nearly two thousand stalls.

The town that used to knit by night now stays up officially.

Speaking of small things that hold the world together.

In Yongjia County there’s a place called Qiaotou — “China’s button capital.” In the late 1970s, People’s Daily reports, a village official named Wang Suinai started selling buttons from a stall by the stone bridge, with about 500 yuan in capital. Her first week’s takings reportedly beat her family’s annual income. In 1983 a market opened on the bridge, and the first 42 households got licenses. At one point Qiaotou held around 90% of China’s button market. Today, according to the same paper, it turns out about 100 billion buttons a year.

Let’s consider what hangs on a button.

The button holds the jacket. The jacket holds the look. The look holds the negotiation. The negotiation holds the contract. The contract holds the factory. The factory holds the town.

And the town, as it turns out, hangs on a button. Full circle. Buttoned up.

Now, fire.

Shaodong, in Hunan, makes over 15 billion lighters a year — about 70% of the world’s supply. It has 114 manufacturers, and more than 200 lighter components can be sourced within a 20-kilometer radius. On one line, People’s Daily reports, automation cut the crew from 200 to 40 while output rose ninefold.

Every time someone, somewhere, lights a cigarette, a birthday candle or a campfire, a statistic quietly ticks over in Hunan.

Great things are made for history. Small things are made for everyone.

And since this is coming out at the end of the year — the tree.

Yiwu, also in Zhejiang, calls itself the world’s biggest hub for Christmas goods. According to Xinhua, it makes about two-thirds of the world’s festive products: more than 20,000 items, shipped to over 100 countries. In 2025, orders didn’t wait for autumn — they started arriving in April. One Kuwaiti buyer quoted by the agency was already choosing Santa hats. Not for this Christmas. For the next one.

Look at your tree. You’re still decorating it, and in Yiwu it’s already last year’s model.

In China, the holidays start in April. They’re just celebrated in December, like everywhere else.

Here’s what I wanted to say before the column runs out.

We look at China and see the big things. Bullet trains, humanoid robots, skyscrapers, factories the size of towns. I’ve looked that way myself. Nine times in this column I started big: a hundred million dancers, over a billion internet users, seven and a half million couriers.

But China starts in each of us with something small. A sock. A button. A lighter in the pocket of someone who doesn’t even smoke and carries it “just in case.” A bauble on your tree that will never tell you it was made in April.

Whole towns spend their lives thinking about things nobody thinks about. So that nobody has to.

The best object is the one you never notice. It means it works.

Now that the column is ending, I reread it and see that all nine pieces were about the same thing. People you can’t see for the scale. The granny with the speaker. The courier without a face. The old man pushing his cart home. The cashier asking, “Shall I heat that up?” The hawker with his ladle. The woman who walked into the traffic. The hand that tops up your cup.

And the farmer in Datang who, in the late 1970s, turned the crank of a hand machine by a light it was wiser not to switch on.

He must be very old now. Or gone. He doesn’t know I wrote about him. He doesn’t know that his socks — or his grandchildren’s — are on my feet today.

I take off my shoes. I look at my sock. Ordinary. Dark. No label.

The man who watched what happens has finally looked down.

Thank you for walking this column with me.
