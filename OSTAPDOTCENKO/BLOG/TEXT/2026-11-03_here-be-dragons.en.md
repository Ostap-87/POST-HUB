> Raising a dragon is easy. Not asking for twenty years when it will fly is the hard part. — Ancient wisdom, invented by me just now

Old mapmakers used to write “Here be dragons” at the edge of the known world. It meant: nobody’s been there, and nobody knows what’s out there. Cartographers were careful people. Wherever they lacked data, they put monsters.

In China, dragons have a better reputation. A dragon isn’t a monster there but a respected creature, treated roughly the way a good CFO is treated: slightly feared, deeply valued. And if today’s tech map has a “Here be dragons” label anywhere, it’s over Hangzhou.

Hangzhou isn’t where you’d expect a revolution. An old Chinese saying goes: “Above there is heaven; below there are Suzhou and Hangzhou.” West Lake, willows, pagodas, and centuries of poets who came here to be sad in style. It’s also home to Longjing tea, whose name means “Dragon Well”.

So dragons have lived here for a long time. They just used to live in a well and get brewed at 80 degrees.

## Six Little Dragons of Hangzhou

In early 2025 the Chinese internet coined the phrase “Six Little Dragons of Hangzhou.” City officials picked it up in their New Year message to local business, and since then everyone from analysts to taxi drivers repeats it. Here they are.

**DeepSeek.** Grew out of Liang Wenfeng’s hedge fund High-Flyer. On January 27, 2025, after its R1 model came out, Nvidia lost about $589 billion in market value in a single trading day, the largest one-day drop in U.S. market history. A hedge fund that never meant to scare anyone ended up scaring everyone, other hedge funds included.

**Game Science.** The studio behind Black Myth: Wukong, a game about the Monkey King. In August 2024 it sold 10 million copies in three days. In the classic novel, the Monkey King spent five hundred years trapped under a mountain. In Hangzhou, he got out in three days.

**Unitree.** Wang Xingxing’s robot dogs and humanoids, founded in 2016. I’ve already written about its dancing robots and its Shanghai IPO, so I’ll just add that its early support came from a city startup program and subsidies from Binjiang district.

**Deep Robotics.** Robot dogs for inspecting power grids, factories and emergency sites. Founded in 2017; co-founder Zhu Qiuguo is a Zhejiang University professor. In 2025 revenue reached 337 million yuan, and the company turned its first profit: 28.7 million. In May 2026 its STAR Market listing application in Shanghai was accepted for review.

**BrainCo.** Non-invasive brain-computer interfaces: bionic hands and legs, sleep and focus devices. Founded in 2015 by Han Bicheng and incubated at the Harvard Innovation Labs. It raised about 2 billion yuan in a single round and, according to Bloomberg, confidentially filed for a Hong Kong IPO in January 2026.

**Manycore.** Cloud-based 3D interior design software, founded in 2011. It became the first of the six dragons to list: Hong Kong, April 17, 2026, at HK$7.62 a share. The retail tranche was oversubscribed 1,591 times, and the stock opened more than 170% higher.

To sum up: artificial intelligence, a mythical monkey, a robot dog, another robot dog, mind reading and kitchen design.

The last one only looks out of place. When the robots take over, they’ll need somewhere to live. Someone is already drawing their kitchens.

## No recipe, but there are ingredients

So how do you grow dragons? There’s no recipe, but there are ingredients.

One: a university. Zhejiang University alumni founded three of the six. Two: Alibaba, which has spent years teaching local engineers to think at scale. Three: a city startup program launched back in 2010, whose first round backed a thousand young companies over five years, especially those founded by graduates and returnees from abroad. Four: help with rent. Xihu district waived or subsidized rent for digital content companies, including game studios, for up to three years.

And five, the rarest ingredient of all: time.

Black Myth: Wukong was first shown in 2020 and released in 2024. Unitree took ten years to go from founding to listing. Manycore took fifteen. And for all that time, as far as anyone can tell, the city didn’t ask every quarter where the dragon was and why it wasn’t flying.

Hangzhou’s mayor, Yao Gaoyuan, says the city wants to become an incubator for more tech dragons. Zhejiang screened nearly 400 candidates and selected 96 “new technology dragons”, most of them AI companies. The Runmiao fund, whose name means “nourishing seedlings”, has an investment horizon of up to 20 years.

Twenty years. I know organizations where the only thing planned twenty years ahead is the office renovation. It still isn’t finished.

## How to raise a dragon the corporate way

Allow me a short how-to. “How to Raise a Dragon the Corporate Way”, based on years of observing just about everything:

1.  Set up a Dragon Working Group.
2.  Approve a five-year dragon strategy.
3.  After six months, request a report on flight altitude.
4.  Discover the dragon isn’t flying yet. Cut its budget.
5.  Ask the dragon to prepare a slide deck explaining why it isn’t flying.
6.  Buy a ready-made dragon abroad. Discover it doesn’t comply with internal policy.

I meant to write a seventh point, but by then the dragon had left for Hangzhou.

To be fair, six dragons aren’t the whole fairy tale. They’re the shop window. Behind them are thousands of companies that never became dragons and never will, and China writes about those far less often than about the winners. Dragon statistics, like all statistics, count the survivors.

## Dragons don’t hatch on schedule

But here’s what strikes me most. Hangzhou didn’t invent its dragons. It just left them alone long enough to grow. It didn’t rush them or poke them. It gave them space, rent money and, apparently, quiet.

Dragons don’t hatch on schedule. They hatch when people stop checking on them.

Someday I’ll go back to Hangzhou, sit by West Lake and order a cup of Longjing, the “Dragon Well”. I’ll look at the water where dragons lived for a thousand years before anyone valued them in billions.

And I’ll think that the most valuable thing you can give a dragon isn’t money.

It’s time. The one thing we never seem to have, and they somehow do.
