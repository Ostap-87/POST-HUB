In Russia we once went looking for a national idea. In the summer of 1996, after the election, the president asked a group of very clever people to come up with one. The clever people thought about it. Long and hard, with tea breaks. The tea, I’d point out, was hot.

They never found the idea. The answer was sitting on the table the whole time, going cold.

China never had to look for its national idea. It just pours it. Free of charge, unlimited refills, at roughly ninety degrees Celsius.

I’m a cold-water man. Not on principle, just by habit. Ice in summer, no ice in winter, but cold either way.

In China I ask for water. I get boiling water. I ask for cold. I get warm. That’s the compromise. The waitress has made a concession for the foreigner, and she looks at me the way you look at a grown man about to walk out into January without a hat: it’s not your place to stop him, but you feel for him.

Over years of trips I have drunk, by my estimate, a tanker truck of hot water in China. The estimate is made up. The tanker truck is not.

## The calmest place on the fastest train

Let’s start with the train.

Chinese high-speed trains have hot-water boilers. Not as a perk, as a rule: according to Sixth Tone, they’re mandatory. Offices, schools, ministries and stations have the same thing: a tap, cups beside it, a queue beside the cups.

The train moves so fast that the view outside never quite settles into a landscape. Meanwhile there’s a line at the boiler. A perfectly still one. A man with a thermos, a woman with instant noodles, someone with a jar of floating leaves. The calmest place on the fastest train in the world is the queue for hot water.

Progress is when the train goes twice as fast and the water still boils at the same speed.

## Not a habit but a history

Where does it come from? I asked. People gave me different answers, but always with the same expression, as if I’d asked why one breathes.

So I read up. And it turns out hot water in China isn’t a habit. It’s a history.

Traditional medicine has split drinks into hot and cold since before the Common Era, and it was never fond of cold. By the nineteenth century, cities in the Yangtze Delta had shops selling boiled water, nicknamed “tiger stoves.” In 1862 cholera broke out in refugee-packed Shanghai, killing up to 3,000 people a day at its peak. Southerners believed hot water had saved them. Researchers later blamed the mail boats. By then, the belief had reached a full boil.

In 1934 the Nationalists launched the New Life Movement, with Chiang Kai-shek and Soong Mei-ling urging the nation to drink boiled water. The Communists in their own base areas preached the same thing and reprimanded soldiers caught drinking unboiled water. In Yan’an, Mao Zedong and Zhou Enlai were often seen with mugs of hot water. In 1952, the Patriotic Health Campaign painted it on school walls: drink boiled water three times a day.

Think about it. Two parties that spent twenty years at war disagreed on everything. Except the kettle.

Ideologies change. The temperature doesn’t.

## Drink more hot water

Speaking of which — the phrase.

China has one piece of advice for everything: duō hē rè shuǐ. “Drink more hot water.” People say it when you have a cold, when you’re tired, when you’re down. It’s been an internet meme for years.

I’ve kept a private list of situations where I’ve heard it, or could have. Incomplete, because life is longer:

Jet-lagged — ~~get some sleep~~ drink more hot water. Talks have stalled — ~~drop your price~~ drink more hot water. Fell out with a partner — ~~call him~~ drink more hot water. The yuan exchange rate — ~~don’t ask~~ drink more hot water. Don’t understand China — drink more hot water. The only item on the list that actually works.

Chinese friends told me the phrase is mocked precisely because it’s useless. And it’s said because there’s nothing else to say, and saying nothing isn’t an option. “Drink more hot water” means “I don’t know how to help, but I care.”

Cold water quenches thirst. Hot water quenches awkwardness.

## Rock cooled to a comfortable temperature

Now, the thermos.

In the summer of 2017, a photographer was shooting the veteran Chinese rock band Black Panther. The drummer, Zhao Mingyi, fifty years old, leather, legend, walked toward him holding a thermos with goji berries floating inside. The photographer couldn’t help himself: unbelievable — once a man of iron, now coming at me with a thermos.

The photo went everywhere. The goji thermos became the national symbol of middle age. Rock didn’t die. It just cooled to a comfortable drinking temperature.

Best thermos ad campaign in history, if you ask me. And nobody paid for it.

Most of those thermoses, incidentally, come from one place. Yongkang County in Zhejiang makes over 600 million cups and kettles a year. Together with its neighbors, that’s more than 70% of China’s output, and China makes about 60% of the world’s insulated cups. According to Economic Daily, the cluster also produces around 80% of the leading Western brands under contract.

So you buy an American thermos, fill it with iced coffee and head off camping. Meanwhile, the thermos has gone home. You just didn’t notice.

## The world has started drinking hot water

And now the funny part. The world has started drinking hot water.

At the end of 2025, TikTok caught the “becoming Chinese” trend: Westerners filming themselves sipping hot water, shuffling around in slippers, doing morning exercises. The glass of hot water is the badge. Exports followed: in the first quarter of 2026, according to Chinese customs, China sold about 5 billion yuan of insulated cups abroad, plus around 200 million yuan each of electric kettles and goji berries. Hot-water bottles, People’s Daily reports, have made it onto French supermarket shelves, where they used to turn up mainly in ski-resort shops.

For decades China exported thermoses. Now it exports the reason to buy one.

Meanwhile young Chinese, we’re told, increasingly drink their beverages cold. With ice. Their parents watch over the rim of a thermos.

Youth is drinking it iced. Maturity is when your kids drink it iced, and you say nothing and top up your own with hot.

What I’ve learned after years of trips and one tanker truck.

Hot water in China isn’t water. It’s a gesture. You arrive for a meeting, and before anyone talks business, a cup appears in front of you. You take a sip, and it’s refilled. Nobody asks. Nobody pauses the conversation. A hand with a kettle appears and disappears, as if it had always been there.

They don’t know you yet. They don’t trust you yet. The contract isn’t signed and may never be. But your cup is full.

A national idea is the thing nobody argues about. Nobody in China argues about hot water.

They just top you up.
