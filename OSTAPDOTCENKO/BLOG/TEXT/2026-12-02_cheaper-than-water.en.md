I’ve been asking “how much?” professionally for twenty years. Lately the question has been about tokens. A token is a scrap of text a model reads or writes — roughly a word or part of one. Companies pay per token when they plug AI into their services. And in China, token prices behaved like no price I have ever seen. First they fell. Then they fell some more.

On May 15, 2024, Tan Dai, head of ByteDance’s cloud arm Volcano Engine, walked onstage in Beijing and announced the price of Doubao’s main model: 0.0008 yuan per thousand tokens, 99.3% below the industry average. For those uneasy with zeros, he translated: one yuan buys 1.25 million tokens. About two million Chinese characters. Three copies of *Romance of the Three Kingdoms*.

Three classic novels for a yuan — roughly fourteen cents.

I wrote that line down. I’ve written ad copy for twenty years and never come up with anything that good. It has everything: price, volume, a classic and a touch of madness.

## Two weeks, seven companies

It would be neat to say ByteDance started it. But DeepSeek was first: on May 6 it released its V2 model at one yuan per million input tokens. On May 11 Zhipu cut its model’s price from five yuan to one. May 15: Doubao. On May 21 Alibaba slashed Qwen-Long by 97%, from 20 yuan per million tokens to 0.5. A few hours later Baidu made two Ernie models free. The next day Tencent made its light Hunyuan model free; so did iFlytek.

Two weeks. Seven companies. Prices down by orders of magnitude, in places to zero.

In my line of work, a 10% discount on a banner ad can take longer than that to agree. Both sides go gray.

Zhang Junlin, head of new-technology R&D at Sina Weibo, summed up the logic for Yicai: if one internet company cuts prices, the others have to follow or fall behind. That’s not economics. That’s physics. For every discount there is an equal and opposite discount.

## Thinking became cheaper than drinking

A bottle of water in a Chinese convenience store costs a couple of yuan. For the same money you could get six *Romances of the Three Kingdoms* from Doubao. You can drink the water, though. Six copies of that novel you can only survive.

Hence the phrase “AI is cheaper than water.” It sounds like a metaphor. In May 2024 it was close to an accounting fact.

For the first time in history, thinking became cheaper than drinking.

## What came of it

What came of it? I tried making a list, and as usual it came out slightly crooked.

1.  Everyone started using AI. In May 2024 Doubao processed 120 billion text tokens a day; by March 2026, 120 trillion. A thousandfold jump in under two years.
2.  Small players got squeezed. How do you sell access to your model when a giant gives its own away? Some startups, such as Kai-Fu Lee’s 01.AI, stopped training their own large models and built on others’.
3.  Everything underneath the models got pricier. According to Sinolink Securities, cited by Caixin, DDR5 memory prices rose roughly sixfold in 2025.
4.  Marketers now generate a hundred banner variants instead of one. The client still picks the first.
5.  I started saying “thank you” to chatbots. Then I learned “thank you” costs tokens too. I keep saying it. Good manners are worth the money.

## The pendulum swung back

In 2026 the pendulum swung back.

On March 11 Tencent Cloud ended free testing for several models, and prices for two Hunyuan 2.0 models rose more than fivefold. On March 18 Alibaba Cloud announced price increases of 5–34% on AI computing products from April 18, citing global demand, supply-chain costs and surging token use. Zhipu raised prices 30% in February and another 8–17% in April. In May ByteDance began testing paid Doubao subscriptions.

The only free cheese is in a mousetrap. The only free tokens last until the first GPU invoice.

Here’s the interesting part. Prices went up, and demand didn’t drop. After all its price hikes, Zhipu’s first-half 2026 revenue was nearly five times the year before, and most of it came from paid model access.

So the price war did its job. It didn’t ruin the market. It trained it.

## War always costs more than peace

War always costs more than peace. Price wars too. The bill just arrives later, and not always to the ones who started it.

I understand why it was done. In marketing we call it “lowering the barrier to entry.” You can’t sell something nobody has tried. Chinese companies let a whole country try it, at less than the price of water. Now the country keeps trying — for money.

I’ll miss that era a little. It lasted less than two years and had something of student life about it: everything cheap, everyone experimenting, nobody thinking about the bill. Such eras are lovely to remember and painful to pay for.

Three classic novels for a yuan. I confess I didn’t read a single one in that time. But I know exactly what it would have cost.
