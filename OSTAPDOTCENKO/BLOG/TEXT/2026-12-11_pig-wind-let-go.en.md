> Stand in the eye of a storm, and even a pig can fly. — Lei Jun
>
> The trick is to find out in advance what happens when the storm ends. — The pig

The business press has a genre I enjoy more than crime fiction. Call it “Why Company X Is Doomed.” There’s a template: paragraph one, a dizzying rise; paragraph two, worrying numbers; paragraph three, an analyst quote; and at the end, a meaningful “time will tell.”

In my younger days, I confess, I made similar slides for clients. The slide was titled “Competitor Risks.” The competitor usually outlived the slide, the client, and me in that job.

Time does tell. Just not always what the author expected.

## What happens when the wind stops

In 2014 Xiaomi sold more than 61 million phones in China and became number one in the world’s biggest market, ahead of Samsung and Apple. It was valued at $45 billion. Founder Lei Jun said something that anyone in China who’s ever written a business plan knows by heart: “Stand in the eye of a storm, and even a pig can fly.”

It’s an honest line with a catch. It leads to a question nobody asks while the pig is airborne: what happens when the wind stops?

In spring 2015 Lei Jun went to New Delhi to launch a new phone. At the end he announced that everyone in the audience would get a fitness band, the crowd cheered, and he asked in English: “Are you OK?” He probably meant “Are you happy?” A user on the Chinese video site Bilibili cut this and other lines into a song set to Lou Bega’s “Angelina,” and it went viral nationwide. Lei Jun replied on Weibo that he’d wanted to please his Indian fans and should probably work on his English.

I watched the clip and, I admit, laughed. But I remember it for another reason. “Are you OK?” turned out to be a very timely question. He just asked the wrong audience.

## We have demons in our hearts

For 2015, Xiaomi set a target of 80 to 100 million phones. It sold more than 70 million. For any other company, a triumph. For a company used to multiplying, its first-ever miss.

At the January 2016 annual meeting, Lei Jun said something remarkable for a CEO: “We have demons in our hearts.” The 80-million figure, he said, weighed on people so heavily that the smiles disappeared from their faces one by one. His top strategy for the new year was “just be happy,” and he promised to keep doing without KPIs.

The plan was missed, so they decided to have fun. Either very wise or very human. As it turned out, neither helped.

## Nobody had taught it to land

2016. According to IDC, Xiaomi’s sales in China fell 36% to 41.5 million. Fifth place, behind OPPO, Huawei, vivo and Apple. OPPO alone doubled its sales in a year.

There were many explanations, all correct. OPPO and vivo had wide store networks in small towns, while Xiaomi sold mostly online. Margins were so thin that store staff had no reason to recommend its phones. Quality complaints. Broken supply. An ecosystem of dozens of gadgets that, as one analyst put it, made the company “care for this and lose that.” And the Western press had already coined a nickname: unicorpse.

That’s when the wind let go of the pig. It turned out the pig flew beautifully. Nobody had taught it to land.

A short glossary. Five terms you need to understand this story. Definitions are my own.

**Wind.** Circumstances you later thank yourself for.

**Pig.** You, when the wind stops.

**KPI.** Something you abolish when the target is missed and reinstate when it’s hit.

**Buke** (补课), “making up missed lessons.” A schoolroom word. Announcing it company-wide is like a grown man sitting back down at a school desk.

**Are you OK?** The best question in management. Provided you ask it of yourself.

## Wisdom is going back to school on your own

The word buke wasn’t my idea. Lei Jun chose it himself. In early 2016 he announced three slogans for the year: “focus,” “make up lessons,” “explore boldly.” Then he did something you don’t expect from a billionaire. On May 17, 2016, he personally took over the phone business and the supply chain.

He split development into four blocks: product, R&D, supply chain, quality. He promoted young engineers. The hardware team grew from 300 to 595 people within the year. Then came offline retail: around twenty Mi Home stores in mid-2016, more than 120 by summer 2017.

I’ve seen the same picture many times at negotiations in China: the bigger the boss, the less he wants to go into details. In 2016 Lei Jun did the opposite. The biggest boss in the company went into the smallest details. Antennas, suppliers, build quality.

Humiliation is being sent back to school. Wisdom is going back on your own.

## The pig has learned to build airplanes

Q2 2017: 23.16 million phones. A company record, up 70% from Q1. Lei Jun later made a claim still quoted in China: no phone company in the world had ever turned around after its sales fell — except Xiaomi.

I won’t try to verify that. But he has a point: in an industry where a fall usually means the beginning of the end, this pig grew, if not wings, then at least a parachute.

On July 9, 2018, Xiaomi listed in Hong Kong. Less triumphantly than hoped: about $54 billion instead of the once-dreamed-of hundred. Before the IPO, the company pledged to keep its net margin on hardware at no more than 5%. Forever. A man who once missed his target now capped his own profit. That’s what I call learning from your mistakes.

What follows is no longer about restarting but about what comes after. On March 30, 2021, Lei Jun announced that Xiaomi would make cars and said: “This is the last major venture of my life.” This column has already featured one hero with a “last startup,” so I treat such words with cautious tenderness.

But here are the numbers for 2025: revenue of 457.3 billion yuan, 165.2 million phones, and a fifth straight year in the global top three. More than 411,000 cars delivered, and the car business’s first annual operating profit.

The pig the wind once dropped has learned to build airplanes.

Now and then I rewatch that clip from New Delhi. A grown man on stage, broken English, an awkward question to the audience. The whole country laughed at him. He took it without offense and promised to work on his English.

A year later, everything at his company went wrong, and it turned out that question was the most important one. Not for the audience — for himself. No crowd, no song, no fitness bands.

Are you OK?

No. But tomorrow, back to class.
