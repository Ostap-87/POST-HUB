> “Ppalli-ppalli!” (“Hurry, hurry!”) — Korea’s unofficial motto. Nobody had time to read the official one

I’ve always thought of myself as fast. In B2B marketing you have to be: while you’re thinking, the client signs with someone else. But in Korea I always feel like the guy standing on the wrong side of the escalator with a suitcase. Food arrives fast, deliveries arrive fast, and elevator doors close before you’ve decided whether to get in.

So I wasn’t surprised that Korea became the country with the most robots per capita.

What did surprise me is the math. Per capita literally means per head. Robots, technically, also have heads. But they’re not counted. Only the human ones.

## One robot for every eight workers

The numbers. In April 2026 the International Federation of Robotics published robot density figures for 2024. South Korea: 1,220 industrial robots per 10,000 manufacturing workers, first in the world. Singapore is second with 818, Germany has 449, Japan 446. The world average is 132.

In plain terms, a Korean factory has roughly one robot for every eight workers. A crew of eight people, and a ninth member who never comes to hoesik, the after-work company dinner with mandatory soju. The most sober person on the team. Management loves him. Colleagues don’t trust him.

The two biggest buyers are electronics and cars. The home of Samsung and Hyundai automated what it does best — and did it ppalli-ppalli, of course.

## An apartment and a furniture warehouse

And China? This is where it gets interesting. Based on updated employment data, China ranks only 22nd in density, with 166 robots per 10,000 workers. But it has about two million industrial robots in operation, and in 2024 it accounted for 54% of all new installations worldwide.

Korea is the densest. China is the biggest. It’s like comparing a nicely furnished apartment to a furniture warehouse. The apartment is cozier. But somehow the furniture always comes from the warehouse.

Density is many robots per person. Scale is many robots per country. The future seems to be deciding in favor of scale.

## The best advertising in robotics

Speaking of buying furniture in bulk. In 2021 Hyundai Motor Group bought a controlling stake in Boston Dynamics from SoftBank, valuing the company at about $1.1 billion. That’s the American firm whose robot dogs and dancing humanoids have racked up more views than plenty of pop bands.

As a marketer, I’ll say it plainly: for a decade, Boston Dynamics made the best advertising in robotics. Millions watched videos of engineers shoving robots that refused to fall, and almost none of those viewers would ever buy one. Hyundai essentially bought the industry’s most famous ad campaign, with an engineering company attached.

At CES in January 2026, Hyundai announced the next step: capacity for 30,000 robots a year by 2028, with the new Atlas starting work at its Georgia Metaplant in 2028, first on parts sequencing and, by 2030, on assembly.

A Korean conglomerate, an American robot, a factory in Georgia. Globalization in a single sentence.

## Into a chaebol, or inside one

Samsung took a more Korean route: rather than buy a celebrity, raise your own. Rainbow Robotics spun out of the humanoid lab at KAIST, Korea’s top technical university, in 2011. Its humanoid Hubo was Korea’s first bipedal robot, and in 2015 KAIST’s Hubo team won the DARPA Robotics Challenge, beating the Americans at their own contest.

Samsung took its time, like a cautious future mother-in-law. It bought just over 10% in 2023. On December 31, 2024, it raised its stake to 35% and became the largest shareholder. The same day it announced a Future Robotics Office reporting directly to the CEO, led by Rainbow co-founder Professor Oh Jun-ho.

In Korea, a startup grows up in one of two ways: into a chaebol, or inside one.

## The world’s most automated customer

And here it’s time to look back. Because while Korea built the densest automation on earth, China built the cheapest.

According to Korea’s AI robot industry association, cited by The Korea Times in March 2026, Chinese companies hold more than 70% of Korea’s service-robot market and 54% of its robot-vacuum market. In late January 2026 Unitree began selling at an Emart hypermarket in Seoul’s Yeongdeungpo district: the G1 humanoid for 30 million won, the Go2 robot dog for 3.99 million. Sales are still in single digits, the paper reported, but the crowds are big.

I can picture the conversation at that stand. Imagined, of course:

— What’s that? — A robot. — Whose? — Chinese. — Expensive? — Thirty million won. — And the vacuum next to it? — Also Chinese. — And the cart I’d take it home in? — The cart’s Korean. For now.

Patents tell the same story. Data from Korea’s intellectual property office, published in late September 2026, show China accounting for 60.8% of humanoid patents filed at the world’s five largest patent offices in the first half of the year. The US had 21.8%. Korea had 4.6%, and falling.

A Korean industry institute put it dryly: relations with China have shifted “from a vertical division to a horizontal competition.” China used to be the workshop and Korea the brain. Now China has a brain of its own, and it seems to think faster than the neighbors would like.

The world’s most automated country risks becoming the world’s most automated customer.

Korea responded the way it knows how: with an alliance. In April 2025 the industry ministry launched the K-Humanoid Alliance, bringing universities, research centers and companies together to build a Korean humanoid. We’ll see. I believe in Korea. A country that went from rice paddies to microchips in a single generation knows how to catch up. Catching up is its national sport.

Except that, for the first time in a long while, it isn’t the only one running.

Ppalli-ppalli. The elevator doors are already closing. The question is who’s inside, and who’s left on the landing.
