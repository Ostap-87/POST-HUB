> Old age ain’t no place for sissies. — Bette Davis. Engineers took it as a product spec

“Could you sit with him for a while?” People everywhere say it to a neighbor, a relative, a hired caregiver with kind eyes. You say it when you have to leave for work and can no longer leave someone alone.

Russian has a word for that caregiver: *sidelka*, literally “the one who sits.” It doesn’t promise to cure, feed or entertain. It promises to sit beside you. That’s a lot.

A robot *sidelka*, by the way, never sits. It has nothing to sit on. It has wheels.

## First classify old age neatly

Start with Japan, because Japan started first. As of September 2026, a record 29.6% of its population is 65 or older. Nearly one in three. The health ministry expects a shortfall of around 570,000 care workers by fiscal 2040.

Japan responded the way it responds to everything: with blueprints. Since 2012, the trade and health ministries have jointly set priority areas for care technology. Since 2015, the central government has funded prefectural subsidies for care robots. By 2025 the list covered 9 areas and 16 categories: transfers, toileting, bathing, monitoring, communication, dementia care.

By 2018 the government had spent more than $300 million on care-robot R&D alone.

Very Japanese: first classify old age neatly, then automate it.

## A small zoo in the nursing homes

Then things got interesting. Japan’s nursing homes hosted a small zoo.

There was Robear, a big white bear head with gentle eyes and arms that could lift a person out of bed. A 2015 prototype that made headlines everywhere. According to MIT Technology Review, it never worked in a care home: too expensive and impractical. The bear retired before its patients did.

There was Paro, the fluffy therapy seal with long eyelashes. Residents loved it. Some got so attached they wouldn’t give it back; others broke it. Like any pet, minus the vet.

And there was Pepper, the humanoid that led exercise sessions. Researchers noticed that to keep residents moving, staff had to copy the robot’s movements. The robot demonstrated, the staff repeated, and the residents watched the staff.

The robot came to help the staff. The staff ended up helping the robot. Interest faded within weeks, and Pepper was later discontinued.

One number explains it all. In a 2019 national survey of more than 9,000 Japanese care institutions, only about 10% had introduced any care robot at all. Monitoring and communication sensors did better: about 30% of facilities use them, according to the U.S. International Trade Administration. Toileting robots: 0.5%.

A very human statistic. We’ll let a machine watch over a person. Everything else, not yet.

## The robot gave her time to sit down

Now the part that rarely gets retold.

Economists from Stanford, the University of Tokyo and Notre Dame studied hundreds of Japanese care facilities. Where robots were adopted, employment went up, not down: about 28% more care workers and 39% more nurses. Part-time staff roughly doubled, and facilities found it easier to keep people. Freed from some of the heavy lifting, caregivers spent more time on what the researchers called the “human touch.”

The robot didn’t replace the caregiver. It gave her time to sit down.

So Japan spent twenty years building a machine that gave the *sidelka* back her actual job. Sitting.

## Chinese old age needs an assembly line

Now China. Different scale, different temperament.

By the end of 2025, according to the Ministry of Civil Affairs, China had 323.38 million people aged 60 and over, 23% of the population, up by a hundred million in a decade. That’s two and a half times Japan’s entire population. Japanese blueprints won’t cover Chinese old age. It needs an assembly line.

In May 2025, the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology and the Ministry of Civil Affairs launched a national pilot for elder-care robots. The terms were very Chinese: applicants had to be able to build at least 200 robots and test them across 20 sites, within two years. By autumn 2025, 32 projects had been selected nationwide for 2025–2027.

Two hundred robots per project. Japan’s bear was a single unit and never made it to a nursing home.

Here is what The Register reported a Chinese care robot was expected to do under the pilot. I’ve added nothing. I wouldn’t dare.

1.  Cook and clean.
2.  Help someone walk outdoors on uneven ground without falling.
3.  Lift a person when needed.
4.  Help with toileting.
5.  Feed someone who can’t feed themselves.
6.  Spot early signs of dementia and prevent wandering.
7.  Chat in several Chinese dialects.
8.  Read books and newspapers aloud and set up video calls.
9.  If necessary, stand in for a pet.

I read item nine three times. Japan built the seal separately. China wrote the seal into the spec.

China also has makers who grew out of exactly this field. Shanghai-based Fourier started in 2015 with rehabilitation robots and exoskeletons that helped patients stand and walk again. The company says its equipment is used in more than 2,000 hospitals and institutions in 40 countries and regions. In August 2025 it unveiled GR-3, which it calls its first care-focused humanoid: 165 cm tall, cushioned upholstery, 31 pressure sensors to feel touch, eyes that blink. The slogan: “Love, above all functionality.”

For now, Fourier candidly says, GR-3 is a companion and service assistant in public spaces; help with mobility and rehabilitation comes later.

## Where the two schools meet

That’s where the two schools meet. Japan said: a robot won’t replace a person, but it can free up their hands. China says: fine, then let’s build two hundred at once, teach them dialects and upholster them in car-grade leather.

I don’t know who’s right. Probably both. Japan worked out how a robot can help a caregiver. China will likely make sure that help reaches not one care home in ten but nearly all of them. That’s what “mass market” means.

But here’s what I think about, reading those lists of dialects and pressure sensors.

You can teach a machine to lift, feed, read the paper and recognize a face. The hardest part of this job isn’t lifting someone. It’s not leaving when they say, “Stay a little longer.”

Could you sit with him for a while?

Even on wheels.
