> Practice makes perfect. — Proverb. It didn’t say whose practice, or who gets to be perfect.

All my life I’ve been told that doing the same thing over and over is boring. Multiplication tables at school. The same slide for different clients in marketing. I put up with it and quietly congratulated myself on finding work where every day is a little different.

Now it turns out that repetition is the job of the future. Except you’re not repeating for yourself. You’re repeating for a robot. And not twenty times. One thousand two hundred and fifty times.

That number isn’t mine. In Beijing’s Shijingshan district sits what Chinese media call the country’s largest humanoid robot training center. It recreates factory floors, kitchens, bedrooms and parcel sorting. Each robot gets two human trainers, like a very exclusive kindergarten. Teaching one scenario takes seven or eight days. Teaching a robot to put a frying pan on a stove took 1,250 repetitions.

I have not put a frying pan on a stove 1,250 times in my life. Maybe that’s why my cooking is mediocre and the robot’s is improving. It also never asks where the oil is.

About 110 people work there, most born in the 2000s. The generation we scold for never holding anything but a phone is now holding a frying pan, 1,250 times, so nobody ever has to hold it again.

A teacher who trains a student to do the job is doing education. A teacher who trains a student to do the job instead of him is doing economics.

## Why do we need people at all

Why do we need people at all, when AI already writes poems and passes exams? The answer is dull and beautiful. Language models learned from text that humanity piled up for centuries. There’s no such archive for movement. Nobody ever uploaded how their fingers pick up a towel by the corner, with what force, at what angle. We just folded towels. Silently. With no idea we were generating valuable data.

So now it’s collected by hand. A person puts on a VR headset, sometimes an exoskeleton or motion-capture suit, performs a motion, and a robot mirrors it in real time. Every repetition is recorded, turned into a dataset, then a skill, then a line in an investor deck.

The scarcest resource, it turns out, isn’t oil or chips. It’s how your grandmother folded a bedsheet.

## They open faster than anyone counts

In Shanghai, the facility is called the Kylin Training Ground. It opened in January 2025 under the national-local humanoid robotics innovation center, with room for more than 100 humanoids training at once, a goal of 1,000 by 2027, and a target of around 10 million physical data entries by the end of 2025.

In Wuhan’s Optics Valley, a state-run center has people remotely operating nearly a hundred humanoids across fourteen scenarios: supermarket, restaurant, residential compound, factory, lab. According to the local development zone, the robots fold 300 pairs of shorts a day, iron clothes 500 times, wipe tables 500 times and pack 500 boxes of medicine.

Reading that, I can’t shake the feeling it’s not a robotics center but the perfect apartment. Everything folded, ironed, wiped. The owner is out. Presumably at a meeting on embodied intelligence.

How many of these places exist? Nobody knows exactly, because they open faster than anyone counts. Interact Analysis put the number at over forty state-owned data centers by the end of 2025, more than twenty already running. Beijing, Tianjin, Shanghai, Guangzhou, Zhengzhou, Wuxi, Jinan: the list reads like an express train timetable.

## Fine, though a bit boring

Rest of World talked to one trainer in Shanghai, a 20-year-old computer science student who spends his shifts in a headset and exoskeleton opening a microwave door, stacking blocks and folding clothes, hundreds of times. “We call ourselves cyber-laborers,” he said. A fine gig, though a bit boring.

“Fine, though a bit boring” is the most honest review of most jobs in human history. Put it over every factory gate.

The pay is nothing special either. Hong Kong’s The Standard reports job ads for data collectors offering about 20 yuan an hour, roughly 200 yuan a day. Like handing out flyers, except the flyers later go to work in your place.

A short scene. Fictional, but I suspect accurate.

“Pick up the towel by the corner.” “Done.” “Not like that. Again.” “Done.” “Again.” “Done.” “Good. Nine hundred and eighty to go.” “And the robot?” “The robot is watching.” “And then?” “Then it does it, and you watch.”

## The other ninety-five are still students

Honestly now. The method works, but slowly. UC Berkeley’s Ken Goldberg, one of the world’s leading manipulation researchers, says that even with hundreds of workers, gathering enough data will take a long time. Zhu Kai, who runs the Beijing center, says even one basic skill takes thousands of datasets. And according to The Standard, of the 23,000 humanoids China shipped in the first half of 2026, fewer than 5% can do practical work on a factory line, mainly for lack of quality real-world data.

Five percent. The other ninety-five are still students. Expensive, good-looking, eager. Students.

China is pouring money into this because whoever has the most motion data will be first to build a robot that does more than run for the cameras. Running turned out to be the easy part. A towel is soft, it has no fixed shape, and it lands differently every time. Just like people.

A robot that runs is news. A robot that folds a towel is a revolution. It’ll get less coverage, because it looks so modest.

## Something humans never wrote down

I keep thinking about those kids in headsets. They’re handing a machine something humans always knew and never wrote down. How to grip. How to press. When to let go. Quiet, homely knowledge that used to pass from mother to daughter, master to apprentice, neighbor to neighbor over the fence.

Now it passes through a headset. 1,250 times.

Somewhere in those terabytes, there’s probably a motion one of the trainers once picked up from his grandmother. The robot will learn it. She’ll never know.

Maybe that’s for the best. She’d only say: “Not like that. Again.”
