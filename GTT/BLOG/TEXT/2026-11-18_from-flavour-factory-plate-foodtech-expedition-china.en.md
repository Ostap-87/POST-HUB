Chinese cuisine is usually studied at the table. It’s pleasant but not very informative: after the eighth course, analytical ability declines, and after the baijiu it disappears.

We suggest studying it from the other side, the production side. It smells just as good, and your head stays clear.

### Why China’s food industry matters right now

Some numbers first. According to preliminary National Bureau of Statistics data, China’s food-service revenue was about 5.8 trillion yuan in 2025, up 3.2%. That is 11.6% of all retail sales in the country.

That’s modest growth by Chinese standards. And when a market grows slowly, companies start counting: cost, labour, speed, waste. That is when automation and standardisation stop being fashionable and become a matter of survival.

The second factor is regulation. In September 2025, the State Council’s Food Safety Office announced it would speed up a national standard for prepared meals, including mandatory disclosure when restaurants use them. In February 2026 the National Health Commission published a draft for consultation: prepared meals without preservatives, shelf life of no more than 12 months, and labels stating how cooked the product is and how to prepare it. Dishes from chain restaurants’ own central kitchens fall outside the definition.

Check the final text and effective date before you travel. The direction is clear, though: a market iiMedia Research valued at 485 billion yuan in 2024 is moving from “fast and plenty” to “transparent and verifiable”.

For food companies elsewhere that’s a useful preview. Many ready-meal markets are heading the same way, a few years behind.

### Stop one: the flavour factory

Sauces, seasonings, yeast, flavourings, fermented products: what the industry calls ingredients and everyone else calls taste.

The best-known example is Haitian from Foshan, China’s largest soy sauce maker. Its history goes back to a 1955 merger of 25 sauce workshops. In June 2025 it completed a Hong Kong listing that raised about HK$10.1 billion, partly to build its overseas supply chain.

What you see at a plant like this: industrial-scale fermentation, raw-material quality control, automated bottling lines, recipes adapted to different markets. The method is traditional. The volumes and controls are not.

Tradition multiplied by scale stops being a craft. It becomes technology with a good backstory.

### Stop two: prepared meals and packaging

The next level is ready and semi-prepared meals. Plants making them for retail, delivery and restaurants show the full toolkit: blast freezing, vacuum and modified-atmosphere packaging, sterilisation, labelling and batch traceability.

The interesting part is the logic. The dish is designed for the process from the start. Not “here’s our recipe, let’s freeze it”, but “here’s how the dish behaves after freezing and reheating, so this is the recipe”.

The first approach is a workaround. The second is engineering.

### Stop three: the plate

The last stage is food service. In large Chinese cities you can see it all at once: robotic kitchens, automated frying and boiling stations, delivery robots between tables, app-only ordering with no paper menu anywhere, and chains whose kitchens look more like assembly cells than kitchens.

Keep a cool head. Some of these systems run every day and pay for themselves. Some exist for photos at a grand opening. Telling them apart is easy: ask how many units the chain has, and how many are working on an ordinary Tuesday.

Where a restaurant kitchen ends and a factory begins is now a serious public debate in China, among diners as well as engineers. Expect that conversation to travel.

### What to bring home

People rarely bring back equipment from a foodtech expedition. They bring back three things.

**An understanding of standards.** How an industry handles traceability, labelling and quality control when both regulators and consumers demand it.

**Designing the product around the process.** Recipe, packaging and logistics designed together, not one after another.

**A sober view of automation.** What food service already automates at scale, what is still experimental, and what probably never should be.

### Who should go

Food and ingredient manufacturers, restaurant chains, ready-meal delivery operators, food technologists and production managers, and anyone building a central kitchen who wants to see how it works for companies with hundreds of them.

The route is built around your goals: southern China (Guangzhou, Foshan, Shenzhen) works well for ingredients and food service, the east (Shanghai and neighbouring provinces) for prepared meals and packaging. Every visit is confirmed by the host, so we start with your questions.

### How not to fail a factory tasting

1.  Don’t refuse a sample. A refusal reads as “we don’t trust you”.
2.  Don’t say “nobody eats this where I come from” before you know what it was.
3.  Don’t ask for the recipe. You won’t get it, and they will remember you asked.
4.  Praise specifically: colour, texture, packaging. Everyone says “delicious”. Only the people who get invited back say “excellent colour stability after reheating”.

### The bottom line

Food is the most conservative industry on earth. Everyone wants it to taste like grandma’s. But grandmothers don’t make a million portions a day, and factories do.

In China you can watch tradition being translated into technology while trying not to lose the taste. It doesn’t always work.

When it does, it’s worth seeing. And tasting.
