There is an old joke about telemedicine. The doctor on the video call asks the patient to open wide; the patient opens wide; and both stare at the laptop camera, unsure what happens next.

China has scaled that joke up to industrial size and, more importantly, worked out what happens next. A prescription in the app, payment in two taps, and a courier with the medicine, in a big city often the same day. You need not open wide at all: under the rules, most online visits are follow-ups anyway.

A caveat first: this is not medical advice. It is about how the “doctor in your phone” business works, what it is allowed to do, and where the money comes from. The last question turns out to be the interesting one.

### Scale: a hospital without corridors

In China, “internet hospital” is not a marketing label but a licence. Local health authorities issue it, and every online hospital must be backed by a conventional, physical medical institution with walls and a reception desk.

According to the National Health Commission, whose head Lei Haichao cited the figures in September 2025, China had 3,756 internet hospitals at the end of 2024, and about 130 million online consultations took place that year.

For a sense of scale: that is roughly as if every resident of a fairly large country saw a doctor by phone once a year. Take your pick from the world’s twenty most populous countries.

### What is allowed: rules written in earnest

The industry rests on supervision rules for internet diagnosis and treatment published by the National Health Commission and the National Administration of Traditional Chinese Medicine in summer 2022. A few provisions are worth knowing for anyone thinking about telemedicine at home.

First visits happen offline. Online care is allowed for follow-up visits for common and chronic conditions, and the patient must show documents with an existing diagnosis: a discharge summary, medical records, a diagnostic certificate. If the doctor sees that the case is new or the condition has changed, they must end the online visit and refer the patient to a physical clinic.

A doctor writes the prescription. Literally: the rules state that using artificial intelligence or other tools to generate prescriptions automatically is strictly prohibited. Doctors must pass identity checks, so that the person on the call is the registered physician.

Prescription first, medicine second. Supplying a prescription drug before the prescription is issued is banned. That sounds obvious, but it is exactly where many platforms used to cut corners, with a “consultation” reduced to a tick box next to the “buy” button.

Everything must be recorded and traceable. Under the rules online and offline visits carry equal weight, and therefore equal responsibility.

A regulator that first liked the idea and then disliked the execution writes very detailed rules.

### JD Health: a pharmacy that comes with a doctor

Now, money. The largest listed company in the sector is JD Health, a subsidiary of [JD.com](/en/companies/jd-com). Its 2025 revenue was 73.4 billion yuan, roughly a quarter more than a year earlier. Adjusted net profit rose 36% to 6.5 billion yuan. It had 218 million annual active users at year-end.

The key number is another one. Sales of medicines and health products brought in 60.9 billion yuan, about 83% of revenue.

In other words, JD Health is first of all an online pharmacy and health store. The doctor’s consultation is not the main product; it is the shop door. A patient with a chronic condition gets a repeat prescription in minutes and buys several months’ supply on the spot. The doctor covers the legal side; JD’s logistics covers everything else.

Retailers call this a loss leader. Healthcare calls it a service. Same idea, nicer name.

JD Health’s latest bet is artificial intelligence: a set of AI agents, including an “AI doctor” and an “AI pharmacist” for round-the-clock consultations, plus tools for hospitals. The rules still apply: AI may ask, triage, remind and prompt the doctor, but a human signs the prescription.

### Ping An Good Doctor: the doctor someone else pays for

The second model belongs to [Ping An](/en/companies/ping-an) Good Doctor, part of the Ping An insurance group. Its 2025 revenue was 5.47 billion yuan, up 13.7%: a fraction of JD Health’s, but structured completely differently.

About 3.3 billion yuan came from what the company calls commercial insurance enablement: its doctors and services are built into the parent group’s insurance products. Another 1.3 billion, almost a quarter of revenue, came from corporate health programmes for employers, up 40.6%, with more than 6,700 paying corporate clients. The company says it works with about 50,000 in-house and contracted doctors.

So here the patient often pays nothing for the consultation. The insurer or the employer pays, and the phone doctor becomes part of the policy or the benefits package. The earlier and cheaper the advice, the less the insurer pays later. Anyone who has ever costed a sick day understands.

The company reports that its profit multiplied in 2025 and that the cost per consultation fell about 45% year on year in the fourth quarter, which it attributes to AI.

### WeDoctor: a reminder that the model beats the idea

The third name from the first wave is WeDoctor, a platform once backed by [Tencent](/en/companies/tencent). It was among the first to build internet hospitals and among the first to learn that a good idea and a sustainable model are different things. It prepared for a Hong Kong listing for several years and kept postponing it; in 2022, according to Bloomberg, it cut staff after the IPO was delayed.

The lesson the industry took away: a doctor in your phone does not sell well on its own. What sells is what is attached to it. A pharmacy, an insurance policy, a corporate contract.

The phone doctor is not the business. It is the way into the business.

### A short scene in the app

“Hello, I have hypertension and need a repeat prescription.” “Please upload your discharge summary with the diagnosis.” “Done.” “Do you measure your blood pressure?” “Every morning.” “And?” “A bit high today. I was waiting for you to reply.” “Prescription issued. Your medicine will arrive in forty minutes.” “And my blood pressure?” “Blood pressure is an in-person matter.”

### What travels beyond China

For medical companies and investors, the Chinese experience is interesting not for its technology (video calls and chat exist everywhere) but for three things.

The rules are clear: follow-ups yes, first visits no, a human writes the prescription, everything is recorded. That is both a constraint and a protection.

The money comes not from the consultation but from what surrounds it: drug sales, insurance, corporate programmes.

Logistics decides. An online visit without fast delivery is just a phone call.

If you work in healthcare and are travelling to China, an internet hospital is worth seeing from the inside: how the online consulting room, the warehouse, the pharmacy and the physical clinic are connected. That usually impresses more than the screen itself.

A hospital without corridors still runs on a corridor. It just leads from the warehouse to your front door now.
