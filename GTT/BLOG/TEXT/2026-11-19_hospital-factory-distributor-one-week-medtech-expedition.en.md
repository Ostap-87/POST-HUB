Medical devices are an industry where one person decides on the purchase, a second pays, a third uses the device and a fourth gets the result. All four are sure they are the one who matters.

China hasn’t abolished that structure. But it has changed the rules inside it a great deal in recent years, and the best way to see that is on site, in a single trip, from three sides at once.

### Why “in one week”

Medtech is usually studied in pieces. Manufacturers go to trade shows, doctors to congresses, distributors to negotiations. Each sees their piece and draws conclusions about the whole.

A medtech expedition lets you see the whole chain in a week: where a device is made, how it is registered, who buys it, at what price, and how it is used in a hospital. Afterwards, participants usually change their view less about China than about their own market.

### Side one: the manufacturer

In fifteen years, China’s medical industry has moved from low-cost consumables to complex equipment: MRI, CT, ultrasound, monitors, endoscopes, surgical robots, laboratory diagnostics. The main clusters are the Yangtze River Delta (Shanghai, Suzhou, Hangzhou), the Pearl River Delta (Shenzhen, Guangzhou) and the Beijing area.

We have already written about some leaders, such as [Mindray](/en/companies/mindray), so here we focus on what you see on the production side in general.

First, the registration machine. According to NMPA data summarised by consultancy Cisema, China issued 14,187 medical device registrations in 2025, up 8%. Seventy-six products were approved under the innovative device pathway, bringing the cumulative total to 391 by the end of 2025.

Second, vertical integration. Large Chinese makers try to build key components in-house rather than buy them: sensors, detectors, software. It costs more at first but protects against supply shocks and gives control over cost.

Third, speed of iteration. New device versions arrive faster than the industry is used to. Registration requirements still apply, so the speed comes from how development is organised, not from lighter oversight.

### Side two: procurement and distribution

The big story of recent years is volume-based procurement. The logic is simple: the state pools demand from many hospitals and runs a tender; the winner gets guaranteed volume and in return cuts the price sharply.

The best-known case is coronary stents. In November 2020, a national tender cut the average stent price from about 13,000 yuan to 700 yuan, a 93% drop: 95% for imported products, 92% for domestic ones.

For patients it was a breakthrough. For manufacturers it meant rebuilding the business model: margin per unit collapses, and the winners are those with the right cost base, volume and logistics. For distributors it raised a hard question: what value do you add once the tender has fixed price and volume?

That question is worth asking Chinese distributors directly. The usual answer: service, training, technical support, and segments the tender didn’t cover. The price of delivering a box tends to zero. The price of making the device work does not.

### Side three: the hospital

A big-city Chinese hospital looks unfamiliar. There are many patients, yet fewer traditional queues: booking, payment, test results and follow-ups run through an app. Inside you find electronic medical records, automated labs and pharmacies, robots carrying drugs and samples, and online consultations that continue an in-person visit.

What manufacturers and distributors gain here: how a hospital chooses equipment, runs it, organises service and calculates cost of ownership. This is where you understand why a device that shines at a trade show sometimes ends up standing in a corner.

A caveat: hospital access is arranged case by case and not always to clinical areas. That’s as it should be. Medical ethics outrank a site visit.

### What the week looks like

A typical route stays within one cluster to avoid losing days to travel. For example, Shanghai with nearby Suzhou or Hangzhou (imaging, consumables, diagnostics, a hospital, a distributor), or Shenzhen and Guangzhou (monitoring, ultrasound, IVD, digital services).

The week can be timed to the CMEF trade show. Its spring edition is usually held in Shanghai and its autumn edition elsewhere; dates should be checked every year. The show gives breadth, the visits give depth. We’ll cover CMEF separately.

Global Tech Tour arranges closed visits to more than 900 companies across 17 industries, but every visit is confirmed by the host. So the route is built around your questions, not the other way round.

### Who should go

Makers of medical equipment and consumables; distributors looking for new manufacturers or rethinking their role; heads of private clinics and networks choosing equipment and IT systems; regulatory and procurement specialists.

The ideal delegation is mixed. A doctor, an engineer and a commercial director look at the same device and see three different devices. The evening discussion after a visit is often worth more than the visit itself.

### Five questions for a Chinese medtech manufacturer

1.  Which components do you make in-house, and which do you buy?
2.  How did your sales model change after volume-based procurement?
3.  Where outside China are you registered, and how long did it take?
4.  How do you train hospital staff, and who handles service?
5.  What do you do differently in markets without centralised procurement?

The fifth question sometimes opens the most candid conversation of the day.

### The bottom line

Medicine is an industry where the cost of a mistake isn’t measured in money alone. So companies enter slowly, choose carefully and change suppliers reluctantly.

China shows that even here the rules can change within a few years. A stent that cost 13,000 yuan now costs 700. More patients are getting one.

Some numbers have charts behind them. Others have people.
