Money has always wanted to disappear. Gold gave way to paper, paper to plastic, plastic to the phone. China ran that course faster than anyone: cash there is now a bit like a landline, something that exists somewhere but nobody actually uses.

Against that backdrop, China’s central bank spent years building yet another kind of invisible money, the digital yuan, or e-CNY. Any business owner will ask the obvious question: why build a new invisibility when the old one works perfectly well? Let us try to answer with numbers.

### A short history of the pilots

The People’s Bank of China began researching a digital currency in 2014, and large-scale trials started in 2020. That year Shenzhen handed out digital yuan to residents by lottery, as “red envelopes”. It was a very Chinese gesture: give people the new money first, explain what it is for later.

Years of pilots followed in dozens of cities: wallets in bank apps and a dedicated e-CNY app, payments on the metro, taxes, public-sector salaries in some places. Throughout, the digital yuan had one oddity. Legally it counted as cash, and cash earns no interest. Keeping money in an e-CNY wallet paid exactly as well as keeping it under the mattress, with the added inconvenience of not being able to sleep on it.

### 1 January 2026: cash becomes a deposit

On 1 January 2026 a new framework took effect, known in China as “digital RMB 2.0”. The key change: the digital yuan stopped being digital cash and became digital deposit money. Wallet balances now sit on the balance sheets of the operating banks as their liabilities.

The practical effects are straightforward. Balances in verified wallets of classes one, two and three earn interest at the demand deposit rate. Class four wallets, which require minimal identification, earn nothing. Interest is paid quarterly, on 20 March, 20 June, 20 September and 20 December. Balances are covered by deposit insurance on the same terms as ordinary bank deposits.

It sounds mundane, but it is a real turn. The digital yuan was conceived as a replacement for cash and has ended up as a bank account with a new interface. The aim was to invent new money; the result was a current account. Not a bad invention, just not a new one.

### What the pilots added up to

According to figures published in January 2026, by the end of 2025 the pilot regions had 230 million personal wallets and 19.08 million corporate wallets. The digital yuan had processed 3.57 billion transactions worth 19.5 trillion yuan.

That sounds enormous until you compare it with something. In 2025 alone, China’s non-bank payment institutions, essentially Alipay and [WeChat](/en/companies/wechat) Pay, processed 1.33 trillion online payments worth 337.81 trillion yuan.

So across all its pilot years, the digital yuan has handled less than six per cent of the value that private payment systems process in a single year. By transaction count the gap is wider still: private systems processed hundreds of times more payments in one year than the digital yuan has in all its years.

The hardest competitor to beat is not the best one. It is the one everyone is already used to.

### Why Alipay and WeChat Pay are holding on

For a Chinese shopper, the digital yuan adds almost nothing. QR code payments work everywhere, instantly and free of charge for the buyer. The wallet is built into the messenger, the taxi app, food delivery and ticketing. Switching needs a reason, and for everyday purchases there is hardly one.

The regulator appears to understand this and is not trying to displace private wallets by force. The digital yuan is being built into the apps people already use, while the search goes on for niches where private systems are weaker: government payments, subsidies, settlements where traceability matters and, above all, cross-border payments.

### mBridge: the most interesting part of the story

At home the digital yuan competes with Alipay. Abroad its task is more ambitious: settling payments between countries without a long chain of correspondent banks.

That is what mBridge was built for, a joint project of the central banks of mainland China, Hong Kong, Thailand and the UAE. The Bank for International Settlements, involved from the start, announced in October 2024 that it was “graduating” from the project and left the central banks to develop the platform themselves. Saudi Arabia joined as a full participant in June 2024; in September 2026 it emerged that its central bank had completed its proof of concept on 13 May 2025 and ceased to be a member.

Volumes are nonetheless growing. According to Chinese figures, from June 2024 to the end of 2025 mBridge handled 4,868 cross-border payments worth about 477.8 billion yuan, roughly 96% of that value in digital yuan. In September 2025 an international operations centre for the digital yuan opened in Shanghai, with platforms for cross-border payments and blockchain services.

Members leave; money stays. In finance that usually means a project is alive, if not to everyone’s taste.

### The digital yuan in five sentences for your board

1.  It is not a cryptocurrency; it is the yuan, issued by the central bank in digital form.
2.  Since 2026, balances in verified wallets earn interest, like a current account.
3.  Inside China it still trails Alipay and WeChat Pay by a wide margin.
4.  Abroad it is used for interbank settlement through mBridge, where volumes are rising.
5.  If anyone in the meeting says the digital yuan has already replaced the dollar, ask them to do the sums out loud.

### What it means for a foreign company

For a tourist or business traveller the digital yuan is not yet necessary: Alipay or WeChat Pay, linked to a foreign card, remain the easiest way to pay in China. The e-CNY app has offered options for foreign visitors in pilot schemes, but terms change and are worth checking before you travel.

For a company that trades with China, the bigger story is elsewhere. Cross-border settlement in digital yuan through mBridge and the Shanghai centre is infrastructure China is building for the long term. Today it operates mostly between banks and in a limited set of jurisdictions. It is worth watching if you settle in yuan and your partner banks take part in such projects. Anything involving settlement, sanctions risk or currency controls should, of course, be checked with your lawyers and your bank.

Global Tech Tour runs business expeditions to China where you see finance and payments not on slides but in how people actually pay in shops, factories and restaurants. Five minutes by a till usually tells you most of it.

### The bottom line

The digital yuan began as the money of the future and has become an interest-bearing deposit. That may look like a disappointment. Or it may look like growing up: a project that spent years as a beautiful idea has finally found a dull and useful job.

The most trustworthy money is the kind you never think about. The digital yuan still makes people think about it. Which may be the best measure of how far it still has to grow.
