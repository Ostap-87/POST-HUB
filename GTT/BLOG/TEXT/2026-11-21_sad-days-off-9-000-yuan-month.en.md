There is a supermarket in China that some shoppers drive 400 kilometres to visit. Not for a shortage item, not for a sale. Just to look.

That alone is odd. A supermarket is the last place anyone goes voluntarily. You go with a trolley and a list, and you come back without the list and with three extra bags. Yet in Xuchang, a provincial city in Henan, people queue outside a grocery store the way people elsewhere queue outside museums.

The chain is called Pangdonglai (胖东来). Below are the numbers, the practices, and an honest answer to the question every executive asks after first reading about it: could we do this?

### The basics: a small chain with big revenue

Yu Donglai founded Pangdonglai in 1995. It operates in just two cities in Henan, Xuchang and Xinxiang, and according to China Daily had 13 stores and about 8,300 employees in early 2025.

Revenue in 2025 reached 23.53 billion yuan, up 38.7% from about 17 billion the year before. Supermarkets brought in 12.64 billion, jewellery 2.45 billion and tea 1.06 billion.

For comparison, [Yonghui](/en/companies/yonghui), with stores across the country, reported 67.6 billion yuan in revenue for 2024 and a net loss of almost 1.5 billion. Thirteen stores versus several hundred, and it is not obvious who should envy whom.

According to Xuchang city authorities, during the 2025 Spring Festival Pangdonglai took in about 100 million yuan in a single day, as much as a famous Henan tourist attraction earned over the entire eight-day holiday. The supermarket beat the landmark. The landmark can console itself: it has been standing there a great deal longer.

### Lesson one: wages are part of the service

Average take-home pay at Pangdonglai is about 9,000 yuan a month (June 2025 figures), very high for provincial retail. The working day is seven hours. Annual paid leave, according to China Daily, is 30 days for front-line staff and 40 for managers.

Then there is the line that went viral: since 2024, employees can take up to 10 days of “unhappy leave”, and managers cannot refuse it. It sounds like a joke from the office group chat. It works as HR policy.

In March 2026 the company announced a profit-sharing scheme under which about half of annual profit goes to employees and management. The founder says he keeps around 5% himself.

The logic is simple, if unfamiliar. Customers do not see company policy. They see the face of the person serving them. A well-treated employee is not a staff cost. It is the shop window.

### Lesson two: protect your people

In November 2025 Pangdonglai published compensation standards for employees whose dignity is violated by customers: 5,000 yuan or more for a false accusation, 10,000 or more for verbal abuse, 30,000 or more for being assaulted on duty. Between January and October 2025 it recorded 33 such cases and paid out 359,000 yuan.

Service businesses usually follow one rule: the customer is always right. Pangdonglai added a footnote: as long as the customer behaves like a decent human being.

It works in the other direction too. In 2024, when a supplier was found to have broken food-safety rules, the chain paid customers 8.8 million yuan in compensation. Trust is built both ways: the company pays those mistreated in the store and those let down by it.

### Lesson three: the small things people notice

The practices reporters keep listing read like a picky shopper’s checklist. No slotting fees for suppliers. No promotions and no membership schemes. Bulk foods labelled with roughly how many pieces make up 500 grams. Toilets with a 3:8 ratio of men’s to women’s stalls, because the queue for the ladies’ room is part of the customer experience too, it just rarely comes up in board meetings.

None of this needs AI, robots or big capital spending. It needs one thing: someone in the company who has actually stood in that queue.

### Lesson four: the model travels, but not in one piece

In May 2024 Yonghui began remodelling stores along Pangdonglai lines, with Yu Donglai personally involved. The first rebuilt store, in Zhengzhou, reopened on 19 June 2024 and drew almost 13,000 shoppers on day one, about five times the usual number. By the end of March 2025 Yonghui had upgraded 47 stores, and those open for more than three months after remodelling were profitable in the first quarter.

Yonghui’s overall picture is more mixed, though: revenue fell, partly because it closed 273 weak stores, and gross margin dipped during the transition. You can repaint the sign and move the shelves in three months. Rebuilding the relationship between company, employee and customer takes longer.

Pangdonglai’s model can be copied in parts. As a whole it only travels together with its founder, and, unfortunately for everyone else, there is only one of him.

### What you could try tomorrow (and what you could not)

1.  Compare what your shop-floor staff earn with what you spend on advertising to bring customers to them.
2.  Write down how your company protects staff from abusive customers. Not a slogan: rules, with amounts.
3.  Walk your own store with a stopwatch and time the queue for the toilets.
4.  Drop one “three for two” promotion and see what happens.
5.  Introduce “unhappy leave”. This one is for the brave. Everyone else can start by looking less unhappy in meetings.

### Why go and see it

You can read about Pangdonglai at home. But what makes it special hardly shows up in reports: how staff talk to shoppers, how goods are laid out, what the price tags say, what the back room looks like. Those things you learn on foot.

Global Tech Tour organises business trips to China, including bespoke programmes built around a company’s goals, and retail is one of the industries where seeing it for yourself teaches the most. If you run a retail chain, a shopping centre or any business where customers meet staff face to face, Xuchang is worth the detour, even if your route was heading somewhere else entirely.

### The bottom line

Pangdonglai has not invented anything you would not find in a customer-service textbook. Pay people properly. Protect them. Do not cheat customers. Sweat the details.

The difference is that most people read the textbook. In Xuchang, somebody seems to have decided to follow it.
