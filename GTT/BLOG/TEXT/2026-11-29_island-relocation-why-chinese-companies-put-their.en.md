Singapore has no giant factories, no raw materials and very little land. What it does have, and what Chinese companies queue up for, is an address.

In recent years a Singapore address has become, for Chinese business, something like a good suit at a negotiation. It doesn’t change who is inside. It changes a great deal about how you are received.

Let’s look at what companies buy when they move to the island, and what can’t be bought.

### What a Singapore address gives you

**Tax and law.** The headline corporate tax rate is 17%, and since 2025 large multinational groups face a 15% minimum tax under OECD rules. The rate is only part of it. Singapore offers English-based law, predictable courts, international arbitration and a wide network of double tax treaties.

**Capital.** International funds are more comfortable investing in a Singapore entity than a mainland one, especially when US–China relations make the news every day.

**Markets.** Singapore is a convenient base for Southeast Asia, a region of more than 600 million people, and it is the region’s financial and legal capital.

**Perception.** To a European or American client, “a Singapore company” sounds different from “a Hangzhou company”. Unfair, but effective.

### The numbers behind it

Take the Vietnamese data from our China-plus-one article. In 2025 Singapore was Vietnam’s largest source of newly registered investment capital: $4.84 billion, almost 28%. China was second with $3.64 billion. Part of that “Singapore” money comes from Chinese groups running regional business through the island. Nobody publishes the exact share, which is telling in itself.

Then there are family offices. According to the Monetary Authority of Singapore, more than 2,000 single-family offices were receiving tax incentives at the end of 2025, The regulator does not break the figure down by country, but industry observers estimate that a significant share is linked to Chinese wealth.

Singapore doesn’t make goods for China. It makes trust for China, and trust is a high-margin product.

### 2023: the island starts checking its guests

In August 2023 Singapore police ran the largest anti-money-laundering operation in the country’s history. Ten foreigners from Fujian province, holding various passports, were arrested. Assets seized or frozen eventually reached about S$3 billion: villas, cars, bank accounts, gold. All ten were convicted. In July 2025 the regulator fined nine financial institutions a combined S$27.45 million for related breaches.

Banks and regulators have since tightened source-of-funds checks considerably. Opening accounts and obtaining approvals takes longer. From 2025 the Employment Pass salary floor is S$5,600 a month (S$6,200 in financial services), plus a points-based assessment.

Singapore still welcomes guests. It now looks not just at the suitcase but at where the money in it came from.

### Manus: an address is not an origin

The most instructive story of the past year is Manus, an AI-agent start-up. Founded in China in 2022, it raised $75 million led by US fund Benchmark in early 2025, then moved its headquarters to Singapore. In December 2025 Meta agreed to buy Manus, reportedly for more than $2 billion.

Then Beijing stepped in. China’s Ministry of Commerce reviewed the deal, and in late April 2026, according to media reports, the National Development and Reform Commission ordered it unwound. The regulators’ logic: the company, its team and its technology originated in China, so moving abroad does not take it outside Chinese rules.

The lesson travels well. A Singapore address helps you raise money and win clients. It does not change where the technology and the people came from. Origin does not change with the postcode.

### Three kinds of Chinese companies in Singapore

1.  **The real regional HQ.** Runs the Southeast Asia business, hires locally, takes decisions. The healthiest kind.
2.  **The holding layer.** A legal entity for money and contracts, while real life happens in Shenzhen. Works until someone asks questions.
3.  **The signboard for foreigners.** An English website and a desk in a business centre shared with a hundred others. Lasts until the first serious contract.

If you work with a Chinese partner, it pays to know which kind its Singapore company is. It determines who actually signs the contract and where any dispute will be heard.

### What it means for partners

A Chinese partner’s Singapore entity is no longer exotic. It may show up in your contract, in payment details or in the supply chain. That is no reason for suspicion, but it is a reason to ask: where is production, who are the owners, which law applies, and which banks handle payments.

One more observation. A move to Singapore often signals that a company is building its future on international markets, not just China. That tells you something about its strategy, its pricing and how much attention it will pay to smaller clients.

### The bottom line

Chinese companies go to Singapore for what they lack at home: neutrality, law and capital. The island sells all three honestly and checks its buyers ever more carefully.

Some things, though, can’t be shipped: the accent, the pedigree, and a regulator who remembers where you were born.

You can change your address in a month. Your origin, never.
