Crane operator used to be the most glamorous job on any quay. You sat in a cab about sixty metres up, looked down at a container ship, and with a flick of a joystick dropped forty tonnes of steel exactly into its slot. People on the ground respected you. Up there you were alone with a flask of tea and the wind.

At Yangshan the crane operator has come down. Not to the quay, but to an office. The wind has been replaced by air conditioning. The flask, we are told, survived.

That is the short version of China’s port automation story. Here is the long one, with numbers.

### The world’s busiest port, sixteen years running

Start with scale. In 2025 the Port of Shanghai handled more than 55 million TEU and stayed the world’s busiest container port for the sixteenth year in a row. Yangshan deep-water port accounted for 28.71 million TEU of that, or 52.2%, up from 50.5% a year earlier. In other words, most of Shanghai’s boxes no longer pass through the Yangtze estuary at all. They go through a group of offshore islands linked to the mainland by the Donghai Bridge.

Yangshan was built in phases. Phase IV, the subject of this piece, began trial operations on 10 December 2017. Reports at the time put its cost at about $2.15 billion and its design capacity at up to 6.3 million TEU a year.

In 2025 it handled more than 8 million TEU, beating its design capacity by roughly a quarter. Fewer people, more boxes. Management consultants have built careers on less.

### What’s inside: seven berths, sixty-one unmanned yard blocks

According to Chinese science press, the terminal has 2,350 metres of quay, 7 deep-water berths and 61 automated yard blocks. At launch the plan called for 26 quay cranes, 120 rail-mounted gantry cranes and 130 automated guided vehicles (AGVs). All of the equipment came from Shanghai-based ZPMC, and the terminal operating system was developed in China too.

The flow works like this. A quay crane lifts a box off the ship, guided by an operator sitting in a control building. The box goes onto a driverless AGV, which carries it to the right yard block, where an automated gantry crane stacks it. When a truck comes to collect, the process runs in reverse. Humans step in where automation is still unsure of itself: picking boxes off the vessel, odd-sized cargo, faults.

The AGVs talk to the system over a wireless network. At launch, a private LTE network was built for the job: latency had to stay under 50 milliseconds, with up to 60 vehicles per berth online at the same time. It sounds dull until you picture sixty driverless trucks being told to stop at once.

A line for your next board meeting: automation is sixty machines obeying one signal. A crisis is that signal arriving a second late.

### What it delivered

Chinese sources cite two effects. Seaside productivity and overall yard capacity rose by about 30% compared with a conventional terminal. Labour costs fell by 70%.

The second number gets the headlines, but the first matters more. A port that works a third faster can handle more ships at the same berths. Building a new berth in open sea takes years. Speeding up an existing one is expensive too, but at least nobody has to build an island.

There is also a number for romantics. Some reports say operators can run a crane from more than 30 kilometres away; others mention around 100. The sources disagree, so let’s put it carefully: a crane operator no longer has to live by the sea. A good network connection will do.

### Where people are still needed

This is the part that matters if you are thinking about automating your own warehouse or plant.

**Operators haven’t vanished; they’ve moved.** Remote quay-crane control is still a person with a joystick, just handling more moves per shift and not sixty metres in the air. Automation took over the yard and the internal transport, the most repetitive work.

**Maintenance.** A driverless AGV never takes a lunch break, but it does need scheduled parts. The number of mechanics, electronics technicians and software engineers does not shrink. The mix changes: fewer people moving cargo, more people making sure cargo moves by itself.

**Exceptions.** Damaged containers, out-of-gauge cargo, bad data, bad weather. Automated systems are brilliant at what they have done a thousand times and clumsy at what they see for the first time. Rather like us, except we at least know how to be surprised.

**Hand-offs.** Boxes arrive by truck over the bridge or leave by barge up the river. Yangshan is testing autonomous trucks: by 2025, 89 vehicles in the smart-truck project had moved over 750,000 TEU and logged more than 18 million kilometres. But most of a container’s journey still depends on people outside the gate.

### Five questions to ask at any automated site

1.  Which operations were automated first, and why those?
2.  What happens when the system is “not sure”? Who decides, and how fast?
3.  How many people work here now, and what did they do before?
4.  How long did it take from launch to design throughput?
5.  What does an hour of downtime cost? People usually ask this last. They shouldn’t.

### What this means beyond the port

Yangshan Phase IV is not unique. Chinese science press reports that the country has already built 60 automated terminals, so this is an industry norm, not a showpiece. Shanghai also plans to develop the north side of Xiaoyangshan island as an automated terminal.

Three lessons travel well to any large warehouse or factory.

Automate the flow first, then the jobs. The port did not become “lights-out” overnight: first the yard and internal transport, then remote crane control, then driverless trucks beyond the gate.

A single equipment ecosystem simplifies life. When cranes, vehicles and software come from one family of developers, it is easier to agree whose fault a failure is. Not everyone needs to do it that way, but “who owns the interface?” is a question to ask before you buy, not after.

Connectivity is infrastructure, just like concrete. A berth can be built in three years. A network that holds 50-millisecond latency, by its supplier’s account, took nearly a year of verification.

At Global Tech Tour we arrange closed [visits](/en/expeditions) to Chinese companies, and we always tell participants to come with questions rather than for the view. An automated port is where the view deceives you most. From above, it looks empty and quiet. Empty, yes. Quiet only because the work has gone into the cables.

You used to be able to see the crane operator from anywhere on the quay. Now you can’t see him at all. The port is none the worse for it. The romance has simply been moved indoors, where, like everything in an office, it runs on a schedule.
