There are two ways to learn from the kid at the next desk. You can copy his homework. Or you can ask him to sit beside you and explain it, and then, once he has gone, solve the problem better than he did.

Suzhou chose the second option. Along the way, the kid at the next desk got a little annoyed.

### A signed textbook

On 26 February 1994, Lee Kuan Yew and Chinese Vice-Premier Li Lanqing signed a government-to-government agreement to develop an industrial zone in Suzhou together. A commercial agreement setting up the development joint venture was signed the same day: 65% to a Singaporean consortium, 35% to the Chinese side.

The most interesting part was not the land or the money. It was something called “software transfer”. This did not mean computer programs. It meant the way Singapore runs industrial development and a city: planning, investor relations, the civil service, the rules. According to Singapore’s National Library Board, around 2,300 officials from Suzhou and the park had been trained in the Singaporean approach by 2014.

China was not buying land. It was buying the manual.

### Seven difficult years

The manual was good; the classroom was restless. Back in 1990 the Suzhou government had set up its own zone, the 52-square-kilometre Suzhou New District. By 1997 it was competing hard with the joint park, and local officials, the same source notes, promoted their own project more actively. Then the 1997 Asian financial crisis hit investment flows.

The first seven years produced cumulative losses of 77 million US dollars.

In 1999 the partners signed a memorandum cutting Singapore’s stake to 35% and raising China’s to 65%. On 1 January 2001, control and management passed to the Chinese side.

This is where the story is usually filed under “failure”. Yet in 2001 the park made its first profit, by 2003 it had recovered its accumulated losses, and in 2004 it paid its first dividend. Singapore stayed on as a shareholder and partner. The teacher moved from the front of the room to the back row, but never left.

A model can be transferred. Ownership of it cannot.

### What was kept, and what was added

Look at the park today and you can see which pages of the Singapore manual survived and which were written in later.

The “infrastructure first, factories second” approach survived: roads, utilities, housing and services built before the investor arrives, so the investor does not have to wait. So did the habit of treating a foreign company as a client rather than a supplicant. According to China Briefing, the park attracted more than 5,000 foreign-invested projects from 70 countries and regions over two decades, including 156 projects by 92 Fortune Global 500 companies.

Then came the original chapters. In 2006 the park picked biomedicine as one of three emerging industries. In late 2007 the first eight companies moved into BioBAY, its dedicated biotech campus. Today, Xinhua reports, BioBAY hosts more than 680 biomedical companies with over 72 approved drugs on the market. In the first half of 2026 alone, BioBAY companies signed nine out-licensing deals with foreign partners, with upfront payments of 2.1 billion dollars and a total deal value of 27.2 billion.

Yesterday’s student is now selling patents to the people it used to buy equipment from. Schools have a phrase for this: outgrowing the syllabus.

Nanotechnology followed the same path. Corporate-level work began in 2006, and Nano City, about one square kilometre, opened in 2013. By the end of 2023 it housed over 550 companies, and People’s Daily put the park’s nanotech output at more than 155 billion yuan.

### Numbers that don’t follow the textbook

The park’s GDP was about 291 billion yuan in 2020 and 400.2 billion in 2024, according to The Paper. Foreign trade in 2024 reached 691 billion yuan. China’s Ministry of Commerce ranks national economic and technological development zones every year, and Suzhou has kept the top spot: by 2020 it had been first among 218 zones for five years running.

Growth has not been a straight line, though: 10% in 2021, just 2.3% in 2022. The country’s best park still lives in the same country, with the same cycles.

### Five rules of borrowing, Suzhou style

1.  Buy the decision-making method, not the equipment.
2.  Train people rather than translate regulations. Regulations go out of date; people can be retrained.
3.  Don’t worry if the teacher gets annoyed. It means the lesson worked.
4.  Add a subject the teacher never taught.
5.  Thirty years later, politely invite the teacher on a site tour.

### A lesson for anyone borrowing a model

Many companies now look at Chinese practice the way Suzhou looked at Singapore in 1994: why reinvent what already works? Fair enough. The only mistake is assuming the copy will run on its own.

Suzhou suggests three things. What transfers is not the scheme but the people who understand it; without those 2,300 trained officials, no rulebook would have worked. A borrowed model almost always goes through a crisis when local conditions start arguing with imported rules; the early losses were the price of adaptation, not proof of failure. And a model becomes yours when you add something its author never had. Singapore did not have a 680-company biotech cluster on former rice paddies.

On Global Tech Tour business [expeditions](/en/expeditions), participants often ask: “Could we do this back home?” The honest answer is usually: you can’t replicate it, but you can adopt it. The difference is roughly that between a photocopy and a textbook with your own notes in the margins.

Suzhou’s textbook is covered in notes by now. The Singaporean handwriting is still there, if you know where to look.
