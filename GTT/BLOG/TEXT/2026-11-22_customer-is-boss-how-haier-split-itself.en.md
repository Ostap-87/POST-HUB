Every large company has someone who knows exactly what the customer wants. Usually it is a department head who last met a customer at the 2019 Christmas party.

[Haier](/en/companies/haier) decided this was the wrong arrangement and removed almost everyone standing between employees and customers. The result is one of the most discussed management models in the world, with a hard-to-pronounce name and a simple idea.

This is not an article about Haier’s fridges and washing machines. It is about how the company itself is built.

### It started with a sledgehammer

Haier’s story usually opens with one scene. In 1984 Zhang Ruimin took over a loss-making refrigerator factory in Qingdao. In 1985 he handed workers sledgehammers, and they smashed 76 defective refrigerators. In a country where a fridge was a major purchase, it looked like vandalism. Zhang meant it as a lesson in quality.

Decades later that factory had become the world’s largest home appliance maker. According to Euromonitor, Haier has been the world’s number one major appliance brand by volume for more than fifteen years running.

The interesting part comes next. A company that began by smashing bad products eventually set about smashing its own org chart.

### What RenDanHeYi means

In 2005 Zhang set out a model he called 人单合一, RenDanHeYi. “Ren” is the employee, “dan” is the order, or more broadly value for the user, and “heyi” means unity. Zhang loosely translated it as “employees and customers become one”.

In practice, instead of departments and a chain of command, the company runs on thousands of small self-managing teams called microenterprises. Zhang told McKinsey in 2021 that there were about 4,000. Each is responsible for a product, a market or a group of users, recruits its own people and shares its own earnings.

Microenterprises group into roughly 300 “ecosystem microcommunities”. Some handle R&D and manufacturing; others deal directly with users and marketing.

The key formula from the same interview: employees are paid not by the company but by the users. A team that creates value for users shares the result. A team that does not has nothing to share.

It sounds like a CFO’s dream. It is also a CFO’s nightmare, because now there are 4,000 profit-and-loss accounts to keep.

### Where middle management went

The most quoted number: Haier eliminated more than 12,000 middle-management positions. HR, Zhang said, shrank from 860 people to 11 after the company moved to a shared services platform.

It is worth being precise about what disappeared. Not people as such: many went on to lead or join microenterprises. What disappeared was the function of passing instructions down and reports up. Haier replaced it with platforms, shared services and direct contact between teams and the market.

Nobody loves middle management. But you can only really get rid of it by giving its work to someone else. The work itself does not go away.

### Testing it on someone else: GE Appliances

A good theory should work away from home. In 2016 Haier bought GE Appliances from General Electric for $5.4 billion and began introducing its model there.

GE was once the company that set management fashions for everyone else. In 2019 Bloomberg summed up the change as Six Sigma giving way to RenDanHeYi. GE Appliances started with seven microenterprises, one of them focused on water heaters. According to INSEAD, in 2017 GE Appliances had its best performance in a decade, with double-digit profit growth, and its CEO Kevin Nolan described it as “a two-year-old start-up”.

For half a century American management taught the world. Then a Chinese teacher arrived and handed out sledgehammers. Figuratively speaking.

### What the model does not solve

In fairness, RenDanHeYi has limits worth remembering before you put the name on your meeting-room door.

It needs very transparent accounting: if you cannot see what value a team created, you cannot pay it “from the user”. It needs people willing to take entrepreneurial risk, and not everyone is. And it leaned heavily on its founder: Zhang spent more than fifteen years building it and stepped down as chairman in 2021.

Finally, Haier is not only microenterprises. It also runs very large, very disciplined plants: in 2025 two of its factories, a washing machine plant in Shanghai and a refrigerator plant in Chongqing, joined the World Economic Forum’s Global Lighthouse Network. Team autonomy does not replace standards on the line. It lives alongside them.

### Five questions to ask at Haier (or yourself)

1.  Who in your company is closest to the customer, and how many sign-offs separate that person from a decision?
2.  Can you measure the value a specific team created, rather than a whole department?
3.  What happens if a team that delivered nothing gets no bonus? Is that fair, or is it a mutiny?
4.  What does your middle management actually do, and who will do it if they are gone?
5.  Are you ready to smash 76 refrigerators? Rhetorical question. Sell them for parts.

### Why see it rather than read about it

Business schools have written dozens of case studies on RenDanHeYi. The trouble with case studies is that they describe the model as designed. What is more interesting is how it looks on an ordinary Tuesday: how a microenterprise reports, how it argues with a platform, how it splits the money.

Global Tech Tour arranges closed visits to Chinese companies and corporate learning programmes for teams that want applicable conclusions rather than impressions. Management models are exactly where an hour with a real microenterprise leader is worth more than ten case studies, provided you bring the right questions.

### The bottom line

Large companies rarely die of competitors. They die of the distance between the person making a decision and the person it is made for.

Haier tried to shrink that distance to zero. It did not get there perfectly. Compared with most, it got remarkably close.
