In any meeting with Chinese partners there is one person who matters more than the CEO. They make no decisions, sign nothing and usually sit slightly behind everyone else. It is the interpreter.

Every word you say goes through them. If it comes out wrong, the blame is yours, not theirs: your partner heard what they heard, and correcting the record in China is far more expensive than getting it right the first time.

An interpreter is not a microphone. A microphone does not get tired, mix up tonnes and yuan, or quietly soften your bluntness to keep the room pleasant.

On the business trips Global Tech Tour organises, we keep seeing the same pattern: about a third of interpreting quality depends on the interpreter and two thirds on how you work with them. (The ratio is our own estimate, but nobody has yet offered to argue with it.) Here is what you can do on your side.

### 1. Brief them: the interpreter should know where you are going

The most common mistake is meeting the interpreter at the factory gate and saying “Shall we?”. The interpreter then discovers that today’s topic is die casting at roughly the same moment you arrive. The difference is that you know what die casting is.

A few days before the visit, send the interpreter the purpose of the meeting and what you want from it, a list of participants on both sides with their roles, your questions for the plant, and a glossary of 20 to 30 key terms from your industry with explanations, plus Chinese equivalents if you have them.

The glossary is the most underrated document of any business trip. It is one page long and saves hours. Technical interpreting without one is like assembling furniture without instructions: something will stand up, but there will be parts left over.

### 2. Pace: speak in sentences, not chapters

Professional interpreting guidelines agree on a few simple rules. Speak in short segments of one or two sentences and pause. Avoid idioms, slang and elaborate comparisons. Address the other person, not the interpreter: look at the person you are speaking to and use the first person. Not “tell him we agree”, but “we agree”.

It sounds minor, but it changes the room. When you look at the interpreter, your counterpart becomes a spectator to someone else’s conversation. When you look at your counterpart, they become part of yours.

And do not tell jokes through an interpreter. Almost any joke, interpreted into Chinese, loses either its meaning or its audience. The best you can hope for is a polite smile that means “the interpreter indicated this was the place to smile”.

### 3. Time: the meeting takes twice as long

With consecutive interpreting, a meeting takes roughly twice as long as a conversation in one language. That is not the interpreter’s fault. It is arithmetic: everything is said twice.

So if you have an hour, you really have thirty minutes. Plan accordingly: five important questions beat fifteen random ones. That is why, for our [expedition](/en/expeditions) programmes, we agree in advance with the host which questions the delegation wants to cover. Time on the shop floor does not stretch, and interpreting does not stretch it.

### 4. Numbers: where the zeros go missing

Numbers are a risk zone of their own. Chinese counts large numbers in units of ten thousand rather than thousands: 万 (wan) is 10,000 and 亿 (yi) is 100 million. One million is “a hundred wan”, one billion is “ten yi”.

An experienced interpreter converts automatically. But at the end of a long day, after the third workshop and a big lunch, even a good one can drop a zero. And a dropped zero in a price or order quantity is not a typo. It is a different deal.

The rule is simple: write down every key figure, including price, volume, lead time and tolerances, on paper, in the chat or on a whiteboard. A number written by hand needs no interpreting.

### 5. What the interpreter should not decide for you

A good interpreter conveys everything that is said, adding, omitting and changing nothing, and offers no advice or opinions while interpreting. In practice this rule is sometimes broken with the best intentions: a blunt sentence is softened, an awkward question disappears, or the interpreter answers a question themselves because “it was obvious”.

Agree in advance: interpret everything, including the uncomfortable parts. If the interpreter thinks a phrasing may offend, they should tell you, not quietly fix it. What gets said to your partner is your decision.

Then there is the question of whose interpreter it is. If the host provides interpreting, the interpreter works for the host. That does not mean they will distort anything, but it does mean they will report back afterwards. For an introductory visit, that is fine. For price negotiations, bring your own.

### 6. After the meeting: ten minutes worth an hour

Right after the meeting, ask the interpreter for ten minutes. They heard not only the words but also the tone, the side remarks between Chinese participants that nobody translated, and the pauses that meant something. Ask: “What did they mean when they said the question was complicated?” Sometimes that answer is worth more than the whole meeting.

### Six things your interpreter would rather not hear

1.  “Just translate it somehow.”
2.  “It’s an untranslatable pun, but make it funny.”
3.  “That was fifteen minutes. Give them the short version.”
4.  “Tell him he’s wrong. Politely. But so he gets it.”
5.  “Don’t translate this”, said in front of a counterpart who understands a bit of your language.
6.  “You’re an interpreter, surely you know what our internal acronym means.”

### The bottom line

The interpreter is the only person in the room both sides hear equally well. Work with them, and they become your voice. Work against them, and they are just someone else’s echo.

Prepare your interpreter the way you prepare your own delegation. They are part of it. They simply speak for everyone.
