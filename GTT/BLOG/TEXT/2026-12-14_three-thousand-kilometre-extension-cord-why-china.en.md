When a flat runs out of sockets, you buy an extension cord. When a country runs out of sockets, the extension cord is three thousand kilometres long and the plug weighs as much as a freight train.

China has been working on this problem for twenty years. The answer is called UHV, ultra-high voltage. It sounds like the physics chapter everyone skipped. In fact it is one of the main reasons a factory in China can be built fast without waiting years for power to reach it.

### The problem: wind in the west, factories in the east

China’s energy map is awkward. Coal, wind, sun and big rivers are mostly in the west and north: Xinjiang, Inner Mongolia, Gansu, Qinghai, Sichuan, Yunnan. People, factories and data centres are mostly in the east and south, along the coast. Thousands of kilometres separate where power is made from where it is used.

Conventional lines lose too much energy over that distance. The higher the voltage, the lower the losses. Hence the idea: push voltage to levels rarely used commercially before, and send power across half a continent.

Grid losses are like taxes. You can’t avoid them, but you can make sure something is left when the bill arrives.

### How many kilometres

By the end of 2025, China had completed 46 UHV projects: 22 AC and 24 DC lines, with a combined length of over 62,000 km. That is roughly one and a half times around the equator, and almost none of it is visible from a plane unless you know where to look.

The flagship is Changji–Guquan, running from Xinjiang to Xuancheng in Anhui province through Gansu, Ningxia, Shaanxi and Henan. It operates at ±1,100 kV DC, carries 12 GW and is about 3,300 km long: roughly London to Moscow and a good stretch beyond, or New York to Salt Lake City. It was approved in December 2015, construction started in January 2016, and it came online around the turn of 2019. Its reported cost was about 40.7 billion yuan, and its operator said it could meet the demand of 50 million households.

China officially runs on a single time zone, so at least the electrons don’t get jet lag.

### Who pays, and how much

The main player is State Grid Corporation of China, the world’s largest grid company, alongside China Southern Power Grid in the south. In 2025 State Grid announced a record investment of more than 650 billion yuan for the year.

In January 2026 it unveiled a plan to invest 4 trillion yuan in fixed assets over the 15th Five-Year Plan (2026–2030), about 40% more than in the previous five years, or roughly 800 billion yuan a year. Goldman Sachs analysts expected UHV to be the fastest-growing grid segment in 2026.

A December 2025 policy document from the NDRC and the National Energy Administration set 2030 targets: more than 420 GW of west-to-east transmission capacity and over 30% growth in inter-regional transmission compared with the end of the previous plan.

Trillions always sound like the budget of a small planet. The logic is simple, though: the grid is being built for renewables in deserts and plateaus, and for customers who have no intention of moving there.

### What it changes for factories and data centres

**Power as an investment condition.** In China, a new industrial site usually comes with an answer to “how many megawatts, and when?” Long-distance lines let local governments promise capacity where local generation is thin. For foreign partners, it is one reason Chinese factory projects move faster than they expect: electricity stops being the bottleneck.

**Moving demand west.** You can bring energy to the customer, or the customer to the energy. The “East Data, West Computing” programme, launched in 2022, places part of the country’s computing capacity in western regions with cheap power and cool climates. UHV and data centres follow one logic: data travels by fibre, electricity by UHV, and the question is which is cheaper to ship.

**Renewables need wires.** A desert solar farm without a line east mostly produces reports. That is why Chinese plans almost always pair wind and solar with transmission, storage and inter-regional backup.

**An industry inside the industry.** UHV is also a supply chain of transformers, converter stations, cables, insulators and control software. Chinese makers grew on domestic orders and increasingly sell abroad.

### UHV in three sentences, for your next meeting

1.  Power is made far away and used close by.
2.  The higher the voltage, the less you lose on the way.
3.  China has built the world’s longest network of these highways and keeps building.

The CFO usually adds a fourth: “Could we do that?” That one takes more than three sentences.

### The limits

UHV is no magic wand. Lines are expensive, slow to build and need approvals across several provinces. If one end lacks generation or the other lacks demand, a line runs underused, and Chinese industry press has discussed such cases. Wires alone don’t solve night-time for solar or calm days for wind, so storage and flexible generation are growing alongside.

And the bigger the line, the bigger the consequences of a fault. Much of the spending goes on things nobody photographs: redundancy, digital control, inter-regional links.

### Why it is worth seeing in person

A DC converter station makes little sense in a photo: a field of grey towers and white insulators. It starts to make sense when an engineer explains where the power came from, where it goes and what happens if the station stops.
