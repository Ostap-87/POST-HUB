The easiest place to watch robots today is your phone. A humanoid does a backflip, a robot dog climbs stairs, a hand folds a towel. The clip lasts forty seconds, and for forty seconds the robot is perfect.

What the clip doesn’t show is how many takes there were, who is standing off camera with a controller, and what happens in second forty-one.

Second forty-one is why we take people to China.

According to Xinhua, China has more than 140 humanoid makers, which shipped around 14,400 units in 2025, over 80% of the global market. Analysts’ numbers vary, but the direction is clear: if you want to see robotics at work rather than in adverts, this is where to go. The only question is where exactly. There are three hubs, and they are very different.

### Shenzhen: everything within an hour

Shenzhen is a city where a robot is built from whatever is nearby. Motors, gearboxes, boards, sensors, batteries, housings: suppliers for almost everything are a short drive away. A local tactile-sensor maker told Xinhua that its circuit-board iteration cycle has shrunk to one or two weeks.

In Nanshan district, a strip about 10 km long between two mountains is called Robot Valley. It holds more than 200 robotics firms and around a dozen universities. City officials put Shenzhen’s robotics output above 240 billion yuan in 2025. The city is home to [UBTech](/en/companies/ubtech), [Dobot](/en/companies/dobot) (which says it has led global cobot exports for eight years), EngineAI, Leju and robotics units of large tech firms.

That last group matters. In April 2026, the Beijing robot half-marathon was won by a humanoid from Honor, a smartphone maker. Running autonomously, it covered 21 km in 50 minutes 26 seconds, faster than the human world record. A year earlier, the winning robot needed 2 hours 40 minutes.

What to see in Shenzhen: production and the supply chain. This is the city for engineers, buyers and anyone who wants to know why Chinese robots cost less. The answer is not wages. It’s the distance between factories.

### Hangzhou: start-ups with attitude

In robotics, Hangzhou is the city of companies that grew from small teams. In 2025 local media coined the “six little dragons of Hangzhou”: [Unitree](/en/companies/unitree), DeepSeek, Game Science, BrainCo, Deep Robotics and Manycore. Two of the six build robots.

Unitree is the story of 2026. Founded in 2016, it listed on Shanghai’s STAR Market in August. Its prospectus, as reported by Xinhua, shows revenue rising from 159 million yuan in 2023 to about 1.7 billion yuan in 2025, with more than 5,500 humanoids shipped in 2025. The retail tranche was reportedly oversubscribed thousands of times, and the shares multiplied in price on day one.

Deep Robotics builds quadrupeds for inspection: substations, tunnels, industrial sites. Less spectacular than a backflip, much closer to revenue.

What to see in Hangzhou: how a small team becomes a company with a production product, and how the city helps. This is the city for founders, investors and anyone launching a new business line. It feels more like a campus than an industrial zone, and that’s a compliment.

### Beijing: science, state and stage

Beijing is the third pole and works differently. Universities, research institutes, state programmes and large customers carry weight here. Beijing E-Town (Yizhuang) hosts the Beijing Humanoid Robot Innovation Center, the robot half-marathon and the annual World Robot Conference. In 2025 the city also staged the World Humanoid Robot Games.

Beijing is a city of the stage. Robotics is shown to the public, investors and officials, and shown well. That is not a criticism: competitions are both a test bench and advertising. But watch the stage with one question in mind: what of this will be working on Monday?

What to see in Beijing: research, foundation models for robots, developer platforms, state-backed projects. This is the city for R&D teams, universities and companies thinking about their own development, not just purchasing.

### How to choose

In one line each:

**Shenzhen**: how it is made and why it is cheap. **Hangzhou**: how it is invented and turned into a company. **Beijing**: how it is researched, funded and shown.

The ideal week covers two of the three. All three in a week is possible, but you’ll see more stations than robots. Shenzhen and Hangzhou are linked by direct flights, Hangzhou and Beijing by high-speed rail.

### Questions worth bringing

1.  How many hours does the robot run between charges on a real task, not on the spec sheet?
2.  What share of last year’s shipments is working at customer sites rather than in showrooms and universities?
3.  Who services the robot outside China, and how?
4.  What in the video is autonomous, and what is teleoperated?
5.  What did this robot cost three years ago, and what will it cost next year?

People rarely ask the last one. They should. Chinese robot prices are falling so fast that it can matter more than all the others.

### The bottom line

The robot in the video is always perfect. The robot on the shop floor sometimes stumbles, sometimes waits for an operator, sometimes just sits on its charger. But it is real.

China is currently the only place where you can see real robots by the hundred on a single route. In a few years there will be more of them and less surprise.

Go while it still surprises you.
