Shoppers in many markets are already spoiled: groceries in half an hour, next-day parcels, pick-up points on every corner. It feels as if nothing could surprise us.

China doesn’t surprise you with speed. It surprises you with the fact that a shop has stopped being a shop. It has become a warehouse, a studio, a showroom and a fulfilment point at once, and the customer standing inside is welcome but optional.

That’s what we go to see.

### The scale you need to understand

According to the National Bureau of Statistics, China’s retail sales of consumer goods reached 50.12 trillion yuan in 2025. Online sales of physical goods were 13.09 trillion yuan, or 26.1% of the total.

Note what that means: nearly three quarters of retail still doesn’t go through online channels. Physical retail didn’t die. It learned to work like e-commerce.

### Shanghai: the store within three kilometres

The textbook case is Hema (Freshippo), Alibaba’s grocery chain. Its first store opened in Shanghai in 2016, and the formula has barely changed: the store is both sales floor and warehouse, orders are picked straight off the shelves, bags travel along overhead rails, and delivery is promised within 30 minutes inside a three-kilometre radius.

Hema lost money for years and experimented constantly, opening and closing formats. According to press reports, in the fiscal year ending March 2025 it posted its first annual profit, with around 430 stores in its core format.

The lesson is not the overhead rail. It’s nine years of honest experimentation: close what fails, keep what works. The rails are a by-product.

Shanghai also shows the next stage: instant delivery of everything. In summer 2025, Meituan, Alibaba and JD.com fought a price war in instant retail and food delivery. Alibaba announced a 50-billion-yuan subsidy programme, regulators told the companies to calm down, and Meituan guided to a 2025 net loss of 23 to 24 billion yuan after a profit the year before.

The outcome is simple. Shoppers got nearly free coffee, companies got losses, and the market got a habit of 30-minute delivery. The habit looks set to outlast the subsidies.

### Chengdu: where brands go first

Chengdu is a different story. It isn’t a tech capital, it’s a consumption capital. The city lives slowly, eats very well and is regarded as one of China’s biggest spenders, with annual retail sales above 1 trillion yuan.

Its signature policy is the “first-store economy”: attracting brands to open their first store in the region, in China, or in a new format. According to People’s Daily, more than 4,000 such stores have opened since 2019, and the target for 2027 is 6,500.

For a retailer, Chengdu shows in a couple of days how brands choose a test city, how flagship and concept stores are built, and how open-air districts such as Taikoo Li work without a classic roofed mall. People come for the time, not the purchase. The purchase happens on the way.

Shanghai shows the store turning into a warehouse. Chengdu shows the store turning into a place. Seeing both helps you work out where your own business sits between them.

### Live commerce: the salesperson who never clocks off

Live-stream selling on Douyin, Taobao and other platforms stopped being exotic long ago. Brands, shops, factories and sometimes digital avatars all do it.

Visiting a live-stream studio is not about glamour. You see that selling on camera is production: a script, lighting, assortment, a warehouse behind the wall, real-time analytics and a team of several people behind each host.

The host smiles. Behind the host, someone is counting.

### What transfers and what doesn’t

**Transfers almost fully:** the store-as-warehouse model for grocery in dense urban areas; the discipline of experimentation (launch, measure, close if it fails); first stores as a marketing tool; live streams treated as a production process.

**Transfers partly:** delivery speed, since population density in most cities is lower; automated order picking, which only pays back above a certain volume.

**Hardly transfers:** the scale of subsidies and tech-giant price wars; the population density of a Shanghai district that makes a three-kilometre radius work.

Copying the shop window is pointless. Copying the logic is the whole point.

### Who should go

Grocery and fashion chains, retail property developers, e-commerce teams, heads of logistics. We especially recommend bringing whoever runs your warehouse. They will see things in the store that a marketer will take for interior design.

### Four questions to ask in any Chinese store

1.  What share of orders is picked for online, and what share of shoppers walk in?
2.  How many SKUs, and who decides what gets delisted?
3.  How often does the floor layout change?
4.  What did you close in the past year, and why?

The last question is the most awkward and the most valuable. Companies talk about successes readily. They talk about mistakes to people who ask.

### The bottom line

We tend to think online beat offline. In China it’s clear there was no war. There was a marriage of convenience: the store gave online its shelves, and online gave the store its customers.

Judging by the numbers, the marriage works. Even if there have been some rows about money.
