Nobody tips taxi drivers in China, and nobody ever did. So the headline cheats a little. What is new is that you can now leave out the driver as well.

Robotaxis in China stopped being a stunt for journalists some time ago. They are a service you hail from an app like any other car, and in several cities they share the streets with ordinary taxis. The three most visible companies are [Baidu](/en/companies/baidu)’s Apollo Go, [Pony.ai](/en/companies/pony-ai) and WeRide. Here is how many cars they run, how many rides they give and where they are heading next. All figures come from company reports as of late summer 2026.

### Apollo Go: three million rides a quarter

Apollo Go is the largest operator. In the first quarter of 2026 it completed 3.2 million fully driverless rides, with no safety driver on board. Total rides grew more than 120% year on year, and in March weekly volume peaked at over 350,000. By April 2026 the service had given the public more than 22 million rides in total.

In its second-quarter report Baidu listed 28 cities worldwide and more than 350 million autonomous kilometres, over 240 million of them with nobody at the wheel. A round trip to the Moon is about 770,000 km. Apollo Go has covered that some three hundred times and, as far as the filings show, never once stopped to ask for directions.

The service’s best-known city is Wuhan. By August 2024 more than 400 robotaxis were operating there, and Baidu planned to reach 1,000 by the end of that year. That was when Chinese social media began debating whether rides were too cheap and whether taxi drivers should worry. Drivers interviewed by local media worried in different ways: some about competing with a car that never needs a break, others about who is liable when something goes wrong.

### Who is responsible when nobody is driving?

The drivers had a point. China has no single national law on autonomous driving, only national testing rules and local regulations. Wuhan, for instance, holds the owner or operator of a driverless vehicle liable for accidents, with the right to seek compensation from other parties. Beijing brought its own autonomous vehicle regulations into force on 1 April 2025.

The result is a telling pattern: the technology is tested nationally, while the rules are written city by city. That is not a flaw in the system but the way it works. A city experiments first; the country generalises later.

In a driverless taxi, responsibility does not disappear. It just moves from the front seat to the legal department.

### Pony.ai: four megacities and per-car profitability

Pony.ai operates in China’s four largest cities: Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou and Shenzhen. On 30 June 2026 it had 1,975 robotaxis, and it plans more than 3,500 seventh-generation vehicles by year-end. In Guangzhou its service area has expanded since January from the Nansha district into four central districts, adding over 300 square kilometres. In Shenzhen it serves destinations including Bao’an airport and passenger terminals.

The more important number for understanding the business is a different one. Pony.ai says it has reached positive unit economics per vehicle in Guangzhou and Shenzhen: each car earns more than it costs to run. That does not mean the company as a whole is profitable; its net loss only narrowed in the second quarter. But for an industry that has lived on investor money for a decade, it is a milestone.

Robotaxi revenue reached 81.9 million yuan in the second quarter of 2026, almost eight times the year before, and for the first time made up a third of total revenue. Registered app users in China passed 1.5 million by August.

### WeRide and the road abroad

If Pony.ai is betting on four megacities, WeRide is most visible outside China. In Abu Dhabi, together with Uber, it launched the Middle East’s first fully driverless commercial robotaxi service. In February 2026 WeRide and Uber announced plans to put at least 1,200 robotaxis on the roads of Abu Dhabi, Dubai and Riyadh, possibly by 2027. At the time WeRide had more than 200 vehicles in the region.

All three are expanding abroad on a broad front. Apollo Go has started fully driverless commercial operations in Dubai, including via the Uber app, received Hong Kong’s first fully driverless testing permits, and is testing in London with Uber and Lyft and in Switzerland with PostBus. Pony.ai launched a commercial service in Zagreb with the Croatian partner Verne, began testing in Luxembourg and carries passengers in Singapore through ComfortDelGro’s app.

Notice which partner keeps turning up: Uber. A company that once promised to turn everyone into a taxi driver is now helping to do without the driver altogether. Nothing personal. Just platforms.

### The robotaxi passenger: a quick guide

1.  Book the car in the app, choosing a pickup point the car is allowed to find.
2.  Prove you are you: phone number, a code on the screen, or a door that unlocks only for you.
3.  Sit in the back. The steering wheel turns on its own, and that is best watched from a distance.
4.  Buckle up. The car will remind you, and unlike a human driver it will not sigh.
5.  Get out. You may say thank you. Nobody is obliged to answer.

### What the passenger sees and what the operator sees

The passenger sees a car that arrives on time, drives carefully, sometimes too carefully, and never puts on the radio you did not ask for. The passenger also sees the limits: robotaxis run in defined zones, cannot stop just anywhere and can mean a long wait at rush hour.

The operator sees something else entirely. A taxi’s biggest cost is the driver, and that is the cost robotaxis promise to remove. In its place come expensive sensors, computing hardware, remote operators, insurance, cleaning, charging and maintenance. The economics work when a car drives a lot, idles little and costs little to build. That is why companies talk so much about new vehicle generations: each one is cheaper than the last.

For foreign businesses, robotaxis matter beyond transport. They are a ready-made model of how China takes a new technology into mass use: the city grants a permit and a zone, the company builds up mileage, the regulator studies the statistics, and the zone grows. The same sequence shows up in drone delivery, healthcare and energy.

### The bottom line

Cab drivers have inspired a whole genre of urban folklore: political commentary, life advice, an unerring sense of where to eat. A robotaxi offers none of this. It just gets you there.

Perhaps that is all anyone wanted from a taxi in the first place. Or perhaps, a few years from now, we will start to miss the political commentary. We leave the ending to you.
