Artificial intelligence has one inconvenient feature for travellers: you cannot touch it. You can photograph a robot, walk through a factory, kick the tyres of a car. A model lives in a server room, and nobody takes visitors into server rooms. Not that there is much to see: racks, blinking lights, air conditioning set to “polar”.

Which raises a fair question: why go to China for AI if it all lives in the cloud anyway?

Because the cloud is only half the story. The other half is people: the ones who decide where to deploy a model, what data to train it on, what it costs and who pays. Those people are visible. You can talk to them. Sometimes the queue at the coffee machine in a startup incubator tells you more about where the money is than any report.

We have already written about China’s three robotics clusters. This piece covers the same cities with a different question: not where the hardware is made, but where the intelligence is built and put to use. Every figure comes with a date. In this industry, half-year-old data ages faster than a brochure can be printed.

### Beijing: where models get their papers

If you go by the paperwork, Beijing is the capital of Chinese AI. Generative AI services in China must be filed with the Cyberspace Administration, and according to Xinhua, in April 2026 Beijing accounted for 225 such models, around 30% of the national total. By June 2026 China Daily put the figure at 241. The city also has more than 2,500 AI companies and nearly 40 AI unicorns, more than half of China’s total, and its core AI industry generated over RMB 450 billion in 2025.

Baidu, ByteDance, Zhipu, Moonshot, Baichuan and 01.AI all work here, alongside Peking and Tsinghua universities, research labs and Haidian’s Wudaokou neighbourhood, where student cafés sit next to startup offices. Incubators such as Beijing AI Genesis Community operate here too. A representative of one told China Daily that you come in for a coffee and leave with your next investor.

Most cities offer the coffee. Beijing apparently throws in the investor.

What to see in Beijing: how foundation models are built and filed, how universities hand research to companies, how regulation works in practice. Beijing suits heads of strategy, R&D teams and anyone trying to see where the industry will be in two or three years. “What should we buy tomorrow” is a harder question to answer here.

### Hangzhou: where AI comes with vouchers

Hangzhou is the home of Alibaba, its Qwen models and Alibaba Cloud. It is also home to DeepSeek, which everyone has heard of and which says very little about itself. It tends to be first on visitors’ wish lists and absent from their actual schedules.

For an executive, something else is more interesting: how the city helps ordinary companies use AI. In 2025 Hangzhou announced a RMB 1 billion computing-voucher programme over four years, covering up to 60% of local firms’ costs for buying compute and model access. Its targets for the end of 2025 were to double computing capacity from 25 to 50 EFLOPS, build more than 25 industry-specific models and push core AI industry revenue above RMB 390 billion. In December 2025 the city declared its ambition to become China’s leading AI hub, naming DeepSeek, Unitree and BrainCo as its champions.

A compute voucher sounds like a ration card for electricity. But in an era when models are nearly free and compute is expensive, it is the most practical form of support there is: the government does not tell you what to do with intelligence, it pays for the plug.

What to see in Hangzhou: the cloud platform and how mid-sized businesses in e-commerce, manufacturing and services plug models in through it; Future Sci-Tech City in the western innovation corridor; the mechanics of subsidies. Hangzhou suits business owners and digital transformation leads who need to know how to start without a data centre of their own.

### Shenzhen: where AI goes inside the box

Shenzhen approaches AI the way an engineer approaches a box: what is inside, and how many per shift? According to the city government, its core AI industry brought in about RMB 220 billion in 2025. For 2026 the city has set a target of RMB 1 trillion in smart-device output, more than 150 million units and over 50 AI products: phones, computers, all-in-one servers with a model preinstalled, and wearables.

The city hands out vouchers too, itemised: RMB 500 million for model training, RMB 50 million for data and RMB 100 million for models. A RMB 10 billion AI and robotics fund is in place. The same source says large models are deployed in 30 top-tier hospitals, and at BYD machine vision detects battery defects with 99.8% accuracy. At Honor’s campus in Pingshan, a device rolls off the line every 28.5 seconds. Longgang district, the city says, has set up China’s first dedicated administration for AI and robotics.

Shenzhen is also home to the headquarters of Huawei, maker of the Ascend AI chips, and Tencent, developer of the Hunyuan models.

In Shenzhen, AI does not give keynote speeches. It sits in a box with a barcode already printed on it.

What to see in Shenzhen: how AI becomes a product with a unit cost, how factories use machine vision and models for quality control, and how “AI in a box” solutions work for companies that do not want their data in someone else’s cloud. Shenzhen suits manufacturers, electronics distributors and anyone thinking about what to sell next.

### Putting the route together

In short: Beijing answers “where is this all going”, Hangzhou answers “how do we use it”, and Shenzhen answers “what is it made of and what does it cost”.

In a week, two of the three cities is sensible. All three is possible, but then the trip’s main memories will be railway stations rather than people. And choose cities by task rather than by famous names: which single problem in your company do you want AI to solve?

The worst AI itinerary has nine meetings and none of your own questions.

### Six questions to bring

1.  Which models do you use, and why these rather than the alternatives?
2.  What did the launch and the first year cost, and what turned out more expensive than planned?
3.  What data did you need, and how long did it take to prepare?
4.  How do you measure results, and who checks those measurements?
5.  Which parts of your solution could work outside China, and what would happen to the data then?
6.  What did you deploy and then switch off?

The sixth question matters most. People answer it reluctantly but honestly. Failed projects never feature on a company tour, yet they are the ones that save you a year.

### In place of a conclusion

People used to travel to the capital to get smarter. Then abroad. Now, apparently, to a server room they are not allowed into.

The good news is that in China the intelligence is not only in the server room. It is in the engineer who explains why the model makes more mistakes on Fridays, in the shop owner who handed advertising to an agent and now sleeps an hour longer, in the official counting vouchers. Those people you can actually reach.

The model can wait. It is in the cloud anyway.
