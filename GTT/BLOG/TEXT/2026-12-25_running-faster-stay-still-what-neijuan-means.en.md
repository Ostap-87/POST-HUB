There is one Chinese word that explains the economy better than most charts. The word is neijuan, 内卷. It is usually translated as “involution”, a translation that clears up nothing at all.

So here is the plain version. Ten coffee shops on one street. One cuts the price of a latte by a yuan. The others cut by two. Six months later coffee costs the same as water, the barista does three jobs, the owner sleeps in the storeroom, and the street has exactly as many customers as before. Everyone ran flat out. Nobody got anywhere.

That is neijuan: competition that leaves a market not bigger, just more tired.

### Where the word comes from

The term started in anthropology. In 1963 the American scholar Clifford Geertz described “agricultural involution” in Java: more and more labour went into the same rice fields, and output per person did not rise. The fields grew more intricate. Life did not improve.

Chinese social media picked the word up around 2020 and applied it to everything: weekend tutoring for schoolchildren, offices where nobody leaves before the boss, students cycling with a laptop balanced on the handlebars. It began as a joke about life. Then it turned out to describe entire industries.

When a word moves from memes into Politburo-level documents, the joke is over.

### From complaint to policy

On 1 July 2025 the Central Financial and Economic Affairs Commission, chaired by Xi Jinping, pledged to rein in “disorderly low-price competition” and deal with excess capacity. Markets reacted at once, with solar, steel and cement stocks rising on hopes that the dumping would end.

Legislation followed. On 27 June 2025 the NPC Standing Committee had already passed a revised Anti-Unfair Competition Law, in force since 15 October 2025. Platforms may no longer force sellers to price below cost. Large companies may not use their weight to impose unreasonable payment terms on smaller suppliers or sit on their money. A draft amendment to the Pricing Law, also targeting below-cost sales, was published in summer 2025.

In March 2026 the phrase made it into the government work report: “address excessive involution-style competition”, a few lines from a growth target of 4.5–5%. It is not every day that a government asks business to compete a little less.

### Three fronts: cars, food, sunlight

**Cars.** The auto price war ran for more than two years before anyone tackled it seriously. In May 2025 the industry association CAAM publicly criticised carmakers that had started a fresh round of discounts, and the industry ministry backed it. In June, 17 major manufacturers including BYD, FAW and SAIC pledged to pay suppliers within 60 days. In November the market regulator SAMR noted that selling at a loss carries legal risk.

Sixty days. For a supplier, that is not a payment term. That is a holiday.

**Food delivery.** JD.com entered food delivery in February 2025. In July, Alibaba announced 50 billion yuan in subsidies for its instant-commerce arm. Meituan, until then comfortably profitable, matched them. SAMR summoned executives from all three in May and again in July. The bill shows up in the accounts: according to Caixin, Meituan, which earned 35.8 billion yuan net in 2024, lost 18.6 billion in the third quarter of 2025 alone and ended the year in the red.

Customers ate almost for free for half a year. Shareholders, couriers and restaurants picked up the tab. There is such a thing as a free lunch, it turns out. Someone else just gets the bill.

**Solar.** So much capacity was built that polysilicon and module prices fell faster than costs for years. On 31 July 2026 SAMR gathered manufacturers in Yancheng and told them to tighten cost accounting and pricing compliance. Days earlier, a voluntary industry standard for cost accounting across the chain, from polysilicon to modules, had been published. Nobody set a price floor. Yet on 3 August the most-traded polysilicon futures contract in Guangzhou hit its 8.99% daily limit, closing at 35,890 yuan a tonne. The market took the hint.

### A mock memo to industry

Rendered in official prose, the campaign would read something like this (the memo is invented; any resemblance to real directives is coincidental and nearly unavoidable):

1.  Compete. But not like that.
2.  Cut prices. But not below cost.
3.  Calculate your costs. Properly, this time.
4.  Pay your suppliers. Ideally this quarter.
5.  Expand capacity where it is lacking. Where it is not, do not.
6.  Reread item 5.

### What changes for partners and buyers

For anyone sourcing in China, recent years have been golden: every year you could walk into a negotiation and ask for another five percent off. The anti-involution campaign makes that habit less dependable. A few things are worth keeping in mind.

Below-market prices may be the first to go. If a supplier sold at a loss to keep its lines busy, it now has fewer reasons and more risk in doing so. Good news for quality, less good for the annual haggle.

Cost calculations become a negotiating tool. In sectors with new cost-accounting standards, your counterpart may arrive not with “let’s find a deal” but with a spreadsheet. It pays to be able to read it.

Payment terms become a topic. Rules against large firms squeezing suppliers apply to supply chains with foreign participants too.

Most importantly, weaker players will exit. Consolidation in solar, autos and delivery is close to inevitable. Choose a five-year partner by resilience, not by the lowest line on the price list.

The cheapest price is the one your supplier can survive.

### What you can see on site

In reports and headlines, involution is a number. On a factory floor it looks different: how busy the lines are, how full the finished-goods warehouse is, how an engineer answers a question about unit cost, and who turns up to the meeting at all. On Global Tech Tour expeditions we encourage participants to ask these questions directly and to note not only the answers but the pause before them.

The campaign is in its second year and its outcome is unclear. Too much capacity has already been built, and too many cities depend on it. Stopping a treadmill is harder than starting one: whoever steps off first fears the others will keep running.

Still, the admission itself matters. A country that won for twenty years on speed and price has said, officially, that faster and cheaper is not always better.

Now the ten coffee shops on that street just need to agree.
