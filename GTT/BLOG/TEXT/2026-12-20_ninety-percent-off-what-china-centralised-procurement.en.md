Every supplier dreams of a customer who buys a lot. Every supplier fears a customer who buys everything.

In Chinese healthcare both happened at once. Since 2018 the state has pooled the orders of thousands of hospitals into one large tender and told manufacturers: here is a guaranteed volume for several years, name your price. The prices that followed made it into textbooks. Or at least into investor reports, which read them in the tone usually reserved for obituaries.

This article is not about medicine, and not about whether the system is good or bad for patients. It is about the mechanics of a market that suddenly has one buyer the size of a country. Those mechanics matter to anyone: some of us already have such a buyer, others will meet one.

### How it works: volume for price

The system is called volume-based procurement, VBP, or “jicai” in Chinese. The National Healthcare Security Administration (NHSA) collects hospitals’ demand for a given drug. Manufacturers compete for that volume. Winners get a guaranteed share of public hospital purchases for the contract period, usually several years. Losers keep the right to sell outside the tender, into whatever is left of the market, which tends not to be much.

For a generic maker, it resembles a contract with a giant retail chain. You no longer pay sales reps to [visit](/en/expeditions) every hospital and charm every department head. Volume is guaranteed. Price is the fee.

Sales used to be done by people with briefcases. Now they are done by a spreadsheet.

### The “4+7” pilot: the first number that shocked everyone

It began in late 2018 with a pilot in 11 cities: four municipalities and seven large cities, hence “4+7”. Twenty-five generics went to tender. Winning prices were on average 52% below the lowest prices of 2017. Domestic manufacturers won 23 of the 25 contracts; multinationals won two.

That was the first signal. Off-patent originator drugs that had sold at brand prices for decades lost to local generics. Not because they were worse. Because they were dearer.

The programme then went national and ran in rounds. According to the NHSA, by the end of 2024, after the tenth round, 435 drugs had been procured centrally. The tenth round, the largest to that point, covered 62 drugs, and Xinhua reported average price cuts of more than 60%. Its examples: a year of antiviral hepatitis B treatment fell from 4,000–5,000 yuan to 100–200 yuan, a lenalidomide capsule from about 200 yuan to 15.

The numbers look like typos. You check them again. They stay the same.

### Stents: the story that became a symbol

The most quoted story involved devices rather than pills. In November 2020 a national tender for coronary stents was held. According to the National Health Commission, the average price per stent fell from about 13,000 yuan to 700. That is 93% off, which makes our headline modest.

What happened to manufacturers? They survived, but reorganised. MicroPort’s interim report for the first half of 2021 named lower stent prices under VBP as one of the two main reasons for its loss. Its stents had won the tender, and it ranked first by awarded volume. Winning the tender and booking a loss is an entirely normal combination in this system.

In 2023 Medtronic acknowledged that VBP had hit it harder than many competitors, and its CEO said up to 80% of its China portfolio could be affected. He also made an important observation: “the government is living up to the volume commitments from those VBPs at these lower prices.” The promised volume really does arrive. It just costs less than you would like.

Later rounds added cochlear implants, artificial joints and surgical consumables. In December 2024, People’s Daily reported that the average price of a cochlear implant with a speech processor was expected to fall from over 200,000 yuan to around 50,000.

### The 11th round: the buyer learns too

The most interesting part came not in the early rounds but in the latest one. The 11th round, announced in summer 2025, used new rules. Provisional results were published on 27 October 2025: 55 drugs, 445 companies submitting 794 products, 272 companies with 453 products winning supply rights. Across 11 rounds, 490 drugs have now gone through the system.

The new rules come with four slogans: stabilise clinical use, ensure quality, stop “involution” (destructive price racing), and prevent bid-rigging. In practice, the lowest price no longer wins automatically. Price benchmarks are no longer calculated from the lowest bid, and the lowest bidder must justify its price against production cost. Generic makers need experience producing similar drugs and a clean GMP record for the specific production line. Hospitals can now order by brand, not only by generic name.

In plain market language: the buyer realised it had squeezed too hard. When prices fall below cost, suppliers start saving on things that should not be saved on. The state noticed and put in a floor.

The biggest buyer is not the one who pays least. It is the one who can afford to pay a little more and know exactly what for.

### What it means if you have your own “NHSA”

Centralised procurement is a Chinese healthcare story, but its logic appears wherever a dominant buyer emerges: a national retail chain, a state company with a single procurement centre, a marketplace that carries half your sales. A few conclusions suggest themselves.

1.  Guaranteed volume is not a gift; it is a trade. Work out in advance what price you can name and where the margin will come from.
2.  The most exposed are those who sold on brand rather than on cost.
3.  The winners have the lowest costs and can deliver large volumes at consistent quality.
4.  After you win, head for where there is no tender: new products, the private segment, exports.
5.  The buyer changes the rules too. You can profit from loopholes in the first tender; by the second they will be closed.
6.  Yes, the spreadsheet is now your top salesperson. Even spreadsheets get recalculated.

### Who survived and where they went

Chinese drugmakers that lived on generics faced a choice after several VBP rounds: become the lowest-cost, highest-volume producer, or move into original research. Many chose the latter, which is one reason global pharma now licenses Chinese innovative molecules. We will cover that in a separate article.

Device makers moved in several directions at once: exports, categories not yet in procurement, and premium products. Multinationals partly withdrew from segments where they lost on price and partly learned to play by the new rules, with mixed success.

### The bottom line

If you make or distribute medical products and are travelling to China, VBP is not an abstract topic but the backdrop to every meeting. Ask your Chinese counterparts about it: what changed in their sales, how they rebuilt their margins, what they do in markets without centralised tenders. The answers are usually more candid than the annual report.

The price of a stent fell by 93%. The cost of getting your own unit economics wrong has only gone up.
