We were promised flying cars. The future has arrived, the cars are still on the ground, but lunch now flies. On reflection that is the more sensible order: lunch weighs less than a car and rarely argues with air traffic control.

In China this future has an official name: 低空经济, the “low-altitude economy”. It covers economic activity built around crewed and uncrewed aircraft operating, as a rule, within about 1,000 metres of the ground. Some documents set the ceiling at 3,000 metres, but the idea is the same. The sky below the clouds has been declared a new high street, and shops are expected to open on it.

So let us see which shops are already trading and which are still painted on the hoarding.

### From conference title to government report

A few years ago “low-altitude economy” sounded like the name of a trade conference. In March 2024 it appeared for the first time in the government work report, listed among new engines of growth. After that things moved on Chinese time: once a term reaches the work report, ministries grow around it.

In December 2024 the National Development and Reform Commission (NDRC) created a dedicated low-altitude economy department. In May 2026 the Civil Aviation Administration of China (CAAC) set up a low-altitude safety department to draft development plans, coordinate safety and build flight service platforms. Shenzhen had already brought in its own regulation to support the industry in February 2024.

One department means it is no longer a dream. Two departments mean it is no longer simple.

### What it is worth

The market estimates are large. The NDRC, citing the CAAC, has put the low-altitude economy at 1.5 trillion yuan in 2025 and more than 3.5 trillion yuan by 2035. These are broad aggregates that include everything from drone manufacturing to infrastructure, tourism and services, so comparing them with any one company’s revenue is not meaningful.

The regulators’ operating data are more tangible. By the end of 2025 China had 3.287 million registered civil drones, up 51% in a year. They logged 45.3 million flight hours in 2025, up 69.9%. Civil drone output was valued at 176.1 billion yuan: 125.9 billion for industrial models and 50.2 billion for consumer ones.

Note the ratio. Industrial drones outsold consumer ones two and a half to one. China’s sky is mostly at work, not filming weddings.

### Lunch by air: how Meituan flies

For city dwellers, the most visible part of this economy is delivery. [Meituan](/en/companies/meituan), China’s largest food delivery platform, completed its first real drone order in Shenzhen in early 2021 and opened its first regular route in Shanghai in December 2022.

By the end of 2025, the company says, its drones had completed more than 780,000 orders on 70 routes in six cities: Shenzhen, Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou, Hong Kong and Dubai. Daily orders per site grew from around 10 to as many as 400, and the cost per order fell by 40–50% a year. In 2026 Meituan put its drone delivery infrastructure into commercial service and signed up ten service providers.

Now for scale. 780,000 orders over five years is fewer than Meituan’s riders deliver on the ground in an hour of an ordinary day. Drones are not replacing couriers. They fly where couriers struggle: across rivers, up tourist mountains, into parks and along waterfronts.

The future rarely starts where life is easy. Where life is easy, the present copes fine.

### A taxi with no pilot: EHang’s long road to a ticket

The more spectacular half of the story is passenger eVTOLs, aircraft that take off and land vertically. The best-known Chinese example is the two-seat, pilotless EHang EH216-S from Guangzhou.

Its certification path is worth retelling because it shows how the Chinese regulator handles new technology. The CAAC issued a type certificate in October 2023, a standard airworthiness certificate in December 2023 and a production certificate in April 2024. In March 2025 two operators, the subsidiary Guangdong EHang General Aviation and the joint venture Hefei HeYi Aviation, received China’s first air operator certificates for passenger-carrying pilotless aircraft. Permitted uses: low-altitude tourism, urban sightseeing and commercial passenger flights at sites in Guangzhou and Hefei.

Four certificates in eighteen months. Then comes the interesting part. In its first-quarter 2026 results, EHang said the operators had flown more than 3,000 flights in internal trial operations since March 2025 without incident, while ticket sales to the public were awaiting final CAAC approval. For Hefei the company cited an early-bird fare of 299 yuan per seat and a plan for 14 flights a day. Quarterly revenue was 25.7 million yuan, against full-year 2026 guidance of 600 million. Thailand was named as the first overseas market.

Check the dates when you read these numbers. In this industry status changes faster than articles are published.

### Five signs you are looking at a trade show, not an economy

1.  The aircraft flies only on opening day and only over the stand.
2.  The ticket price has been announced; the ticket office has not.
3.  The route map has no rivers, buildings or power lines on it.
4.  Air traffic control is a man with a radio next to the marquee.
5.  The most confident thing in the project is the date by which it will “transform the city”.

### What works and what is still a demo

Sort the low-altitude economy by maturity and the picture looks like this.

Working at scale: industrial drones in farming, power line inspection, surveying and mapping. Millions of aircraft, tens of millions of flight hours, and the main news is that there is hardly any news.

Working commercially, but in pockets: drone delivery in selected districts of big cities, at tourist sites and in places that are hard to reach by road.

Leaving the pilot stage: pilotless passenger aircraft on sightseeing routes. The certificates, aircraft and sites exist. The mass passenger does not yet.

Still on paper: air taxis as regular urban transport with hundreds of routes. That needs low-altitude traffic management, insurance, accident liability rules and rooftop landing pads. Which is exactly what the new departments are for.

### What it means for business

For a foreign business, the low-altitude economy matters in three ways. As a market for equipment and components: motors, batteries, communications and ground infrastructure. As a regulatory model: China is writing the rules alongside the market, and watching that up close is useful for anyone working with new technology. And as a working service you can study on the ground: drone delivery in Shenzhen is not a slide, it is a business.

Global Tech Tour runs business expeditions to China with closed visits to companies across 17 industries. The low sky is a good example of why it is worth going in person. On video every aircraft flies equally well. The difference between a trade show and an economy is only visible from the ground.

### The bottom line

The cars still do not fly. Lunch flies, fertiliser flies over fields, cameras fly along power lines. It is not the picture the magazines drew in the last century, but it is a far more useful one.

As for flying cars, they will probably follow the usual pattern. First they will take off in China. Then we will read an article about it.
