Ten years ago, “a Chinese drug” meant one of two things to a Western pharma executive. Herbs. Or a generic, interesting precisely because it cost less than the original.

Today the phrase has a third meaning: a molecule for which a large American or British company pays hundreds of millions of dollars up front, before the drug has reached its own market.

I am not a pharmacologist, and this is not about how the drugs work. It is about the deals: who pays whom, for what, and why now. The numbers are big enough that you have to read them slowly. Otherwise you suspect a missing decimal point.

### Numbers to read slowly

According to China’s National Medical Products Administration (NMPA), as reported by the South China Morning Post in January 2026, Chinese drugmakers signed 157 out-licensing deals in 2025, worth a combined US$135.7 billion. A year earlier it was 94 deals worth US$51.9 billion.

In the first half of 2026, according to the same regulator as reported by CCTV and Reuters, there were 81 deals worth about US$110 billion: roughly 80% of the whole of 2025, in six months. The licensees were mainly from the US, Britain, France and Italy; the main areas were oncology, metabolism, immunology and neurology.

One more figure, from Jefferies: in the first half of 2025 about a third of the industry’s licensing spend went on China-originated drugs, compared with about 21% in 2023 and 2024 and single digits before that.

A caveat, without which these numbers mislead. “Deal value” almost always means the upfront payment plus every possible milestone and sales payment. The upfront is paid now. The rest arrives only if the drug passes trials, wins approvals and sells. Most of those billions currently exist in the same state as a bonus for beating targets: on paper, and conditional.

The upfront is money. The rest is a promise with very good lawyers.

### Akeso and ivonescimab: the deal that changed the tone

If you had to pick one story that made the industry look at China differently, it would be ivonescimab from Akeso, based in Zhongshan. It is a bispecific antibody that blocks both PD-1 and VEGF: one molecule doing two jobs that used to need two drugs.

In December 2022 Summit Therapeutics licensed ivonescimab for the US, Canada, Europe and Japan: US$500 million up front, up to US$5 billion in total with milestones, plus royalties. Akeso kept China.

The noise started in 2024. In HARMONi-2, a Phase III trial in China, ivonescimab was compared with pembrolizumab (Keytruda), the world’s best-selling cancer drug. Median progression-free survival was 11.14 months versus 5.82. Nearly double. In autumn 2026 Akeso and Summit reported a statistically significant overall survival benefit in the same trial.

The drug is already approved in China for several indications. In the US, the FDA accepted Summit’s application for one indication, EGFR-mutated non-small cell lung cancer after prior targeted therapy, and set a target action date of 14 November 2026. By the time you read this, the decision may already be out; check the company’s and the FDA’s announcements.

What matters for the industry is less any single result than the fact itself: a Chinese molecule went head to head with the biggest name in oncology. Big pharma went looking for similar molecules in China. In May 2025 [Pfizer](/en/companies/pfizer) paid 3SBio US$1.25 billion up front for another antibody of the same class.

Chinese companies used to be the ones catching up. Now others are catching up with them, partly by buying from them.

### Hengrui: from generics king to portfolio seller

The second telling story is Jiangsu Hengrui, for years China’s largest generic drugmaker. Companies like it were hit hardest by centralised procurement, which we covered in a separate article: once a generic’s tender price falls by half or more, you have to earn money some other way.

Hengrui started investing in its own research long before that, and by the mid-2020s it was selling not single molecules but portfolios. In July 2025 GSK paid Hengrui US$500 million up front for an early-stage inhaled COPD drug and options on up to 11 more programmes in respiratory medicine, immunology and oncology, with a total potential value of up to US$12 billion plus royalties. GSK’s rights cover the world outside Greater China.

That is a different format. Not “we’ll buy your drug”, but “we’ll keep buying your drugs”.

### Why now

Several reasons stack up.

Speed. Chinese biotechs move through early development faster and more cheaply: large patient populations, big clinical centres, a dense network of contract research organisations. For a Western company, it is a fast way to get a candidate into the clinic without building it from scratch.

The patent cliff. Over the next few years big pharma loses patent protection on drugs with multibillion-dollar sales. The pipeline has to be refilled, and in-house labs cannot keep up.

Pressure at home. Centralised procurement made generics a thin-margin business and gave Chinese drugmakers a strong push towards innovation, while the regulator sped up approval of new drugs.

Money. For a Chinese biotech, especially a small one, a foreign upfront payment of a few hundred million dollars is often cheaper and faster than an IPO.

### How to read a “US$5 billion deal” headline

1.  Find the upfront. It is the only number that has already turned into money.
2.  Check the territory. China almost always stays with the Chinese company.
3.  Check the stage. A Phase I asset and a post-Phase III asset are different stories under identical headlines.
4.  See who runs development and who pays for the trials.
5.  Look at royalties. Modest percentages of big sales can be worth more than all the milestones combined.
6.  Mentally remove a zero from the headline. Not always accurate, but very calming.

### What it means outside pharma

The story is useful beyond pharma. It shows what the move from “making it cheaper” to “inventing it first” looks like, and how long it takes: ten to fifteen years of systematic investment in research, people and regulation before the deal everyone writes about.

Medical companies travelling to China should look not only at factories but at how research is organised: clinical centres, contract research organisations, biotech clusters. That is where today’s molecules are born, the ones that will appear in global pharma catalogues in a couple of years under other companies’ logos.

China once bought licences so it could manufacture. Now it sells licences so others can. The circle has closed; it just runs the other way.
