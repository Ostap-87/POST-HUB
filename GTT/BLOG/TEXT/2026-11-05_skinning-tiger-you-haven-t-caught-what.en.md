When executives ask which Chinese AI company will win, they are usually asking the wrong question. The winner will be named later, in hindsight, and everyone will say it was obvious. The right question is humbler: which of them will still be around when your contract runs out?

The story of China’s “Six Tigers” is useful precisely because it answers that. In two and a half years, six startups went through what most industries take a decade to do: from “each worth a billion” to stock listings, career changes and the quiet abandonment of their flagship product. Watching from the sidelines was fascinating. Being their customer at the time must have been even more so.

There is an old warning about selling the skin before you have caught the tiger. Nobody said you can’t inspect the skin in advance.

### Where the tigers came from

In 2023 China went through what was called the “battle of a hundred models”: almost anyone with compute and investors released a large language model. By early 2024 six startups valued above a billion dollars stood out: Zhipu, Moonshot (maker of the Kimi chatbot), Baichuan and 01.AI in Beijing, and MiniMax and StepFun in Shanghai. The press called them the “six little tigers”.

Then three things hit them at once: the 2024 price war among the big platforms, free assistants from ByteDance, Alibaba and Baidu, and, in January 2025, DeepSeek’s open models, which made the expensive cheap almost overnight.

Here is the scoreboard as of October 2026.

### The scoreboard, autumn 2026

**Zhipu** (international brand Z.ai) listed in Hong Kong on 8 January 2026. First-half revenue rose 400% to RMB 953.9 million, while the net loss narrowed from RMB 2.4 billion to RMB 2 billion. Its market value briefly topped HK$1 trillion in June; by late August the shares had lost nearly half their value.

**MiniMax** listed the next day, 9 January. First-half revenue was US$116.6 million, up 283% and already above its full-year 2025 figure. The net loss was US$358 million. The company reports more than 300 million users in over 200 countries and regions, and about US$1.3 billion in cash and deposits.

**Moonshot**, according to the South China Morning Post citing sources, filed confidentially for a Hong Kong IPO in September and is aiming to list from the first quarter of 2027 at the earliest. The company does not comment on rumours.

**StepFun** raised US$2.5 billion in the spring of 2026 and restructured for a listing; in June the Wall Street Journal reported it was preparing a Hong Kong IPO filing. Asia Tech Review estimates its models run on more than 40 million smartphones, and Geely is among its partners.

**Baichuan** was the first of the six to retreat into a niche: medicine. In January 2026 it open-sourced its Baichuan-M3 medical model. Founder Wang Xiaochuan says it has about RMB 3 billion in cash and may list in 2027.

**01.AI**, founded by Kai-Fu Lee, stopped training its own foundation models. It now fine-tunes other companies’ open models (DeepSeek, Qwen, GLM) and installs them on clients’ own servers. It has about 240 staff, roughly half its business outside China, and a listing pencilled in for 2027.

Nobody died. But nobody would call them six of a kind any more.

### Lesson one: revenue is growing faster than the cash is running out. It is still running out

Growth of four or five times in a year looks wonderful until you read the next line. Both listed companies spend considerably more than they earn and live on investor and public-market money. For a partner, it means something simple: the terms you get today are partly paid for with someone else’s capital.

Chinese AI is cheap. Sometimes because it is efficient. Sometimes because someone else is still paying for your discount.

So beyond the price per million tokens, find out how many months of cash the vendor has. For listed companies that is in the filings; for private ones, only in conversation.

### Lesson two: tigers eat different things

From the outside all six sell “large models”. Inside, they are six different businesses.

Zhipu earns much of its money installing models on customers’ premises: according to its prospectus, as summarised by ChinaTalk, the public sector accounted for about 29% of that revenue in the first nine months of 2025 and telecoms for almost 14% more. These are reliable customers with their own particularities. In January 2025 Zhipu was also added to the US Entity List. For a foreign partner, that is not a reason to walk away, but it is a reason to call the lawyers before the engineers.

MiniMax, by contrast, runs global consumer products: the Hailuo video generator, companion apps and a developer platform. StepFun embeds models in phones and cars. 01.AI works as an integrator. Baichuan went into the clinic.

Choosing “Chinese AI” in general is like ordering “Chinese food” in general. First work out whether you came for dinner or for a spice supplier.

### Lesson three: your vendor may change careers

The most useful story for business is not on the stock exchange. After DeepSeek’s releases, Kai-Fu Lee publicly conceded that it no longer made economic sense for a startup to train foundation models from scratch, and 01.AI stopped. The company is alive and working. But a client who once chose it as the developer of its own model now has an integrator of other people’s.

Baichuan made a similar turn, into medicine.

The practical takeaway: an AI project needs a plan for the day the vendor changes its mind. Here, oddly enough, the Chinese market offers insurance. Many models are released with open weights. You can download them, run them yourself and keep using them even if the developer has moved into another industry.

Open weights are a will that a model writes while it is still alive.

### Five questions for a Chinese AI partner

1.  What share of your revenue comes from customers like us, by industry, size and country?
2.  Which chips does the model you are offering run on, and what happens if the supply of those chips changes?
3.  Does the model have open weights, and can we deploy it ourselves if you stop supporting it?
4.  Where will our data physically sit, and which regulations apply to it?
5.  How many times did you change prices in the last year, and in which direction?

If the answer to the fifth is “never”, ask again. In 2026 the only AI vendors that haven’t changed prices are the ones that haven’t started selling yet.

### Why see it for yourself

Four of the six tigers are based in Beijing, two in Shanghai. Listed companies’ filings can be read from anywhere in the world, but filings do not answer questions. People do: the engineer who explains what it costs to move a model onto different chips, the sales manager who either lights up or changes the subject when you say “open weights”.

At Global Tech Tour we run business expeditions to China, including custom programmes built around one company’s task. For an AI-focused trip, such a programme usually starts not with a list of famous names but with a list of questions. Famous names change faster than business cards can be printed.

### In place of a conclusion

There were six tigers, and all six are still alive. In a year the scoreboard will change again: someone will list, someone will switch niches, someone will grow into a big company, and someone will become a department of one.

Choosing a partner in an industry like this means choosing not the one who roars loudest but the one whose future you can picture.

The tiger you pick may stay a tiger. Or it may become an excellent vet. Better to find out before you sign.
