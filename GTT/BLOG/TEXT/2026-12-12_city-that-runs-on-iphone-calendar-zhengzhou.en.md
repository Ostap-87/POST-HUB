Zhengzhou has two calendars. One is the ordinary kind, with public holidays and weekends. The other is Apple’s product launch calendar. Everyone lives by the first. An entire district’s economy lives by the second.

That is an exaggeration, of course. But not much of one.

### The phone that built a runway

Zhengzhou is the capital of Henan, one of China’s most populous provinces. It is a long way from the sea and a long way from any border. For centuries it lived off being a crossroads in the middle of the country: a rail junction, grain, transit. A good place to pass through.

In March 2011, the first Apple phone came off the line near the city’s airport, assembled by Foxconn, the world’s largest contract electronics manufacturer. Two years later, on 7 March 2013, China’s State Council approved the development plan for the Zhengzhou Airport Economy Zone, covering 415 square kilometres. According to the local Zhengzhou Daily, it was the country’s first, and at the time only, airport economy zone approved at State Council level.

The logic was simple. Phones are valuable and light, so it pays to fly them. Make phones next to the airport, turn the airport into a cargo hub, and a city in the middle of the country gets direct access to world markets without a coastline.

A seaport is where the land runs out. An airport is where your patience with container ships runs out.

By the end of 2018 the zone had produced more than 1.3 billion phones in total.

### 200,000 people and one customer

The scale of the plant is hard to picture. According to Caixin, in autumn 2022 Foxconn’s Zhengzhou site employed around 200,000 people. It housed about 80% of Apple’s capacity for the iPhone 14 series and, according to a Counterpoint analyst, more than 85% of iPhone 14 Pro capacity.

That is the population of a mid-sized city, working on one site and, ultimately, for one customer.

In 2024, China News reports, the Zhengzhou Airport Economy Zone handled 409.4 billion yuan of foreign trade, roughly half of Henan province’s total.

The key word is “half”. When half a province’s trade runs through one zone, and the main plant in that zone works mostly for one client, the province gets two things: enormous growth and enormous risk. The first gets discussed at forums. The second, in private.

### Autumn 2022

The risk materialised in autumn 2022. In mid-October, COVID outbreaks hit the plant and Zhengzhou applied its “dynamic zero” policy to the site. Workers began to leave. Videos of people walking home along highways spread online; the Henan authorities later arranged transport.

Caixin reported that the plant needed around 10,000 new hires to restart production, and Foxconn raised pay to lure workers back. In late November there were protests over the terms of new contracts. On 25 November the company apologised for a “technical error” in pay and promised departing workers 10,000 yuan. We will not retell the public disputes over what happened. The business lesson is simpler: one site, one customer and one decision by city authorities together halted a significant part of global output of the world’s best-known phone.

An anchor customer is wonderful, as long as it is an anchor and not a millstone.

### What Zhengzhou did next

After 2022 the city did not abandon electronics, but it noticeably invested in things that do not depend on one customer.

First, the airport itself. According to People’s Daily, Xinzheng airport handled 1.033 million tonnes of cargo and mail in 2025, up 25.2%. In global cargo airport rankings it climbed from 44th in 2023 to 36th in 2024 and into the top 30 in 2025. International cargo topped 660,000 tonnes, fifth in China. The airport hosts 29 all-cargo airlines and 49 cargo routes to 50 cities in 26 countries. Built for phones, it now flies whatever is worth flying, and depends far less on one customer’s schedule.

Second, new industries. Zhengzhou Customs data show Henan’s foreign trade rose 19.3% in January–August 2025, to about 550 billion yuan. Exports of electric passenger cars jumped 261.8% to 16.8 billion yuan, the “new three” (EVs, lithium batteries and solar cells) rose 159.3%, and high-tech exports grew 47.7%.

Zhengzhou’s GDP reached 1.45 trillion yuan in 2024, up 5.7%, placing a former crossroads among China’s 20 largest city economies.

### Signs your business runs on the iPhone calendar

1.  You know your client’s product launch date better than your own holiday dates.
2.  Your sales plan for next year is written by someone outside your sales team.
3.  When your client sneezes, you catch a cold. When your client catches a cold, you go into quarantine.
4.  You have a diversification strategy. It lives in a folder called “Later”.
5.  You are reading this and recognising yourself. That is a good sign: it is not too late.

### A lesson for anyone with an anchor customer

Zhengzhou’s story applies far beyond China and electronics. Many companies have their own Foxconn or their own Apple: one customer that brings in most of the revenue, or one supplier without whom everything stops.

Zhengzhou suggests three things. An anchor customer provides a start you would not otherwise get: without the phones, there would be no cargo airport. But infrastructure built for the anchor must be useful without it. The airport turned out to be that kind of infrastructure, which is why its traffic is still growing. And diversification is best started before the crisis. People start it after a crisis too, just in a very different mood.

On Global Tech Tour business expeditions, we often show participants not only individual plants but how the local economy is built around them. Sometimes the most interesting question is not on the production line but beside it: what happens to this place if the main customer leaves?

Zhengzhou is answering that question in practice, by building an airport that serves everyone rather than one client. Apple’s launch calendar is still remembered here. But the city no longer lives by it alone.
