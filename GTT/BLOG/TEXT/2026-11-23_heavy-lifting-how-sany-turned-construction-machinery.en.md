Some industries seem made for digitisation. Electronics, phones, batteries: everything is small, identical and moves down the conveyor in neat rows. A computer feels at home there.

Then there is heavy machinery. A concrete pump truck weighs tens of tonnes, its boom is as long as a house, and every other order differs from the last. Computers are not comfortable here. Historically, the comfortable ones were the welder with thirty years’ experience and the foreman who “has a feel for steel”.

Sany decided steel could be felt by sensors too. Judging by the results, it was right.

### The basics

Sany Heavy Industry is one of the world’s largest construction machinery makers: excavators, concrete pumps, cranes, road and piling machinery. It was founded in Hunan province and is headquartered in Changsha. Since October 2025 its shares have traded in Hong Kong as well as Shanghai.

In 2025 revenue was about $12.5 billion (up 14.7%) and net profit about $1.18 billion (up 41%). The number that really explains the company is another one: 64% of revenue, or $7.83 billion, came from outside China. Sany sells in more than 180 countries and regions.

China’s own construction sector has not exactly been booming in recent years. Sany made up for it abroad. And you do not win foreign markets on price alone if your quality needs explaining.

### What a “lighthouse” is, and why a concrete pump qualifies

The Global Lighthouse Network is a World Economic Forum initiative launched with McKinsey. It selects factories that lead in applying digital technologies such as AI, big data analytics and automation. By September 2025 it counted 201 sites worldwide, a large share of them in China.

Sany has at least three.

**Beijing, 2021.** Its drilling and piling machinery plant became the world’s first lighthouse in heavy machinery. According to WEF figures cited by the company, productivity rose 185% and production lead time fell from 30 days to 7.

**Changsha, 2022.** “Factory No. 18”, which makes concrete pumps. 100,000 square metres, 1,540 sensors, 200 networked robots and more than 30 terabytes of data a day. According to the company, capacity rose 123%, productivity 98%, and unit manufacturing costs fell 29%. Robots with 3D vision cut and sort steel to within a millimetre, in 60% less time and with about half the scrap. The pump truck turntable, a part 3.7 metres long and weighing 2.6 tonnes, is gripped and welded here without fixtures, a first for the industry. And the plant makes 263 product variants.

**Shaoshan, 2024.** A Sany Renewable Energy site making wind turbine blades. A line almost 600 metres long, capacity of 3,000 blades a year, and the time to make a 100-metre blade cut from more than 40 hours to about 30.

Thirty terabytes a day. No foreman could feel that much steel in an entire career. But data without the foreman means little: someone still has to know which numbers to look at.

### It is not the robots. It is the rollout

The most underrated figure in the Factory No. 18 story is not a productivity percentage. According to Sany, the solutions proven in Changsha were extended to 45 other company plants, bringing the total of digitised sites to 46.

That is the difference between “we have a pilot project” and “this is how we work”. You can build one showcase plant for visiting officials. You cannot build forty-six. There are not that many delegations.

The second lesson is sequence. Changsha received about 500 million yuan of investment from 2020 and produced 55 in-house technical breakthroughs. The company first worked out which operations were most expensive and least stable, such as cutting and welding heavy parts, and only then brought in robots. Not the other way round.

You can automate chaos. You get automated chaos, only faster.

### Why it matters if you do not make excavators

Sany’s experience matters beyond machinery. It shows that deep digitisation is possible in low-volume production of heavy, varied products, which is exactly where most executives say in advance, “it won’t work for us, our products are complicated”.

Complicated products are not an argument against digitisation. They are an argument for starting with data rather than robots.

### Six questions for a visit to a digital factory

1.  Which operation did you automate first, and why that one?
2.  How long did it take from pilot to rollout across other sites?
3.  Who owns the line data: IT, production or a dedicated team?
4.  What does the welder replaced by a robot do now, and where?
5.  Which solutions did you buy, and which did you build?
6.  What didn’t work? It is the question asked least often, and the answer is worth the most.

### The bottom line

Heavy industry has long measured progress in tonnes. Sany showed it can also be measured in days: thirty versus seven.

The steel is as heavy as ever. Thinking about it has become lighter.
