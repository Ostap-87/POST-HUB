There are two ways to visit a factory in China. You can fly in, put on shoe covers, walk the line, take a photo by the logo and fly home feeling you have seen it all. Or you can do exactly the same, having decided in advance what you came for.

From the outside, the two trips look identical. The difference shows up six months later. One company has changed something at home. The other has framed the photo of the logo.

At Global Tech Tour we arrange closed visits to Chinese companies and call our participants researchers, not tourists. The label is not the point. Preparation is. Here is what we recommend before departure, on site and after you return.

### Step 1. Bring three to five questions, not “let’s see how they do it”

“Let’s see how they do it” is a mood, not a goal. Your hosts will give you the standard route, the corporate video and excellent tea. It will be pleasant and almost useless.

Good questions are specific. How long does a line changeover take, and how is it organised? Who can stop the line when a defect appears, the operator or the supervisor? How do they work with component suppliers? Which operations were automated most recently, and why those?

The sharper your questions, the more likely you are to be shown the parts of the plant that are not on the standard route.

### Step 2. Send the right people

The ideal delegation covers three roles: someone who makes decisions, someone who understands the process (an engineer, a production manager), and someone who will implement whatever you decide. Send only the first group, and you bring back impressions. Send only the second, and you bring back ideas nobody can approve.

Size matters too. Twenty people on a shop floor is a guided tour. Six to eight is a conversation.

### Step 3. What to send your hosts in advance

Chinese hosts prepare seriously and like to know exactly who is coming. Send, through your organiser, a short company profile in English (Chinese is even better), a list of participants with job titles, your questions, and any topics you would like to discuss separately.

Titles are not a formality. Hierarchy matters, and the seniority of your delegation shapes the seniority of the people who come out to meet you. Arrive as a CEO but describe the visit as “just a look around”, and you will be met by someone who will just show you around.

### Step 4. Apps, payments, paper

Install WeChat and set up an account before you travel: in China it is your messenger, your contact book and often your wallet. Set up Alipay or WeChat Pay in advance. Check current visa rules and timelines a month ahead; they have changed more than once in recent years, and the airport is a poor place to find out.

And yes, bring printed business cards. They are still offered and received with both hands, and actually read rather than pocketed. In some meetings the business card gets more careful attention than the contract.

### Step 5. On the shop floor

A few rules that save everyone’s nerves. Take photos only with permission, and ask before the phone is raised, not after; many workshops do not allow photography at all. Wear what you are given: helmets, coats, shoe covers. Touch nothing, even if it looks just like the machine you have at home. Stay with the group. Factories are big, and you have one interpreter.

Ask specific questions as you go. Chinese engineers are generous with specifics and politely vague about generalities. If you hear “that is a complicated question”, consider it a complete answer.

Do not be late for lunch. It often starts around 11:30, and it is part of the programme rather than a break. Sometimes it is the most informative part.

### Step 6. Negotiations: tea before terms

Do not expect decisions at the first meeting. The first meeting is an introduction, the second shows interest, and the real work starts after that. Chinese business is famously fast, but the speed has an order to it: trust first, then velocity.

Tea at the start of a conversation is not a ceremony for guests. It is how your hosts work out who you are. We suggest you use the time the same way.

### Five things to leave at home

1.  A 60-slide company presentation. Five slides will do.
2.  A plan to “sign something on this trip”.
3.  A gift you would be embarrassed to present with both hands.
4.  The conviction that “we have the same thing, only cheaper”.
5.  A bigger suitcase. It will come back full of the wrong things anyway.

### Step 7. The important part starts afterwards

The best debrief happens the same evening, before impressions shrink into “it was interesting”. Ask everyone to write down three things they saw and one thing they could try at home.

Within 48 hours, send your hosts a thank-you note with specifics: what stood out, which questions remain. A month later, ask your own team what you have actually tried. It is an awkward question. It is also the reason you made the trip.

The trip does not end at the airport. Only the souvenir shopping does.

### The bottom line

You cannot understand a Chinese factory from a slide deck, and one tour will not do it either. You understand it through the questions you bring and the answers you take home.

Do the preparation. The tea will be taken care of.
