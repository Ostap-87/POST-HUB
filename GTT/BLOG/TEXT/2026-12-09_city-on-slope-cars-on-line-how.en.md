When people say a city has “switched to electric cars”, they usually mean the old industry ended and a new one began. Chongqing did not get that memo. The new thing began. The old thing carried on.

That may be the single most important fact about Chongqing: it throws nothing away. Motorcycles, laptops, cars. None of them replaced the others; they stacked up like floors on a hillside. The city itself is built that way. In parts of Chongqing you can walk out of a seventh-floor window straight onto the street, because the street is on the seventh floor too.

### Number one, nowhere near the sea

According to Chinese business media citing city statistics, Chongqing produced 2.788 million vehicles in 2025, up 9.7%, the most of any Chinese city. New energy vehicles accounted for 1.296 million, up 36%. The automotive cluster is now worth more than 800 billion yuan.

In our piece on Hefei we described the city that leads China in EV output. Chongqing leads in total output. Two cities, two models, one country, and both feel crowded.

Chongqing sits on the upper Yangtze, thousands of kilometres from an ocean port, on hills where flat land is scarcer than any plant manager would like. A city that seemed unsuited to heavy industry made it its main business. Geography here is not destiny. It is a design brief.

### Motorcycles: moved on, didn’t sell up

The usual story goes: Chongqing used to make motorcycles, and now it makes cars. The first half is true. The second half is incomplete.

In 2025 Chongqing made 7.857 million motorcycles, 35.5% of China’s total of 22.1 million. The number keeps rising: 4.49 million in 2022, 6.57 million in 2024. In the first quarter of 2026, output jumped another 44.1%, the fastest growth of any industrial product in the city. The 2027 plan calls for more than 8 million bikes a year, 4 million of them electric, with 5 million for export.

The city did not trade the motorbike for a car. It bought the car and kept the bike parked outside. Meanwhile the bike found an export market.

Some industries retire. Others just move overseas.

### Laptops, and a train built for them

The second floor of the story is electronics. In 2011 six major notebook makers moved to Chongqing, including Quanta Computer, the world’s largest contract laptop manufacturer. CGTN estimates that Chongqing makes about a third of the world’s laptops. In the first 11 months of 2022 the city exported around 52 million of them, more than anywhere else in China, and laptops made up over 65% of its foreign trade.

Trucking that many laptops thousands of kilometres to the coast is slow. So in 2011 Chongqing launched the Yuxinou freight train, Chongqing to Xinjiang to Europe, China’s first regular container service to Europe. CGTN says it was designed specifically to carry laptops from local IT companies.

Usually you build a road and then work out what to send down it. Chongqing had the cargo first, then built a railway the length of a continent to carry it.

By July 2026, Xinhua reports, Chongqing had sent more than 20,000 China-Europe trains carrying goods worth 650 billion yuan. The range now covers over 10,000 product types. IT products account for more than 100,000 containers worth 190 billion yuan; finished vehicles for nearly 74 billion. In June 2026 a single train carried 182 Chongqing-built cars. A southern corridor running since July 2025 cuts at least 10 days off the trip compared with “middle corridor” routes.

The laptop laid the track. The car followed.

### Changan, Seres and a deal with Huawei

The top floor is cars, with two main characters.

The first is Changan, a large state-owned car group headquartered in Chongqing. In 2025 it sold 2.913 million vehicles, 1.11 million of them new energy vehicles, a segment that grew 51.1%.

The second is Seres, a company most people outside China first heard of through its alliance with Huawei and the AITO brand. In 2025 Seres sold 472,300 new energy vehicles.

Their arrangement with Huawei is instructive. Huawei says plainly that it does not make cars; it supplies intelligent connected vehicle technology and helps carmakers design and sell vehicles. In July 2024 Seres announced it would buy 919 trademarks, including the AITO series, and 44 design patents from Huawei for 2.5 billion yuan. A same-day appraisal valued the assets at 10.23 billion. Huawei continues to work with Seres on designing and marketing AITO models.

So a tech company gave a car its brains and its name, then sold the name to the carmaker while keeping the brains on a long-term arrangement. Translated into any industry: a brand can belong to one company while its meaning belongs to another. Worth remembering before signing a partnership with a big tech player.

### Chongqing’s three floors

1.  Ground floor: motorcycles. Still here, still growing, now exporting.
2.  Middle floor: laptops. A third of the world, with their own train to Europe.
3.  Top floor: electric cars. China’s number one car-making city.
4.  The lift between floors: the railway. Without it, none of the floors would be habitable.

### What it means for business

Chongqing undermines two popular ideas.

The first is that new industries displace old ones. Here the old industry stayed, and its suppliers, metalworking skills, engineers and habits of mass production fed the new one. The car cluster did not grow on empty ground; it grew on decades of experience with engines and metal.

The second is that you cannot build an export economy without a coastline. Chongqing showed that logistics can be designed around cargo, as long as there is enough of it. It started with laptops and moved on to cars.

For companies working with Chinese carmakers or suppliers, Chongqing is a living textbook: an old state-owned group, a private maker allied with a tech giant, an electronics base and a railway that carries it all out. On Global Tech Tour business expeditions, we value cities like this precisely because one company cannot explain them. You have to take them floor by floor.

Chongqing is often called the mountain city. True, but incomplete. It is the city where a motorbike, a laptop and an electric car each took their turn up the hill, and nobody gave way.
