Western negotiations often work like a staircase: agree on one point, step up to the next. Chinese negotiations look more like a banquet table with a turntable in the middle. Every issue sits on the table at once, and you never know which one will rotate towards you next.

That explains most misunderstandings. The foreign side believes the price was agreed on Tuesday. The Chinese side believes the price was discussed on Tuesday and will be agreed together with everything else, once everything else is agreed.

Nothing is agreed until everything is agreed.

This is not a trick. It is a way of thinking. Back in 2003, John Graham and Mark Lam described it in Harvard Business Review as holistic thinking, alongside other key concepts of Chinese negotiation: connections, status, harmony, thrift, face and endurance. China has changed beyond recognition since then. Those words have not.

Here is the usual path from first meeting to contract, as our business [expedition](/en/expeditions) participants see it.

### Stage 1. Introduction: they assess you before your offer

The first meeting is almost never about the deal. It is a test of who you are, how serious you are and who they are dealing with. That is why the seniority of your delegation matters. If you send a middle manager and they field a vice-president, it is awkward for everyone. The reverse is awkward too.

Chinese business culture is hierarchical. The senior person speaks, juniors add detail when asked. If your whole team talks at once and interrupts each other, your hosts will not conclude that you are refreshingly flat. They will conclude that nobody knows who decides.

The goal of the first meeting is not to pitch the product but to come across as a reliable partner. A 60-slide deck will be read. An impression will be remembered.

### Stage 2. Guanxi: relationships as a working tool

Guanxi is usually translated as “connections”, which undersells it. It is social capital built on long-term reciprocity: I helped you, and you remember that I did. Ignoring reciprocity is seen not merely as rude but as unethical.

That is why intermediaries matter. Someone who knows both sides and can introduce you saves months. Ideally, the same person eases tension when talks stall, because they can say what is awkward to say directly.

Relationships are built outside the meeting room too: dinners, joint plant visits, a return visit to your own country. None of this is protocol. It is part of the deal. A contract without a relationship can still be signed in China. It will just be performed without much enthusiasm.

### Stage 3. Bargaining: the opening price is an invitation

Your Chinese counterpart will bargain and expects you to do the same. The opening price is a starting position, not a final offer. Silence is an argument too. Silence after your proposal does not mean you were misunderstood. It means you were understood perfectly, and they are waiting to see whether you will say something else. Usually people do, at their own expense.

A useful technique is to ask not “why is it so expensive” but what the price is made of: delivery terms, payment schedule, warranty, scope, training. Each is a separate lever, and a concession on one may be worth more than a discount.

### Stage 4. “We need to discuss it with management”

You will hear this phrase. It can mean three things. First, exactly what it says: the person at the table cannot decide and genuinely needs approval. Second, a polite “no”. A direct refusal puts the other side in an awkward position and risks their face, so “no” often sounds like “that is a complicated question”, “we will study it” or “we need to discuss it”. Third, a pause for bargaining: you are being given time to consider improving your offer.

How do you tell which one it was? By what happens next. Follow-up questions within a week: the first. Silence and polite non-answers: the second. A counter-proposal: the third.

In China, “yes” often means “I hear you”. “No” means something went wrong back at the first meeting.

### Stage 5. Face: what never to do

Mianzi, or face, is a person’s reputation and standing in the eyes of others. You can cost someone face by correcting them in public, embarrassing them or losing your own temper. A raised voice at the table costs both sides face at once.

The rules follow. Do not argue in front of your counterpart’s subordinates. Point out mistakes privately or through an intermediary. Keep your composure, however tempting it is not to. In Chinese negotiations the winner is not the loudest voice but the one whose polite expression lasts longest. Endurance is valued as highly as talent, and Chinese teams typically prepare more thoroughly and expect longer bargaining.

### Stage 6. The contract: the chop outranks the signature

When it comes to paper, remember the company chop. A company seal on a contract shows that the company itself authorised it, not only the individual who signed, so the company cannot later claim the signatory lacked authority.

China lawyers suggest that, for routine contracts, you at least ask for the signatory’s title and name in Chinese and English, a copy of their business card and a copy of the company’s business licence. The main risks are not forged seals but a signatory without authority, or a company that does not exist in the form presented to you. The Chinese company name in the contract should match the licence. In characters, not in transliteration.

A contract signed in China is not the finish line. It is the start of performance, and the relationship matters as much after signing as before.

### Five signs your negotiations are going well

1.  You were invited to dinner and not seated by the door.
2.  At the second meeting, someone more senior than last time joined from their side.
3.  They ask you about delivery schedules, not about the weather back home.
4.  They offer a visit to another plant that was not on the programme.
5.  They have stopped telling you everything is wonderful.

### The bottom line

You cannot close a deal with a Chinese company in one trip. But in one trip you can make a deal possible. That is what business expeditions are for: to see a partner on their own ground, understand who makes decisions, and start building the guanxi without which a contract is just paper.

Negotiations in China begin long before the signing and do not end after it. Like tea, they are not meant to be gulped.
