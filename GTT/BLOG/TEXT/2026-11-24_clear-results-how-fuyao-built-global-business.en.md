Car glass is a product people notice on only two occasions: when it cracks and when they have to pay for it. The rest of the time we look straight through it. Literally.

On this invisible product, Fuyao built one of China’s most successful global businesses, and along the way became the subject of an Oscar-winning film. It is not often that someone makes a movie about the glass rather than about what is behind it.

### The basics

Cao Dewang founded Fuyao Glass in 1987 in Fuqing, Fujian province. Today it describes itself as the world’s largest automotive glass maker; estimates of its global share vary between sources, but they all put it at a third or more. In October 2025 the founder handed the chairmanship to his son, Cao Hui, and became honorary chairman.

In 2025 revenue reached 45.79 billion yuan (up 16.65%) and net profit 9.31 billion yuan (up 24.2%), both company records. Overseas revenue was 20.86 billion yuan, almost 46% of the total. Fuyao has plants in the United States, Germany, Russia and Morocco.

A net margin above 20%, on glass. Not chips, not software. Glass, which is made from sand.

### Ohio: a factory where a factory used to be

In January 2014 Fuyao announced it was buying part of a former General Motors assembly plant in Moraine, Ohio. GM had closed it in 2008, at the height of the crisis, and the region felt the loss badly.

Fuyao promised jobs and investment, and delivered. Full-scale production started in October 2016; by then, according to the company, investment in its US business had reached about $1 billion. Fuyao later added a float glass plant in Decatur, Illinois, producing the raw material for its American windscreens itself. In 2025 it announced a further $400 million expansion of the Illinois plant.

According to Yicai, the Moraine site now employs more than 2,500 local workers and is described as the largest automotive glass plant in the world.

### American Factory: when cultures meet on the shop floor

In 2019 the documentary American Factory, about Fuyao’s Ohio plant, was released and went on to win the Oscar for Best Documentary Feature. It is neither an advert nor an exposé. It simply shows how hard it is for a Chinese company, Chinese managers and American workers to understand one another.

Different attitudes to overtime, to safety, to unions, to how a boss speaks and how people answer back. In 2017 workers voted on joining the UAW: 886 against, 441 in favour.

The numbers in the reports came together faster than the people on the floor. That may be the film’s main lesson for any company going abroad.

### The road to profit

The US business lost money in its early years. In 2017, according to the Dayton Daily News, it turned its first profit: about $750,000 on revenue of nearly $319 million. Modest, but a turning point. In 2018 net profit was already around $24.5 million.

It kept growing. According to Yicai, in 2024 the US market delivered about $88 million in net profit on revenue of roughly $887 million. In 2025 the US subsidiaries reported 7.92 billion yuan in revenue and 884 million yuan in net profit.

Anyone who called the Ohio plant a mistake in 2016 was right until 2017.

### What helped with tariffs

When the US began raising tariffs on Chinese goods, Fuyao already had its answer in place. According to Cao Dewang, around 70% of what the company sells to US customers is made in the US. An import tariff does little to a company that does not import.

Localisation is not a goodwill gesture. It is insurance, best bought early, while it is still cheap.

### What Fuyao’s story teaches

**Follow the customer.** Fuyao began supplying GM in 2001 and later came to Ohio, close to the car plants. Glass is heavy and fragile, and shipping it across an ocean is expensive. A plant next to the customer beats the most efficient plant far away.

**Control the chain.** Its own float glass in Illinois means independence from raw material suppliers in a foreign country.

**Think in decades.** Fuyao’s US business did not pay off in one year or two. Companies that expect quick results abroad rarely last long enough to see any.

**Do not underestimate culture.** Technology can be transferred in a year. The relationship between a manager and a worker cannot.

### Five questions before entering a foreign market

1.  Where is your customer, and what does it cost to get your product to them?
2.  Which part of the supply chain can you control in the new location?
3.  How many years are you prepared to lose money, and does your board agree?
4.  Which of your managers genuinely understands local employees?
5.  Are you ready for someone to make a film about you? Not necessarily a flattering one.

### The bottom line

Good glass is glass you do not notice. Good globalisation may be the same: the moment an Ohio plant stops being “a Chinese factory in America” and becomes simply a factory that makes glass and pays wages.

It took Fuyao ten years and one Oscar. That is faster than it sounds.
