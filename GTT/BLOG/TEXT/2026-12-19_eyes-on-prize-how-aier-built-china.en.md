We like to think healthcare and retail are different worlds. One heals, the other sells. One has the Hippocratic Oath, the other has sales per square foot.

China’s Aier Eye Hospital has spent twenty years blurring that line. The easiest way to explain how it works is in retail language: format, location, standards, new openings, like-for-like growth. The shelves are operating theatres, and the person at the till is a surgeon.

A disclaimer up front: I am not an ophthalmologist, and nothing below is advice about your eyesight. It is about the health of a business model, which is a different kind of vision.

### From one hospital to six hundred-plus sites

Aier was founded by Chen Bang. Its first specialist eye hospital opened in Changsha, capital of Hunan province, in the early 2000s. In 2009 it listed in Shenzhen, one of the first private hospital groups on a mainland Chinese exchange. At listing it had 19 hospitals.

By the end of 2025, according to its annual report, Aier ran 391 hospitals and 272 outpatient clinics in China, plus 179 eye centres and clinics abroad, across Hong Kong, Europe, the United States and Southeast Asia. In that year it handled 18.9 million outpatient visits and 1.68 million operations.

Revenue for 2025 was 22.35 billion yuan, up 6.5%.

Nineteen hospitals became six hundred-plus sites. At that point you are no longer a clinic. You are a map.

### Model one: a pyramid, not a scatter

Aier’s central idea is a tiered network. At the top sit large hospitals with research capacity and complex surgery. Below them, hospitals in provincial capitals. Below those, prefecture cities, then counties. Difficult patients move up the ladder; doctors and protocols move down.

Retailers would call this “flagship plus neighbourhood stores”. The flagship builds the name, the neighbourhood stores bring the traffic. The difference is that a shopper chooses where to go, while a patient is referred. When the referral stays inside your own network, you have a closed loop: someone who walks into a county clinic for an eye test can end up in surgery at a provincial hospital without ever meeting a competitor.

A network’s strength is not the number of dots. It is the ladder between them.

### Model two: the doctor as co-owner

The second idea was a partner programme for doctors, announced in April 2014. Key doctors and managers could invest in new hospitals alongside the company. According to Chinese business press at the time, partners held 20–30% of a new hospital. Once the hospital became steadily profitable, Aier bought their stakes at a fair price, in cash or shares.

Anyone who has opened a branch office understands the logic. A new site loses money for its first years, and the real asset of a medical network is not the building or the laser. It is the surgeon patients travel to see. Make that surgeon a co-owner, and he stops being an employee of the site and starts behaving like its landlord. Landlords do not leave for a 20% pay rise. They already have their 20%.

Retailers hire staff. Hospital chains have to take on partners.

### Model three: an incubator off the balance sheet

The third piece is the most financial and, as it turned out, the most controversial. From 2014 Aier set up industry funds with outside investors, putting in a small share itself. According to Leida Finance, republished by 36Kr in May 2026, the company invested around 2.1 billion yuan in these funds and brought in more than 21.9 billion yuan of outside money. At the end of 2023, the funds held 311 medical institutions.

The mechanics: a fund builds or buys a clinic, the clinic goes through its loss-making years outside the listed company, and when it turns profitable Aier buys it in. Elegant. Shareholders only ever meet the grown-up, profitable hospitals. The teething happens somewhere off stage.

The elegance comes with an invoice. Buying mature clinics costs more than their book value, and the difference piles up as goodwill. By the end of the first quarter of 2026 Aier’s goodwill was close to 12 billion yuan, and some of it has already been written down.

A deferred loss is still a loss. It just has better manners.

### Why growth slowed

For years Aier was a market darling. In July 2021 its market value approached 400 billion yuan and the shares traded at more than 100 times earnings. Then they fell roughly 70% from the peak.

The 2025 report shows why. Revenue growth slowed. Net profit fell 8.9% to 3.24 billion yuan, which 36Kr describes as the first annual decline since the IPO. Gross margin dropped to its lowest level since 2019. Cataract revenue, a core line, slipped slightly. Competition, price cuts and rising costs all played a part.

Then came a tax item: in May 2026, after a self-inspection, the company disclosed back taxes and late fees totalling about 524 million yuan.

It is a classic chain story: the network grew faster than its ability to manage itself. Keeping six hundred sites on one standard is hard. Harder still when the standard is a surgeon’s hands rather than a sauce recipe.

### Going abroad: buy, don’t build

Abroad, Aier does the opposite of what it does at home: instead of building from scratch, it buys established chains. It entered Hong Kong in 2015, took control of Spain’s Clínica Baviera, Europe’s largest eye-clinic chain, in 2017, and bought a clinic in the US the same year. In 2019 it became the majority shareholder of Singapore’s ISEC (SEC Healthcare), which operates in Singapore, Malaysia and Myanmar. Its subsidiaries now include operations in Spain, Germany, Italy, the UK, the US, Singapore, Malaysia and elsewhere.

Overseas revenue grew 16.5% in 2025 to 3.06 billion yuan, about 13.7% of the total. In spring 2026 the company filed to list in Hong Kong, presumably because money for international expansion is easier to raise where international investors are watching.

At home you know how to build a clinic from zero. In a foreign country it is easier to buy one people already trust. Patient trust cannot be imported. It can only be acquired, together with the sign over the door.

### Five questions Aier asks of any chain business

1.  Is there a ladder between your sites, or just many identical sites?
2.  Who is your “surgeon”, the person without whom a site empties, and do they share in the result?
3.  Where do new branches spend their loss-making years: in your accounts or in your optimistic slide decks?
4.  What happens to margins if your biggest buyer or your regulator cuts the price one day?
5.  How many sites can you open before the standard becomes a suggestion?

### The bottom line

Aier’s story is useful not because you can copy it. You cannot: it grew on an enormous market where short-sightedness, quite literally, became one of the few truly mass consumer categories. It is useful because it shows the full life cycle of a network model: standard, ladder, partnership, financial ingenuity, slowdown, and the bill for the ingenuity.

Medical companies that come to China on business [expeditions](/en/expeditions) usually look at equipment and hospitals. It is worth looking at how the hospitals are wired together, too. Equipment can be bought. The wiring has to be designed.

Vision is a long game. Even for the people who sell it.
