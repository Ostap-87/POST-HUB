Laos has no coastline. For a long time it had no real railway either: a few kilometres of track near the Thai border, and that was it. Freight went by truck over mountain roads, slowly and expensively, and “logistics” in Laos mostly meant patience.

In December 2021 a 1,035 km railway opened between Kunming and Vientiane. A country without a sea got something almost as good: tracks to China.

Here is what came of it. The numbers are more interesting than any brochure.

### What it carries

Chinese rail operators say the line had carried 76.8 million tonnes of freight by February 2026 and more than 90 million by August. Cross-border freight passed 21 million tonnes by August 2026.

More than 80,000 freight trains have run, over 23,000 of them international, with up to 23 cross-border trains on peak days. The maximum load per train has risen from 2,000 to 2,800 tonnes.

The most telling figure is the product list. At launch the line carried just over ten types of goods; now it carries more than 4,000, and its network reaches 19 countries and regions. Trade carried on the line in the first half of 2026 was worth RMB 17.2 billion, up about a third on a year earlier.

First, rails carry what would have moved anyway. Then they carry what nobody shipped before. That second stage is called trade.

### Durian as a benchmark

If you want to see what has changed, look at durian. China consumes more than 90% of the world’s durian, and durian does not wait: it ripens, smells and spoils on its own schedule.

In July 2024 the Lao news agency reported that more than 100,000 tonnes of fruit had moved on the line that year, up about 120%. A batch of Thai durian travelled from Vientiane South station to Kunming in 29 hours, and border clearance had been cut to five hours or less.

The first freight train from Thailand crossed Laos into China in February 2023. Thai and Vietnamese fruit now have one more route to the Chinese market. At Guangzhou’s wholesale market, reference durian prices in August 2026 were noticeably lower than a year earlier, which Chinese media attribute to rising imports and new transport links.

Fruit delivery is the best test of any logistics chain. Fruit cannot wait, and it does not care what the slide deck says.

### Who travels

By February 2026 the line had carried 66.1 million passenger trips, 12.2 million of them on the Lao section. International passenger service between Kunming and Vientiane started in April 2023; the cross-border journey takes about eleven and a half hours, with stops in Vang Vieng and Luang Prabang.

For Laos this is more than tourism. People come to look around and stay to do business: shops, warehouses, restaurants, representative offices. The train brings tourists, and it also brings future tenants.

### The price tag

Now the part mentioned less often. The railway cost about $6 billion. Laos holds a minority stake, and much of the financing came as Chinese loans. Australia’s Lowy Institute estimates that Laos’s public and publicly guaranteed debt exceeds 100% of GDP, with China as the largest creditor and repayments repeatedly deferred.

That does not make the railway a mistake. It makes it an expensive purchase on a long instalment plan. The benefits are growing, but faster for those who ship along it than for those who pay for it. The key question for Laos is whether it can earn from transit rather than simply host it.

### The four ages of a new railway

1.  Opening day. Everyone takes a photo with the train.
2.  Year one. Sceptics count empty wagons.
3.  Year three. Sceptics count full wagons and complain they carry the wrong things.
4.  Year five. Warehouses line the tracks, and there is no one left to argue with.

### What it means for trade in Indochina

First, Yunnan is becoming China’s land gateway to Southeast Asia, with Kunming as the hub where freight from Laos, Thailand and beyond converges.

Second, routes now compete differently. Goods from Thailand to southern China used to go by sea or by truck over the mountains. There is now a rail option, especially attractive for perishables and for cargo that needs a predictable delivery date.

Third, China gains most. It has a stable channel south for its goods and north for raw materials and food. Thailand and Vietnam have another road to the region’s largest market. Laos has transit, debt and a chance.
