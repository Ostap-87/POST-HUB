Every family business has a document more important than its charter: the will. It is never shown to investors, never discussed by the board, and always left for later. Later always arrives unexpectedly.

The story of Wahaha, the drinks maker whose sweet AD calcium milk in tiny bottles is a childhood memory for most of China, is a fine textbook on the subject. It is written as a TV drama, though, and so far it has more episodes than chapters.

A caveat up front: we report only what open sources confirm, and the story is still unfolding. Everything below reflects the situation in autumn 2026.

### The founder who was the company

Zong Qinghou founded Wahaha in Hangzhou in 1987, starting from a school shop. Over the next three decades it became one of China’s biggest beverage makers, survived a bruising dispute with France’s Danone that ended in 2009, and made Zong the country’s richest man more than once.

By all accounts he ran it personally and closely. His signature was required on papers that a department head would sign elsewhere. That works as long as the chairman is there. Zong Qinghou died in February 2024, aged 79.

A company built around one person lasts exactly as long as that person.

### Who owns Wahaha

This is where it gets interesting. Wahaha Group is not a straightforward family company. According to 36Kr, 46% belongs to the state through Hangzhou’s Shangcheng district, 29.4% to the founder’s estate, inherited by his daughter Kelly Zong Fuli, and 24.6% to employees through an internal shareholders’ association.

Meanwhile much of the production and sales runs through companies that Zong Fuli controls herself, notably Hongsheng Group. The founder built the business both inside the group and alongside it.

As long as everyone faces the same way, the structure is invisible. When they turn in different directions, it becomes the whole story.

### An inheritance, in instalments

Zong Fuli returned from the United States in 2004 and spent years being groomed as successor. In 2021 she became vice chair and general manager.

Then things moved fast. In July 2024, a few months after her father’s death, she resigned following a clash with other shareholders, and about a week later, after “friendly negotiations”, she was back. By August 2024 she was chair, general manager and legal representative.

In February 2025, according to 36Kr, there was an attempt to transfer 387 Wahaha trademarks to a company 51% owned by Zong Fuli. The state shareholder blocked it. Her companies registered new brand names in parallel.

In summer 2025 the courts joined in. Three people who say they are Zong Qinghou’s children filed suits in Hong Kong and Hangzhou, claiming rights to a US$2.1 billion family trust and to the founder’s stake. In September 2025, Caixin reports, Hong Kong’s High Court dismissed Zong Fuli’s appeal and kept the freeze on the trust funds in place.

On 12 September 2025 Zong Fuli stepped down as chair again, and Hongsheng announced that from 2026 it would sell drinks under a new brand. The new brand lasted 41 days. On 23 October Hongsheng said it would keep using Wahaha in 2026; distributor renewals, Chinese media reported, were tied to the old name.

In March 2026, according to Caixin, Zong Fuli reached a compromise with the state shareholder to buy its 46% stake for more than 2 billion yuan, subject to approvals. Later that month Hongsheng paused nearly all its plants for a week, except bottled-water lines. Explanations in the press ranged from excess inventory to the control reshuffle.

Forty-one days is not a rebrand. It is a test of who really owns a brand: the lawyer, the shareholder or the shopper.

### Meanwhile, on the shelves

The market did not wait for the shareholders. According to 36Kr, Wahaha’s sales have hovered around 50 billion yuan since 2014, while rivals such as Nongfu Spring and Genki Forest kept growing. Nielsen estimates cited by 36Kr show AD milk sales in East China down 37% in the first half of 2025 and Wahaha’s bottled-water share falling from 18% to 12%. The distributor network, by the same account, may have roughly halved.

Shoppers do not read Hong Kong court rulings. They just reach for the next bottle on the shelf.

### Six documents founders leave for later

1.  The will.
2.  A shareholder agreement on who decides when not everyone agrees.
3.  A clear record of who owns the trademark.
4.  Contracts between the group and the family’s “neighbouring” companies.
5.  A handover plan for the day the founder does not come in.
6.  A letter to distributors for that same day. Nobody writes this one. They should.

### Lessons for family companies

We are not in a position to judge who is right; that is for the courts and the parties. But some lessons hold whatever the ending.

A brand should belong to one owner, in writing. When the trademark sits in one entity, production in another and distributor loyalty in a third, an inheritance becomes a negotiation.

A successor is a person and a procedure. Zong Fuli was prepared for two decades. Preparing the person did not replace preparing the rules for when shareholders disagree.

Mixed ownership demands discipline. A state co-owner is a powerful ally against a foreign partner, as the Danone dispute showed. In a family dispute, it has interests of its own.

Conflict costs money even for the winner. Lost sales and departing distributors are paid for by every side at once.

Good succession is boring. No headlines, no 41-day brands, no news. Its main symptom is that nobody writes about it.

### Why foreign partners should care

Many Chinese manufacturers, especially mid-sized ones, are first-generation family firms whose founders started in the 1980s and 1990s. For them succession is no longer theoretical, and for a supplier, distributor or buyer it is a risk that never appears on a price list.

At meetings in China, including on Global Tech Tour expeditions, we encourage participants to ask not only about capacity and certificates but about governance: who signs, who inherits, who is number two. It is a delicate question. The answer is worth more than any factory tour.

Zong Qinghou spent nearly forty years building the company. Sorting out how to divide it will take longer than anyone involved would like.

And AD milk is still on the shelves. A small bottle from childhood that does not yet know who it belongs to.
