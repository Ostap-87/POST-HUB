In some countries students look for a job after graduating. In China only the very self-assured do that. Everyone else starts a year before their degree, and the best start eighteen months ahead, through an internship.

Chinese campus recruitment, 校招, is not a careers fair in the university gym. It is a well-oiled machine with its own calendar, seasons and paperwork. In sheer scale it is closer to national service, only competitive and with lunch paid for by the employer.

### The scale: 12.7 million

According to China’s Ministry of Education, Chinese universities produced 12.7 million graduates in 2026, a record that is broken almost every year.

To picture it: that is more than the entire population of Greater London, all graduating at once and heading for interviews. Not to one company, but to twenty.

On the other side is a tough labour market. According to the National Bureau of Statistics, unemployment among urban 16–24-year-olds, excluding students, was 17.9% in July 2026 and rose to 18.9% in August, a one-year high. The summer spike comes every year as a new class arrives, but the level stays high.

When twelve million people look for work at the same time, it is not the person who finds the job. It is the calendar.

### The calendar: two seasons and an internship

The main season is autumn, 秋招, aimed at students who will graduate the following summer. Big companies open it in August: in 2026 ByteDance and JD.com launched hiring for the class of 2027 on 3 August, Alibaba on 4 August and Ant Group on 10 August. The peak runs through September and October, known as “golden September, silver October”. By November most big employers have made their offers.

Spring, 春招, is the second chance, for students who did not land a job in autumn and companies that did not fill their places. Meituan, for instance, was reported to be topping up its 2026 intake to 6,000 graduates in spring 2026.

The most interesting part happens between the seasons: internships designed to convert into full-time jobs, 实习转正. In March 2026 ByteDance announced it would recruit more than 7,000 interns worldwide from the class of 2027, its largest such programme ever, and said more than half were expected to receive offers. Tencent announced the same day that it would take more than 10,000 interns graduating between September 2026 and December 2027, with technical roles up 36% and product roles up 39%. Meituan opened more than 3,000 such places for the class of 2027.

An internship in China is not work experience. It is a three-month job interview, assessed daily.

For students, that means the fight for a job begins a year before the final year. For companies, it means seeing a person at work before signing a contract.

### The paperwork: a three-way agreement

The system has a paperwork quirk too. A graduate who accepts an offer usually signs a tripartite agreement, 三方协议, between student, employer and university. The university cares because graduate employment is one of the metrics it is judged on. The employer cares because a student who has signed is far less likely to change their mind.

In this arrangement the university is not just a place of study but a party to the deal: the dean’s office with its stamp is a third participant in a salary negotiation that never mentions salary.

### AI is reshaping the roles

The big theme of 2026 is artificial intelligence, and companies are competing to announce AI hiring. Ant Group reportedly said 80% of its openings for the class of 2027 are technical, and more than 80% of those are AI-related. ByteDance cites a sharply higher share of AI roles and “industry-leading” pay for AI talent. Bilibili launched a dedicated AI talent programme.

The other side is discussed too: AI is making some entry-level roles that once went to graduates unnecessary. Demand is shifting from “a graduate” to “a graduate who can work with models”. The average candidate gets squeezed: not strong enough for the top AI roles, while the simpler roles shrink.

### A Chinese student’s calendar, the honest version

1.  Year one: “Far too early to think about jobs.”
2.  Year two: “Wait, is everyone already interning?”
3.  Year three: an internship, a second internship, an AI course, and civil-service exam prep, just in case.
4.  The summer before final year: the intern-to-hire internship.
5.  Final-year autumn: twenty applications, eight online tests, five interviews, one offer.
6.  Spring: a signed tripartite agreement, and the parents’ first question: “Why not the civil service?”

### What employers can borrow

An employer hiring dozens of graduates rather than thousands does not need China’s scale. But several principles travel.

Start earlier. If you recruit graduates in spring, the best were hired by whoever started in autumn, and the very best by whoever took them on as interns the year before.

Use internships as selection. A paid internship with a clear chance of a permanent job is the fairest way to assess someone and the most persuasive way to keep them. Clarity is the key: “more than half will get an offer” motivates far more than “we’ll see”.

Treat universities as partners. Not one careers fair a year but a programme: coursework on your real problems, placements, agreements on target graduates.

Be honest about requirements. If you need someone who can work with AI tools, say so in the job ad and test for it. That is exactly how Chinese companies have rebuilt their hiring over the past two years.

These things are worth seeing from the inside: how a big company’s campus recruitment team works, how selection runs, what an intern sees in week one. On Global Tech Tour business expeditions and corporate training programmes, we encourage participants to raise this topic with Chinese companies too. People decide more than machines do.

### The bottom line

Chinese campus hiring is a machine that processes more than ten million futures a year. Much of it is harsh: competition, tests, high youth unemployment, family pressure. But it also has what many markets lack: a predictable calendar to plan around, and the internship as a bridge between study and work.

A graduate is looking for a first job. A good company is looking for that person’s second, third and tenth.

Twelve million graduates means twelve million first days at work. The lucky ones will be greeted not with a security pass, but with a mentor.
