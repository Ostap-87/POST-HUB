For an ordinary business trip you pack an ordinary suitcase: a suit, shirts, and a charger you will forget anyway. For China you need a second suitcase, a digital one. It weighs nothing, but forget it and you may as well not unpack the first. Without a properly set-up phone in China you cannot book a taxi, pay for a coffee or find the factory that is “just past the bridge”.

Cash is still accepted. It is simply looked at the way people elsewhere look at a chequebook: with respect for its age.

Here is what we at Global Tech Tour ask expedition participants to pack into their phones a week or two before departure. Not at the airport. At the airport, as you will discover, half the websites you need will no longer load.

### 1. WeChat: messenger, business card and pass

[WeChat](/en/companies/wechat) is not just a messaging app in China. It is your business card (at meetings, people increasingly scan each other’s QR codes instead of exchanging cards), your chat with partners, your wallet and, through mini-programs, the restaurant menu, ticket office and even the arrival card at the border.

Install it, register, verify your number and complete identity checks if prompted. New foreign accounts sometimes need confirmation from an existing user, so do not leave this until the night before you fly. Set up WeChat Pay at home too: it involves a passport check, and that does not always pass on the first try.

A tip from experience: create a WeChat group for the delegation before the trip. Once in China, it will be the one channel you know works.

### 2. Alipay: the wallet that speaks foreign card

In recent years, Alipay and WeChat Pay have made life much easier for visitors by accepting international cards such as Visa and Mastercard. According to travel guides, Alipay charges no fee on foreign-card payments of 200 yuan or less and 3% above that. Your own bank may add a currency conversion fee.

Some cards work better than others, and some issuers block transactions they find unusual. Tell your bank you are travelling, check your limits, and run a small test payment before you leave. Then bring a backup card and some yuan in cash.

In China, a single payment method is not a payment method. It is a hope.

### 3. Connectivity: where the internet you know ends

Many familiar services do not work in mainland China: Google Search and Gmail, YouTube, WhatsApp, Telegram, Instagram, ChatGPT and others. This is not a glitch. It is the architecture.

You have three ways to stay connected. International roaming routes traffic through your home network, so familiar apps usually work; check the price first. A travel eSIM often routes traffic through gateways outside the mainland with the same effect; buy and install it before departure, because the seller’s website may not load once you land. A local SIM card is cheap and quick, but it plays by local rules.

And keep your home number active. That is where your bank’s verification codes go. Losing them abroad is like losing your house key while standing at your own front door.

### 4. Maps and taxis: Google Maps will not help

Google Maps is blocked in China and, even via roaming, its data does not always match reality. The main local maps are Amap (Gaode) and [Baidu](/en/companies/baidu) Maps. In January 2025, Amap launched an English version with English labels, lane-level guidance and even countdowns to the next traffic-light change.

For taxis, use DiDi, which has an English interface and also runs as a mini-program inside Alipay and WeChat. You pay in the same app.

One tip that sounds obvious and saves an hour: save the addresses of your hotel and every factory on the programme in Chinese, as screenshots. Your driver will read the characters faster than your English, and certainly faster than your Mandarin.

### 5. Translation and the rest

Install an offline translator with a Chinese dictionary downloaded in advance. The camera mode will help with menus, signs and, frankly, souvenir packaging. For trains, use the 12306 website or app in English. For notes, use something that syncs without Google, or your trip report will be stuck in a cloud you can only reach from home.

### Final check

1.  WeChat installed, registered, payment linked, delegation group created.
2.  Alipay installed, passport verified, card linked and tested with a small purchase.
3.  Bank informed, limits known.
4.  Roaming or eSIM set up and tested before departure.
5.  Amap, DiDi, translator with offline dictionary.
6.  Hotel and factory addresses in Chinese, as screenshots.
7.  A power bank. In China you do everything on your phone, and the battery behaves accordingly.
8.  Some cash in yuan, in case everything above decides to take a day off at once.

### The bottom line

We take business delegations to China, and we have watched the first day of a trip change. It used to start at a currency exchange. Now it starts with a phone that has everything installed. Or does not, in which case it starts with a search for Wi-Fi.

You can go to China without a digital suitcase. You will just be the tourist who keeps asking for directions, not the researcher who came for answers.
