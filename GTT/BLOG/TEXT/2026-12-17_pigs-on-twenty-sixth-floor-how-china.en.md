In picture books, a pig lives in a sty. There’s a hen house next door, a cow shed behind and a field in the distance, all on one double page. If the picture book were drawn in Hubei province in 2026, the page would have to be turned on its side. And it would need a lift.

In the city of Ezhou stand two 26-storey towers. From outside they look like ordinary apartment blocks; Chinese social media nicknamed them the “Bajie Buildings”, after Zhu Bajie, the pig from *Journey to the West*. The residents are, in fact, pigs. About 1.2 million a year.

### Why the pigs moved upstairs

Pork in China is not just meat. It is the country’s main meat and part of the consumer price index that economists and officials watch closely. Official data show China produced about 57.94 million tonnes of pork in 2023.

In August 2018 African swine fever was detected in China. It is harmless to people, lethal to pigs, and has no vaccine approved in China. Herds shrank sharply, pork prices jumped, and the state began pushing the industry hard towards large industrial farms with strict biosecurity.

A backyard with a dozen pigs cannot be protected the way a factory can: one entrance, filtered air, controlled movement of people and vehicles. A large complex can. And where land is scarce, as it always is in China, the complex grows upwards.

A line for the vet: biosecurity is when it’s harder to see the pig than to see the CEO.

### Ezhou: a farm that looks like a factory

The Ezhou complex was built by Zhongxin Kaiwei Modern Animal Husbandry, which began moving in its first sows in late 2022. According to the Irish farm publication Agriland, the project cost about €570 million, with two buildings of roughly 400,000 square metres each.

What’s inside, according to public descriptions: each floor runs as a separate farm covering the full cycle from farrowing to finishing. Temperature, humidity and ventilation are controlled remotely, and vets monitor operations around the clock. Feeding is automated. Manure was planned to feed an on-site anaerobic digester producing up to 120,000 cubic metres of biogas a day. That was the plan at launch, not a confirmed operating figure.

The floors are not only about saving land. If disease appears on one floor, it can be isolated without shutting down the whole complex, the same logic as separate units in a chemical plant or compartments on a ship.

Twenty-six floors of pigs, lifts, sensors and central control. “Sty” no longer fits. “Plant” does.

### Muyuan: pigs as an industry

Ezhou is the most striking example, not the biggest player. China’s largest pork producer is Muyuan Foods from Henan province. In 2025 it sold about 78 million market hogs, up 26% year on year, giving it more than 10% of the market. Net profit for 2025 was 15.49 billion yuan, down 13.4% from 2024: Chinese hog prices move in cycles, and even the industry leader can’t escape them. Its slaughter and meat-processing business nearly doubled its revenue.

Muyuan is known for keeping the whole chain in-house: its own feed, breeding, farming and, more recently, slaughter. And for counting costs obsessively: in its 2025 report it put its full cost at about 12 yuan per kilogram of live weight, the lowest in the industry, with a target of 11.5 yuan.

Half a yuan per kilo sounds trivial until you multiply it by 78 million pigs at well over a hundred kilos each.

### What changed in the industry

According to China’s agriculture ministry, the share of pork produced on large-scale farms passed 68% in 2023, up about three percentage points in a year. The industry is moving fast from “every household keeps a few pigs” to “pigs are raised by industrial companies”.

That changes almost everything.

**Data.** A large farm tracks weight, feed, growth, mortality and water use for every group of animals. Decisions follow dashboards, not a farmer’s hunch.

**People.** A mega-farm needs fewer stockmen and more vets, ventilation engineers, nutritionists and control-room operators.

**The cycle.** The bigger the producers, the more their decisions move prices. Beijing regularly adjusts sow herds and pork reserves to smooth the cycle. At the end of 2023 the sow herd stood at about 41.42 million, some 2.5 million below its peak.

**Criticism.** High-rise farms have their critics: animal welfare, the risk of ventilation failures, concentrated manure. These are real limits, and they are debated in China too.

### What to ask at an industrial farm

1.  How does staff entry work, and how long does the sanitary airlock take?
2.  What happens when power or ventilation fails?
3.  How many people work on each floor, and what are their jobs?
4.  Where does the manure go, and what does that cost?

The manure question is rarely asked, and it should be. In livestock farming it is a budget line, just like feed.

### Beyond China

Chinese pig farming shows agriculture turning into industry, and the same thing is visible in poultry, dairy and greenhouse farming. The principles repeat: a closed perimeter, data instead of intuition, central control, specialists instead of general labour.

At Global Tech Tour we run business [expeditions](/en/expeditions) to China and closed visits to companies across 17 industries. For agribusiness, sites like these matter not only for their scale but for the questions they force: how to run biosecurity, who controls the climate, how to calculate the cost per kilo. One honest caveat: such sites are harder to get into than a car plant. Biosecurity applies to guests too.

The picture-book pig lived in a sty and worried about nothing. Her granddaughter lives on the twenty-sixth floor, breathes filtered air and is watched by a vet around the clock. That’s probably progress. Unfortunately, nobody has yet found a way to ask the pig.
