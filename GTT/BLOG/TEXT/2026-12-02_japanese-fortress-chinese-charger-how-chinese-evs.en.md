For half a century South-East Asia was Japan’s automotive backyard. [Toyota](/en/companies/toyota), Daihatsu, Honda, Mitsubishi and Suzuki built plants here while China’s car industry was still assembling copies of other people’s models. The region got so used to Japanese cars that “reliable” came to be spoken with a Japanese accent.

Now an electric car has driven into that backyard. Quietly, since electric cars cannot do it any other way.

Let us look at the two key markets, Indonesia and Malaysia, using 2025–2026 figures, and at what this means for anyone studying Chinese carmakers as a model of overseas expansion.

### Indonesia: the market shrank, China grew

First the context. According to the Indonesian carmakers’ association Gaikindo, 803,687 cars were sold in Indonesia in 2025, down 7.2% on the year before. The market was shrinking, and Japanese brands felt it: Toyota sales fell 13.3% to 250,431 units, Daihatsu fell 19.8% and Honda 40.4%.

Chinese brands grew in the same year. [BYD](/en/companies/byd) sold about 46,700 cars in Indonesia (wholesale, Gaikindo data reported by AfMA), roughly three times its 2024 figure, for a 5.8% share and first place among Chinese brands. Retail data tell the same story: 44,342 cars and the lead among Chinese brands, followed by Wuling, Chery and Denza, BYD’s own premium brand.

BYD only started selling in Indonesia in January 2024.

Japanese brands still sell most of the cars in the country: in spring 2025 Toyota and Daihatsu together held about half the market. But half is no longer everything. The fortress is still standing; it just has someone else’s gate in the wall.

### Why now: an incentive with an expiry date

The jump in Chinese EV sales was no accident. In 2024–2025 Indonesia exempted imported electric cars from import duty and luxury tax, cutting the combined burden to around 12% from rates that had reached as high as 77% for some models.

The condition was strict: in return, carmakers committed to building cars in Indonesia in volumes matching their imports, backed by bank guarantees. The incentive expired on 31 December 2025. From 2026 EVs must be assembled locally, and local content requirements rise: 40% from 2026, 60% in 2027–2028 and 80% by 2030.

The logic is clear. Use imports to test demand, then lock it in with a factory. Indonesia effectively leased out its market with an option to buy, on condition that the tenant later builds a house on the plot.

### The plant in Subang

BYD kept its side of the deal. On 3 September 2026 its plant opened in the Subang Smartpolitan industrial park in West Java: a 126-hectare site, an investment of about 11 trillion rupiah (roughly US$700 million) and a target capacity of up to 150,000 vehicles a year.

Chinese carmakers are not newcomers to Indonesia, though. Back in August 2022 Wuling started building the small Air ev city car at its Cikarang plant, one of the country’s first mass-market locally made EVs.

### Toyota’s answer

The Japanese are not standing still. In 2025 Toyota began assembling the bZ4X electric crossover at its Karawang plant from knocked-down kits, with around 40% local parts, priced at 799 million rupiah. Alongside it came the Urban Cruiser, imported from Maruti Suzuki’s plant in India.

Look at the numbers: a Japanese EV in Indonesia costs about 800 million rupiah, while the mass-market Chinese models that gave BYD its volume are noticeably cheaper. Toyota is answering less with price than with what it already has: factories, a nationwide dealer and service network, and hybrids that Indonesian buyers know and trust.

China has speed. Japan has customer habit. We will see which one holds its charge longer.

### Malaysia: a record year and protecting your own

Malaysia is different because it has strong national brands. According to the Malaysian Automotive Association (MAA), a record 820,752 vehicles were sold in 2025. Perodua sold 359,904, about 44% of the market, and national brands together (Perodua and Proton) took 62.3%.

Here it gets interesting. Perodua’s manufacturing partner is Daihatsu, wholly owned by Toyota since 2016. Proton has been 49.9% owned by China’s [Geely](/en/companies/geely) since 2017. So Malaysia’s two “national” carmakers are, respectively, a Japanese and a Chinese project with a Malaysian passport.

In September 2025 Proton opened its first EV assembly plant in Tanjung Malim. The first model is the e.MAS 7, essentially a rebadged Geely EX5 assembled from Chinese kits. Capacity is 20,000 units a year, expandable to 45,000.

Among imported EVs, BYD leads: Focus2Move estimates it took about 44% of Malaysia’s EV market in 2025.

### Malaysia tightens the rules

That is exactly why Malaysia moved to protect local production in 2026. From 1 July 2026, according to Paul Tan, fully imported EVs must have a CIF value of at least RM200,000 and an output of at least 180 kW, and this applies to all models on sale, not only new ones. Locally assembled cars are exempt. By the same publication’s estimate, this pushes many affordable Chinese models out of the sub-RM300,000 segment.

BYD had planned its own plant in Tanjung Malim, but, as Free Malaysia Today reported in March 2026 citing investment minister Johari Ghani, the ministry set conditions: export at least 80% of output and price the rest above RM200,000. BYD had not accepted the terms, and the project was under review. “We have to protect our auto industry,” the minister said.

Malaysia shows the other side of expansion. First a country opens the door to let technology in. Then it pulls the door half-shut so that the technology does not push out its own players.

### What follows

1.  **China enters South-East Asia through factories, not just ports.** Imports are reconnaissance; local assembly secures the ground. Indonesia is a textbook case for anyone studying Chinese expansion.
2.  **The rules change faster than the model line-up.** In two years Indonesia introduced and ended an incentive, and Malaysia tightened imports. “Ship it in and sell it” does not last long in this region.
3.  **Japan’s advantage is not the engine but the infrastructure.** Dealers, service, financing, residual values. Chinese brands still have to build all of that, and it takes longer than a factory.
4.  **The most interesting hybrids are companies, not cars.** Proton with Geely, Perodua with Daihatsu: South-East Asia increasingly chooses not between Japan and China, but a combination of both.

### The bottom line

The Japanese came to South-East Asia for the long haul: with factories, training schools for mechanics and patience. The Chinese came fast and, it seems, also for the long haul. The region has not yet decided whom it loves more. It is deciding who will build it a factory.

Fifty years ago Japanese cars were the newcomers here too. People said, “Let’s see how long they last.”

They lasted.
