Ask where a fresh tomato comes from in January and in most of the northern hemisphere the honest answer is “somewhere far away, by truck or plane”. In China there is often a more precise answer, with an address: Shouguang, Shandong province. It is a county-level city within Weifang, and it is called China’s vegetable capital without any irony. We’ll have to supply the irony ourselves.

### Seventeen greenhouses and one stubborn man

The story begins in 1989 in Sanyuanzhu village. The village Party secretary, Wang Leyi, had just toured six provinces and cities looking for better ways to grow vegetables. Back home, he built a greenhouse that needed no coal in winter and was heated by sunlight alone.

The design is simple and clever. A thick, solid north wall stores heat during the day and releases it at night. The south slope is covered with plastic film, and at night with insulating mats. In China these are called “winter-warm greenhouses”.

In mid-1989 Wang persuaded 16 neighbours to join him, giving the village 17 greenhouses. They started with cucumbers. That winter the 17 families earned much more than everyone else, and what followed is what happens in China when an idea makes money: the village followed, then the county, then other regions.

A lesson for founders: to change an industry, you don’t need an investor. You need sixteen neighbours who believe you.

### Scale: 157,000 greenhouses and 9 million tonnes

According to Xinhua, as of 2025 Shouguang had 157,000 vegetable greenhouses producing more than 4.5 million tonnes a year, enough to feed about 40 million people for a year.

Production is only half the story. The other half is trade. More than 9 million tonnes of vegetables pass through Shouguang every year, about 3 million of them through the city’s agricultural logistics park. More than 20,000 trucks come and go every day, and by Xinhua’s count over 17 tonnes of vegetables leave Shouguang every minute for more than 200 cities.

So Shouguang doesn’t just grow; it gathers. A cucumber from a neighbouring province may travel to Beijing via Shouguang because this is where it can be sold, tested and loaded fastest. The logistics park’s lab, per Xinhua, checks about 1,400 batches a day, with a pass rate above 99%.

### The new-generation greenhouse

The 1989 greenhouses occupied about two mu of land (a mu is roughly 667 m²), with about half a mu actually planted. The newest ones occupy eight mu or more, with at least four under crops. City data say disease rates are 10% lower and yields 15–20% higher.

85% of new greenhouses come with smart equipment: sensors, automated irrigation, climate control from a phone. A farmer who once got up at night to pull the mats over can now, in theory, do it from bed. The catch is that it’s no longer the greenhouse waking him up. It’s the app.

### Seeds: the quiet revolution

The most important part of Shouguang’s story is not glass or film but seeds. For years Chinese greenhouse farmers depended on expensive imported seed. According to Xinhua, a decade or so ago a single seed could cost more than 10 yuan; now it costs a little over one.

Shouguang has 277 vegetable varieties with plant-breeder protection, 20 of them new in 2025, and aims for 400 within five years. The share of domestic seed in its market rose from 54% in 2010 to more than 70%. The city works with more than 40 research institutes and universities and hosts 12 national-level research platforms.

It is the classic Chinese trajectory: import, localise, then develop your own. The same path as electronics and cars, just at the scale of a cucumber seed.

### Exporting the model

Shouguang sells not just vegetables but know-how. Xinhua reports a pool of 108,000 technical specialists and partnerships with 59 vegetable parks in 29 provinces, autonomous regions and municipalities.

The model travels abroad, too. More than 30 local companies and cooperatives work in over 40 countries and regions, and Shouguang-linked firms have built more than 270 growing parks in 40 countries. In 2025 a glass greenhouse complex of about 70,000 square metres was being prepared for shipment to Belarus.

Then there is the China (Shouguang) International Vegetable Expo, held since 2000, usually from 20 April to 30 May, at the city’s high-tech demonstration park: part trade fair, part science conference, part industry showcase.

### The limits

**Land and soil.** A winter-warm greenhouse with its massive wall is not the most land-efficient design: one mu of greenhouse needs about two mu of land. Years of intensive cropping also wear out the soil.

**Prices.** Vegetable markets are volatile. A bumper harvest drives prices down, and a farmer with a loan on a smart greenhouse feels it first.

**Standardisation.** The city says 1,600 vegetable cooperatives have been upgraded and more than 80% of farmers follow production standards. That means better organisation, but also higher demands on small growers who struggle to keep up.

### What to ask in a greenhouse cluster

1.  Who owns the greenhouse: the farmer, a cooperative or a company?
2.  Where do the seeds come from, and what do they cost compared with five years ago?
3.  How do vegetables get from greenhouse to wholesale market, and how many hours does it take?
4.  What happens when the price drops below cost?

The last question applies to any business. “We wait” counts as an answer.

### What to take home

At Global Tech Tour we run business [expeditions](/en/expeditions) to China and closed visits to companies across many industries, food among them. For agribusiness and retail, Shouguang is interesting as a cluster where production, trade, breeding and logistics sit in one city. It is an ecosystem, not a company, and it is worth seeing as a whole rather than one greenhouse at a time.

In 1989 a village Party secretary wanted his neighbours to have money in winter. What he ended up with is a huge country eating winter cucumbers with a precise address. Sometimes a big strategy starts with a small greenhouse and a thick north wall that simply holds the heat well.
