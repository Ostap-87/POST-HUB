“Retires” is not quite the right word. Retirees rest. Dongguan is retraining. Think of someone who signs up for a coding course at sixty and, a couple of years later, is hiring their former classmates.

But let’s start at the beginning. With a handbag.

### Factory number one

In 1978 the Taiping handbag factory opened in Humen, a town in Dongguan. It became Dongguan’s first enterprise of the “three imports and one compensation” type: a foreign partner, usually from Hong Kong, supplied materials, designs and equipment; the local factory assembled and was paid a processing fee. At its peak it covered about 8,000 square metres. It closed at the end of May 2007, and the buildings were demolished.

The first factory did not survive to see the model’s thirtieth birthday. The model would not have survived either, had it not changed careers.

In between, Dongguan became what textbooks call the world’s factory. According to The Paper, from 1996 to 2002 it was China’s third-largest exporting city seven years running, behind Shenzhen and Shanghai. In the 1990s, migrant workers poured in. By 2001 the city had nearly 130,000 registered private enterprises.

The formula was simple: cheap, fast, lots. If you have ever held a toy, a trainer or a phone charger stamped “Made in China”, there is a fair chance part of it passed through Dongguan.

### When cheap ran out

Simple formulas break simply. Wages rose, migrant workers became less eager to come, and the 2008 global crisis hit export orders. In the first quarter of 2009 Dongguan’s GDP fell 2.3%. Between 2009 and 2014, Southern Metropolis Daily reported, the city missed its annual growth target in four years out of six.

Population growth tells the same story: an average of 2.5% a year in 2000–2010, just 0.2% in 2010–2017. The city everyone used to move to became a city people visit on business.

This is usually where factory towns begin to age quietly. Plants leave for cheaper places, the rest run on old machines, young people follow the plants. Dongguan took a different, and not particularly comfortable, road.

The world’s factory learned the basic rule of competition: someone will always be cheaper than you. Someone will also always be more expensive. The question is which way you grow.

### Machines instead of people

In 2014 the city launched a “machines replace people” programme. The Paper reports it saved the labour of nearly 200,000 workers. One electronics plant went from 10,000 staff to 3,000. One food production line went from more than a hundred workers to fewer than ten, while output rose roughly tenfold.

The most quoted case was Changying Precision Technology. In 2015, according to TechRepublic, its automated phone-parts plant cut its workforce from 650 to 60, raised output per person from 8,000 to 21,000 pieces, and reduced the defect rate from 25% to under 5%.

It reads like an advertisement for robotics. For the city it meant something else: if a plant needs a tenth of the people, the city needs different people. Not assemblers, but technicians, programmers and engineers. A robot on the line removes ten jobs on the floor and adds one in the department that keeps it running. The question is where that person comes from.

### A lake instead of a workshop

Dongguan looked for the answer by Songshan Lake. In 2013 Huawei announced it would move its devices business there, onto a campus of about 1,900 mu (roughly 127 hectares) with an investment of around 10 billion yuan. The move began in 2018 with 2,700 staff; later nearly 30,000 R&D employees worked there. The campus is built to resemble a European town, and, China Daily notes, tens of thousands of employees commute daily between Shenzhen and Dongguan.

A city that spent twenty years assembling other people’s phones now hosts an office where phones are invented. Those are different jobs, and that is the whole point.

Nearby, XbotPark, a hard-tech start-up incubator founded in 2014, has incubated more than 80 companies with a combined valuation of about 80 billion yuan; around 15% are unicorns or close to it. Its new 110,000-square-metre campus opened in December 2023.

Traditional industry moved too. China Daily cites OPPO’s Dongguan operations: productivity growth above 30% a year and automation above 80%. In Chang’an town, home to more than 7,000 jewellery factories and over 100,000 workers, one factory cut overseas order fulfilment from six months to six days.

### How to tell a factory town that is ageing from one that is learning

1.  The ageing one says “we’re cheaper”. The learning one says “we’re faster”.
2.  The ageing one counts workers. The learning one counts engineers.
3.  The ageing one waits for customers. The learning one becomes a customer for its neighbours.
4.  The ageing one complains that young people have left. The learning one complains that young commuters from Shenzhen take all the train seats.

### Where things stand

In 2021 Dongguan’s GDP passed 1 trillion yuan, making it Guangdong’s fourth trillion-yuan city after Guangzhou, Shenzhen and Foshan. Foreign trade hit a record 1.52 trillion yuan that year. Then came a slump: trade fell until late 2023, and GDP growth in 2023 was the lowest in the province.

In 2025, China News reports, the economy grew 4%, industrial output at larger firms also 4%, and foreign trade rose 13.8% to 1.58 trillion yuan, overtaking the 2021 record.

This is neither a triumph nor a decline. It is the story of a city that understood its old model would end and began rebuilding while it still paid the bills. The rebuild came with crises, missed targets and layoffs. But it happened.

### What it means for business

Many buyers still picture Dongguan as the place where everything is made cheaply. That picture is out of date. The city now combines highly automated plants, R&D campuses and small factories that compete on speed rather than price.

For suppliers, this means price-only negotiation is less and less productive. For manufacturers, it is worth studying how the city handled automation, because the same challenge awaits any industrial company facing rising wages. On Global Tech Tour business expeditions, that is what we suggest looking at: not a single robot, but how an entire city changes when the price of labour does.

The handbag factory in Humen lasted nearly thirty years. Its building is gone. Its idea is not: it moved to other countries, leaving Dongguan with the question of what to do next. It seems to have found an answer. Not a complete one. But a better one than many places that never asked the question at all.
