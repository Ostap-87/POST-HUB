“Remote” has travelled a long way in recent years. First programmers worked remotely. Then accountants. Then everyone else, including people who had insisted their job could never be done from home.

Surgeons held out longest. Their argument was solid: you cannot mail a patient. That remains true. What turns out to be possible is sending the surgeon over the network. Or rather, the surgeon’s hands.

This is not about clinical medicine or who needs surgery when. It is about how a technology shown at trade fairs for years became approved practice, and what that says about China’s medtech industry.

### How it works: the console here, the hands there

In robot-assisted surgery, the surgeon sits at a console, looks into a 3D display and controls the instruments, while the robot’s arms mirror those movements at the operating table. Normally the console is in the same room, a few metres from the patient.

Telesurgery is the same setup, except that the console and the table are separated by hundreds or thousands of kilometres of network. Technically, the main challenge is latency. If a hand movement reaches the instrument with a noticeable delay, the surgery cannot go on. That is why for years the industry talked less about robots than about connections.

A local surgical team always stays with the patient. A remote operation is not “a robot alone in a room” but two teams linked by a network.

The surgeon at the console can see everything. Except the coffee nobody brought. That part has not changed since the days of the operating theatre gallery.

### May 2025: from experiment to approved

The key date is 20 May 2025, when China’s National Medical Products Administration (NMPA) approved MicroPort MedBot’s Toumai system for remote surgery. The company describes it as the world’s first tele-operated surgical robot authorised for commercial clinical use. By then, according to MicroPort, nearly 400 remote procedures had already been performed with it.

The difference between “nearly 400 procedures performed” and “approved” is huge, even if invisible from outside. Procedures before approval are clinical studies and demonstrations, each needing special permission, a protocol and national attention. A procedure after approval is a service a hospital can schedule, cost and build into its routine.

A record shows it can be done. A registration shows it can be done every Tuesday.

### 17,000 kilometres: USA to Angola

The longest distance the company has reported dates from June 2025. Professor Vipul Patel, president of the Society of Robotic Surgery, sat at the console in Orlando, Florida. The patient was in a hospital in Luanda, Angola. The Toumai robot carried out a radical prostatectomy in about 90 minutes over a one-way network distance of around 17,000 km.

Distance is not the only point. According to MicroPort, the operation was done under an FDA Investigational Device Exemption, and the company calls it the world’s first FDA-approved robotic telesurgery on a human. In other words, the US regulator permitted such an experiment with an American surgeon for the first time. The robot itself is Chinese.

For Chinese medtech, that detail is worth more than any advertising. Thirty years ago Chinese hospitals bought Western equipment and learned to use it. Now a Western surgeon works on Chinese equipment, and the US regulator does the paperwork.

### Where demonstration ends and practice begins

It is worth looking at the numbers without the applause. Remote operations are still a small share of surgery, even in China. Robotic surgery as a whole is early there too: according to an estimate cited by BOC International analysts, robots were used in only about 0.7% of endoscopic operations in China in 2024. The installed base of multi-port endoscopic robots grew from 134 systems in 2019 to 502 in 2024. Fast, but for a country of that size it is still hundreds, not thousands.

There are practical questions no single approval answers: who is responsible if the connection drops, how a remote surgeon is paid, how a doctor operating in another country is licensed. The answers will take years.

So the honest version is this: remote surgery has moved from “done once for the cameras” to “approved and being done”. “Routine” is still a long way off.

### Money: a robot that sells abroad

For business, the most interesting part of the story is not the records but the sales. According to The Bamboo Works, MicroPort MedBot’s 2025 revenue was 551 million yuan, more than double the year before. About 73% of sales came from abroad, whereas in 2024 China accounted for about 60% of revenue. Toumai has more than 300 commercial orders worldwide, with commercial installations in 60 markets. Not long ago the company was celebrating its 100th order; by mid-2026 there were more than 300 commercial ones. In July 2026 it forecast its first half-year profit.

Why are exports growing faster than the home market? One reason is obvious: for many hospitals in developing countries a Western surgical robot is too expensive, and a Chinese one opens a market that did not previously exist. Telesurgery is an extra argument: the hospital gets not only a robot but the option of bringing in an experienced surgeon from another country.

Chinese medtech used to sell on price. Now it sells what others do not have.

### Five questions to ask at a surgical robot plant

1.  What signal latency is acceptable, and what happens when it rises?
2.  Who stands at the patient’s side during a remote operation, and how are they trained?
3.  In which countries is the system registered for ordinary surgery, and where for remote surgery?
4.  What is the sales model: robot, disposable instruments, service?
5.  How many remote operations a month happen in ordinary hospital schedules rather than at conferences?

The last question is the most awkward. Which makes it the most useful.

### The bottom line

The Toumai story shows how Chinese medtech is changing roles: from a buyer catching up on other people’s technology to a developer that gets the first registration and then sells it to the world. For medical companies coming to China on a business expedition, it is a reason to look not only at manufacturing but at regulatory strategy: how a company chooses where to register first and in what order to enter markets.

For everyone else, it is a reminder that “this can’t be done remotely” usually means “this hasn’t been approved yet”.

Surgeon and patient still have to trust each other. Now there is an internet provider in the relationship as well.
