A business trip to China comes in two halves: day and evening. During the day you inspect the factory. In the evening, the factory inspects you.

In Chinese business culture, a banquet is neither a reward for a long day nor a break between visits. It is negotiation by other means. At the table your hosts decide whether there will be a second conversation, at what level and in what tone. So prepare for it as seriously as for the shop floor. The only difference: on the shop floor they give you a hard hat. At the table you are on your own.

Here is Global Tech Tour’s short guide to business dining in China. One caveat: China is big, and a banquet in the north can differ from one in the south about as much as a wedding differs from a board meeting, at least in the number of toasts. The fundamentals, though, are shared.

### 1. Seating: your seat is chosen for you

Do not sit down first, and do not sit wherever you like. A formal dinner has a seating plan, and you will be shown to your place.

The seat of honour faces the entrance. That is where the host or the principal guest sits. Opposite, with their back to the door, usually sits the host’s deputy, who handles the ordering and the waiting staff. Everyone else is seated by rank, from the centre outwards. The closer you are to the seat of honour, the higher your standing in your hosts’ eyes. That is no reason for pride, but it is a reason to listen carefully.

The practical takeaway: tell your organiser in advance who leads your delegation. Otherwise your hosts will decide for themselves, and not necessarily by job title. Sometimes it is age, sometimes height, and sometimes it is whoever walked through the door first. Usually the driver.

### 2. The turntable and serving chopsticks

The banquet table is round, with a rotating glass turntable in the middle. Dishes sit on it, and it is turned gently, never while someone is serving themselves. A new dish is usually turned towards the principal guest first.

Shared dishes often come with serving chopsticks; some restaurants use black pairs for personal use and white ones for serving. Never stand your chopsticks upright in rice, lick them or tap them on the plate. When in doubt, copy your host. It is a universal rule for banquets and, arguably, for life.

Try everything you are offered. You need not finish it. Taste it and say it is good. Food on the table is a gift, and gifts are praised, not inspected.

### 3. Toasts: who, when and how

The host gives the first toast. The principal guest replies. Then comes something that looks like chaos from outside and is strictly organised from within: hosts visit guests, guests visit hosts, and everyone drinks with everyone individually.

A few rules that save face. When you drink with someone more senior, hold your glass lower than theirs, literally: the rim of your glass touches theirs just below their rim. Hold the glass with both hands, especially when toasting the host, and stand up to do so. “Ganbei” (干杯) means “dry glass”, and it is meant literally. Take only a sip after a ganbei and someone may joke that you are saving the rest for the fish.

Keep toasts short and specific: to cooperation, to hospitality, to what you saw at the plant today. A three-part toast with a historical introduction is wonderful at a wedding, less so at a table where every sentence is being interpreted.

### 4. Baijiu: a drink that punishes overconfidence

Baijiu is a Chinese grain spirit, most often made from sorghum. It usually runs from 40% to 60% alcohol; the famous [Moutai](/en/companies/moutai) Feitian is 53%. It is served in tiny glasses, and that is a design trap: the glass is small, the toasts are many, and there is only one of you.

The taste is sharp and unfamiliar. Comparing it with vodka is like comparing a banjo with a violin because both have strings. What matters is this: a ganbei with baijiu means bottoms up, and there may be twenty of them in an evening. Do your arithmetic before the first one, not after the twelfth.

### 5. How to decline without offending

You can decline. The key is to do it early and consistently.

The most reliable approach is to say at the start of the evening that you do not drink: for health reasons, by conviction, because you are driving or because you have negotiations in the morning. A refusal announced at the start is accepted calmly. A refusal after the third glass sounds like “I am no longer interested in you”.

You can toast with tea, juice or water. The toast keeps its weight as long as you stand, hold the glass with both hands and make eye contact. A delegation benefits from having one person who drinks for everyone. In China they are appreciated. At home they deserve a day off.

One more recent factor. In May 2025, China’s Communist Party Central Committee and the State Council updated their austerity rules for party and government bodies, banning alcohol and cigarettes at official meals and receptions. If your host is a government body or an industrial park administration, expect a sober banquet. Private companies set their own rules, but it is worth asking your organiser what kind of evening to expect.

### Six things not to say at a banquet

1.  “Could I have a fork?” You can, but try the chopsticks first.
2.  “I’ll sit here, it’s closer to the door.”
3.  “Let’s get down to business.” Business is already being discussed, just differently.
4.  “Back home we have something stronger.”
5.  “That’s it, I can’t drink any more”, after the fifteenth glass. The moment for that was after the first.
6.  “The bill, please.” The host pays. You may offer, but do not insist. Invite them in return when they visit you.

### The bottom line

You will come away with three things: photos of dishes you will never identify, a mild headache, and the feeling that your relationship with your partner has changed. The first two will pass. The third is why you were there.

A Chinese banquet is a negotiation in which nobody talks about business. That is exactly why so much business gets agreed there.
