Artificial intelligence can do two things: work and perform. It performs beautifully. At conferences it writes poems, paints cats in the style of classical ink painting and answers the host’s questions before he has finished asking them.

It works in places conferences never visit: a server room next to an air-conditioner plant, the document-checking office of a district government, the chat window of a shop that sells socks. No stage, no spotlights. Just the thing AI was supposed to deliver in the first place: hours saved and fewer mistakes.

Below are a few such places in China in 2025–2026. The figures are as reported by the companies and agencies themselves, with dates. Everyone counts differently, and we will come back to that.

### First, the quota

In late August 2025 China’s State Council issued its guideline on “AI+”, a programme to bring artificial intelligence into every sector. The targets read like a production quota: penetration of new-generation intelligent devices and AI agents above 70% by 2027 and above 90% by 2030, with the country entering a “new stage of the intelligent economy” by 2035. The 15th Five-Year Plan for 2026–2030 lists “AI+” among its priorities.

China rolls out AI the way it once rolled out motorways: first decide it will be everywhere, then work out where it is needed.

### The factory: two thousand agents and a lot of air conditioners

In Guangzhou’s Nansha district, TCL has built an air-conditioner plant that China Daily, in August 2026, called “truly AI-native”. Phase one cost nearly RMB 2 billion, with designed capacity of 8 million units a year and about 4 million expected in 2026. Data from 32,000 sensors flows into an in-house AI centre of 106 server cabinets, where more than 2,000 AI agents run in parallel. Infrared sensors catch refrigerant leaks; machine vision with special lighting spots tiny defects.

The same report mentions a neighbour: at Gree’s plant in Zhuhai, 80% automation cut the crew on a 480-metre outdoor-unit assembly line from more than 70 people to 20.

Note the order. Sensors first, then data, then agents. An agent without sensors is a brilliant intern without a security badge: it never gets onto the shop floor.

### The bank: use cases by the hundred

In its 2025 annual report, China Merchants Bank said it uses AI in 856 scenarios. It runs 183 specialised domain models, its token volume grew tenfold in a year, and more than 97% of its developers use a coding assistant. IT spending stands at 4.31% of operating income, over RMB 12.9 billion.

ICBC, the country’s largest bank, put the work done by its model platform in 2024 at the equivalent of more than 40,000 people a year. It has not explained the calculation, so read that as an order of magnitude rather than a staffing chart.

Chinese industry press itself warns that there is no common standard for counting “scenarios”. At one bank a scenario is a product; at another, a button in an app.

A “use case” is a unit of measurement with no reference weight in any bureau of standards. It does, however, look very good in an annual report.

### The hospital: installed fast, checked slowly

According to a 2025 scoping review, the first known deployment of DeepSeek-R1 in a Chinese hospital took place on 10 February 2025. By the end of June 2025 the model had been deployed at 48 of China’s top 100 hospitals, 37 of them within the first month. Most were private, on-premises installations for clinical decision support.

The same review has less festive numbers. Only 36% of deployments publicly described a pre-deployment assessment, 22% reported results and 9% identified risks. The authors call for tighter regulation and validation.

Medicine is one field where speed of adoption is not automatically a compliment. A hospital that installs a model in a month earns respect. A hospital that tests it for a year earns trust.

### The district office: seventy staff who skip lunch

On 18 February 2025, Shenzhen’s Futian district put 70 DeepSeek-based “AI employees” to work, trained on a local knowledge base. They cover 240 administrative scenarios, from document processing and public enquiries to emergency management and investment promotion. According to the city government, document review time fell by 90%, accuracy in handling public requests rose from 70% to 95%, and a tailored service that used to take five days now takes minutes.

Bureaucracy is the perfect habitat for a language model. The texts are long, the rules are known, and the human checking margins for the hundredth time always wanted to be doing something else.

AI in government does not begin with the smart city. It begins with the correct margin on an internal memo.

### The shop: every seller gets a department

In e-commerce, agents now work on both sides of the counter. According to The Next Web in May 2026, Tmall upgraded its Business Advisor tool so that every store gets a set of AI agents working around the clock on analytics, advertising, visuals, customer service and after-sales. An AI customer-service assistant was piloted with 200,000 merchants and reportedly lifted conversion by 30%. And in one week in February 2026, Alipay processed 120 million purchases placed by an AI agent inside a chat.

A small Chinese shop can now afford what only a big chain used to have: an analyst, a designer and a support desk. All three, admittedly, live on the marketplace’s servers, and you cannot exactly poach them.

### Five questions when someone shows you “AI in production”

1.  Which model runs inside, theirs or someone else’s, and on whose servers?
2.  What was the baseline before: how many people, hours or errors, and who measured it?
3.  What share of decisions still goes through a human?
4.  What does the system cost to run each year compared with what it cost to launch?
5.  How many of the announced use cases run every day, and not just in the annual report?

The fifth question is awkward. Save it for last, once the tea has been poured.

### What this means for a manager

The Chinese examples share one thing: AI appears where there is already data and repetitive work. Sensors at the TCL plant, the stream of documents in a district office, millions of conversations with shoppers. Where there is no data, agents draw handsome diagrams and wait.

Second, models are cheap and often open, so everything else becomes the expensive part: data preparation, integration, training people and checking results. What makes the Chinese experience useful is not which model they chose but how they organised that “everything else”.

That is hard to grasp from press releases and much easier to see in person. At Global Tech Tour we run business expeditions to China, with closed visits to companies across 17 industries and custom programmes built around a client’s task. For an AI-focused programme, our advice is to look past the showroom and at the desk of a person who works next to an agent.

### In place of a conclusion

AI on stage is always in top form. AI on shift sometimes gets things wrong, sometimes waits for an operator to approve a decision, sometimes just checks margins. But by the end of the day, somebody has an hour back.

Perhaps that is the most honest definition of artificial intelligence: a technology that gives people their time back. What to do with that time is still up to us. The models are no help there yet.
