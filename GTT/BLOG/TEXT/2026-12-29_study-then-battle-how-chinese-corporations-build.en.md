Corporate training looks much the same in many companies. Once a year staff gather in a hotel conference room, a visiting trainer draws a pyramid on a flipchart, and after lunch half the room is looking at their phones and the other half out of the window. On Monday everything goes back to normal. Including the pyramid.

Chinese corporations took a different route. They build their own universities, with campuses, faculty, programmes and, most interestingly, a price list for their own departments.

“University” is a grand word. But the word is not the point. The design is.

### Huawei: training for battle

[Huawei](/en/companies/huawei) University was formally registered in 2005 and sits at the company’s Shenzhen headquarters. Chinese business media describe a teaching area of about 155,000 square metres, more than a hundred classrooms and room for over two thousand trainees at once.

The size impresses, but it is not the point. Huawei University’s formula is 训战结合, “combining training with combat”. Its job is not general education but preparing people for specific tasks in the core business. It is not a separate institution: it is attached to business leaders and works to their needs.

Founder Ren Zhengfei put it with military bluntness. When shown a plan to develop generic flagship courses, he reportedly responded along these lines: “We are about to march north, and you are not training generals for us. How can that be?”

Generals are not produced by generic courses. They are produced where the fighting is.

### Training that departments pay for

The most interesting detail at Huawei is money. Ren insisted the university should charge internal departments for training, but “reasonably”. The logic is simple: what comes free is not valued. If a department pays for a course from its own budget, its manager thinks hard about whom to send and why. And a university that earns its keep depends less on what it is allocated from above.

An internal market for training is an unusual idea. In most companies training is a cost line, the first to be cut. In Huawei’s model it is a service that gets bought when it is useful and dropped when it is not.

The most expensive training is the free kind: it consumes the time of people who did not know why they were there.

### Alibaba: the month that turns a newcomer into an insider

At [Alibaba](/en/companies/alibaba) the main training event happens at the very start. Chinese business writers describe new hires receiving a “flower name”, an internal nickname used across the company, and a booklet called “Alibaba for a Hundred Years” (百年阿里). Then comes at least a month of induction that employees jokingly called a “spiritual baptism”. After the group programme comes training within the business unit, which can last up to a year.

The second feature is the “commissar” (政委) system: HR partners embedded in the business alongside the manager, sharing responsibility for people, entitled to push back and involved in major decisions. Training and culture are not a department on the third floor but a person at the next desk.

Next to Alibaba stood a separate project, Hupan University, founded at the end of 2014 as an independent non-profit for entrepreneurs. Applicants needed a company with at least 30 employees, three years of tax payments, revenue of at least 30 million yuan and three referees. That is a school for business owners rather than corporate training, but it is telling: the company’s culture had begun to spread beyond its walls.

### Tencent and Haier: two other angles

In 2014 [Tencent](/en/companies/tencent) set up Tencent University, aimed largely at partners, with separate academies for WeChat, e-commerce, its open platform and the internet. It teaches not only staff but those who build businesses on Tencent’s products. Training becomes part of the ecosystem: the better a partner understands the platform, the more it earns there.

Haier University opened on 26 December 1999 and, according to Chinese sources, became the world’s first corporate university certified under ISO 10015, the international standard for training management. Haier shows that a corporate university can be not just a talent forge but a system with standards that outsiders can audit.

### Corporate university or corporate event?

1.  A university has an internal client. An event has a budget.
2.  A university asks what changed three months later. An event asks whether you enjoyed lunch.
3.  University teachers have done the job themselves. Event speakers have read about it.
4.  A university is paid for by the department that sent the people. An event is paid for by “the company”, meaning nobody.
5.  A university leaves behind people who can do more. An event leaves behind photos in front of a banner.

### What travels to your company

A hundred-classroom campus makes no sense for a company of three hundred. But the principles scale down.

Tie training to a task. Not “a leadership course” but “preparing managers for the new branch by March”. Ren’s question about generals is worth asking yourself: what campaign are you training people for?

Charge for it internally. Even a notional internal price changes behaviour: managers start choosing.

Invest in the first weeks. Alibaba’s approach is expensive, but it saves years of a newcomer working out how things are done. One well-designed week of onboarding beats a year-long programme for everyone.

Put HR next to the manager. Not in a separate office but inside the business, with a voice.

Train your partners too. If your business depends on dealers, suppliers or franchisees, training them is as much an investment as training your own staff.

### The bottom line

Chinese corporations do not build universities out of love for academia. They build them because markets change faster than the education system can turn out specialists. People have to be trained constantly, and it is best done where the purpose is visible.

A good company hires people who can. A strong one grows people who will learn.

As for the flipchart pyramid, leave it at the hotel. They seem to be expecting it.
