China’s energy story is usually told in numbers with a lot of zeros: gigawatt-hours, trillions of yuan, percentages of the global market. The numbers are right. Their only flaw is that you cannot touch them.

You cannot touch anything on a battery line either. The rules are strict. But you can see it, and that is a different kind of knowledge.

Here is a route we would suggest to a company interested in energy storage, solar generation and factory automation. Three cities, one week, and not a single power plant. New energy is no longer made where electricity is generated. It is made where the things that generate and store it are built.

### Why these three cities

China’s new-energy supply chain is not spread evenly across the country. It sits in clusters, and each cluster shows a different link.

Ningde is home to the world’s largest battery maker. Changzhou calls itself the capital of new energy and makes both batteries and solar modules. Wuxi makes the equipment used to make both.

Put simply: in Ningde you see the product, in Changzhou the industry, in Wuxi the machines. In that order, the picture comes together instead of falling apart.

### Ningde: a city and its battery

Ningde is a modest city, by Chinese standards, in north-eastern Fujian. [CATL](/en/companies/catl) was founded here in 2011, and the city has been known mostly for CATL ever since.

The scale: according to SNE Research, CATL installed 464.7 GWh of EV batteries in 2025, a 39.2% global share. Number two, [BYD](/en/companies/byd), had 16.4%. Nearly two in every five traction batteries in the world come from a single company headquartered in a city many people outside China have never heard of.

CATL’s Ningde plant joined the World Economic Forum’s Global Lighthouse Network in 2021. Its sites in Yibin and Liyang followed.

One thing to know in advance: getting inside a plant of this calibre is not like buying a museum ticket. Every visit is agreed with the host, programmes change, and photography is almost certainly off the table. Even so, the standard part of a visit shows what matters: what manufacturing looks like when defects are counted per billion rather than per million.

### Changzhou: the self-declared capital

Changzhou, in Jiangsu province, lies between Shanghai and Nanjing. The city gave itself the title of “new energy capital” and, to be fair, backs it up: according to the city, the sector’s output exceeded 850 billion yuan in 2024 and was on track to pass 1 trillion yuan in 2025.

Trina Solar, one of the largest solar module makers, founded in 1997, is headquartered here. Liyang, part of Changzhou, hosts CATL’s third lighthouse. When the WEF recognised it in December 2023, it cited a 320% rise in output, a 33% cut in manufacturing costs and a 99% reduction in quality defects.

A 99% cut in defects is not an improvement. It is a career change for the quality inspector.

In 2025 two more Changzhou sites, Eaton and Mettler-Toledo, joined the lighthouse network. For a delegation this is useful: in one city you can compare Chinese and international plants at a similar level of automation and see how each got there.

### Wuxi: the people who build machines for everyone else

Wuxi is less than half an hour from Changzhou by high-speed train. It has played a double role in the history of Chinese solar. Suntech, one of the industry’s first Chinese giants, was founded here in 2001. And here, in 2013, its main operating company went through bankruptcy and restructuring. Even the sun, it turns out, can set.

Today Wuxi is interesting for another reason. It is home to Lead Intelligent Equipment, which since 2002 has been building equipment for lithium-ion battery and solar panel production, among other things. Customers it names on its own website include [Panasonic](/en/companies/panasonic), Sony, BMW and LONGi.

For engineers this may be the most useful stop on the route. At a battery plant you see the result. At an equipment maker you see what the result is assembled from, and what it would cost to replicate.

### “Dark factories”: what you will actually see

A “dark factory” is a lovely term: a plant with no people, so no need for lights. They appear often in presentations. Fully unmanned plants are rare in reality, and what you will most likely see is not darkness but lines where people stand not at every operation, but at every tenth.

That is the interesting part. Not that people are gone, but where they remain: setup, quality control, maintenance, data. The right question on such a floor is not “how many robots do you have?” but “who decides what happens when a robot gets it wrong?”

The lights go off not when people leave, but when people no longer need to watch.

### Who should go, and who should wait

1.  Manufacturers considering energy storage for their own sites. Seeing battery production beats reading three spec sheets.
2.  Engineers and production directors interested in automation as a process, not a picture.
3.  Developers and energy managers modelling solar on roofs and warehouses.
4.  Investors trying to understand why Chinese battery and module prices are what they are.
5.  Anyone planning to buy a plant “just like theirs” and have it running by spring. We suggest going first, doing the maths second, and moving spring.

### Getting around

Changzhou and Wuxi are neighbours on the Shanghai–Nanjing high-speed line, less than half an hour apart. Shanghai is usually the easiest airport. Ningde sits apart, in Fujian, and is best reached via Fuzhou. So the route often runs Ningde first, then north by plane or train, ending the week in the Yangtze River Delta, which is convenient for flying home.

### The bottom line

Energy used to be an industry you could see from a distance: chimneys, towers, power lines. New energy is different. You only see it up close, on a floor where a roll of foil becomes a battery and a silicon wafer becomes a module.

You can read the numbers about China at home. The scale you have to see. And it looks even more convincing with the lights on.
