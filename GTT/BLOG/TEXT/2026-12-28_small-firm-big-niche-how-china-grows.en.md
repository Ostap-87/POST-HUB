When people talk about Chinese industry, they usually name the giants: carmakers, battery makers, telecoms. Everyone knows them, everyone writes about them, everyone wants to tour their plants.

Take any Chinese EV, robot or machine tool apart, though, and you find the giants make surprisingly little of it. The sensor, the gearbox, the special ceramic, the adhesive that holds at minus forty: these come from companies you have never heard of. They employ two hundred or five hundred people, and their product sits inside half the equipment in their industry.

China has a name for them: “little giants”. It sounds like an oxymoron. It works as industrial policy.

### Four characters

The programme’s official name is 专精特新, roughly “specialised, refined, distinctive, novel”. In English it reads like a ballroom-dancing judging sheet, but the meaning is businesslike: a narrow focus, done carefully, with something unique and a commitment to innovation.

It works as a ladder. At the bottom are innovative small and medium-sized firms; above them, provincial-level specialised firms; above those, national-level little giants selected by the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology. At the top sit the “manufacturing champions”, established global leaders in their niche.

The criteria were drafted in 2018 and the first batch, 248 companies, was named in 2019. The second batch had 1,744. The seventh, announced in 2025, had 3,482. By the count of the Australian Strategic Policy Institute (ASPI), which keeps an open database, more than 19,000 companies have appeared across the seven batches from 2019 to 2025. In mid-2025 the ministry put the number of active little giants at 14,600 at the end of 2024, above the target originally set for 2025.

Building a network of giants takes twenty years. A network of little giants grows faster, because each one needs less room.

### What it takes

The requirements, set out in ministry rules in force since August 2022, are more specific than you might expect.

A company must have worked in its niche for at least three years. Its core business must provide at least 70% of revenue and have grown by an average of at least 5% a year over the past two years. Its debt must not exceed 70% of assets. Its main product must hold at least 10% of the national market in its segment.

Then comes innovation. A firm with revenue above 100 million yuan must spend at least 3% of revenue on R&D in each of the past two years; one with 50–100 million, at least 6%. The smallest, under 50 million, qualify differently: at least 80 million yuan of equity raised, at least 30 million spent on R&D, and at least half of staff in research. Add an in-house R&D unit and at least two top-category intellectual property rights tied to the main product and already earning money.

There is also a fast track, for instance a national science and technology award in the past three years.

Note the combination. Not the biggest, not the fastest-growing, but narrow and dominant in a niche. Ten percent of a national market in a narrow segment is not modesty. It is a monopoly too polite to say so.

### What the status brings

The status is more than a plaque by the entrance. According to ASPI, firms get tax credits, reduced certification fees, easier access to credit, promotion at trade fairs at home and abroad, preferential state financing and research partnerships with state institutions. Provinces and cities add their own measures and compete over head counts. Hunan, for example, reported in December 2025 that it had 593 little giants, ninth in the country, including 77 new ones in the seventh batch.

When officials compete on the number of certified firms, there is a temptation to chase volume. So the status is reviewed periodically, and some firms drop out.

In China, a medal for diligence is not issued for life. It has to be defended like a boxing title.

### The Beijing Stock Exchange: a shop window for the small

In November 2021 mainland China opened its third stock exchange, in Beijing, designed from the start for innovative small and mid-sized firms. By its fifth anniversary in September 2026 it listed 339 companies with a combined market value of about 840 billion yuan. According to CGTN, more than 60% are national-level little giants, and their R&D intensity and invention patents are more than double the average for China’s large industrial companies.

That matters. The programme offers not just perks but a path to capital: first the status, then the listing, then the money for the next workshop.

### How to spot a little giant in the corridor

1.  The status plaque hangs by the door, sometimes next to the older provincial one.
2.  The showroom is smaller than a giant’s, but someone can take apart and explain every exhibit.
3.  The CTO comes to the meeting in person and is in no hurry to leave.
4.  Ask who their customers are and they say “everyone in the industry”. Unfortunately, that is true.
5.  You had never heard the company’s name. A year later you will not have forgotten it.

### Why foreign buyers should care

For anyone sourcing in China, the little giant list is a ready-made filter. These firms have been vetted for specialisation, market share, financial health and innovation. That is no guarantee of the quality of a given shipment, but it is a good sign you are dealing with a genuine manufacturer rather than a reseller.

There is a flip side. Many of these companies work in technologies the state considers sensitive: semiconductors, machine tools, materials, industrial automation components. That is why they are tracked abroad, and why some topics will be off the table in negotiations. Better to know that before the meeting than at the second one.

And a third point. A little giant is often stronger than a giant in its niche and weaker everywhere else: a three-person export department, English at the level of technical manuals, and little experience with foreign buyers. That is not a flaw, but it is something to prepare for.

Such companies rarely appear on tourist itineraries. They have no brand museum and no glossy video. But they are exactly the ones to visit if what you need is not an impressive plant but the right component. Closed visits to Chinese companies across many industries are what Global Tech Tour expeditions are for.

### The bottom line

For twenty years China taught the world that industry means scale. The little giants programme makes a different point: scale stands on precision. A huge plant assembles the car. Thousands of small ones make sure it actually drives.

Great companies grow out of small ones. Little giants are the ones in no hurry to grow up.
