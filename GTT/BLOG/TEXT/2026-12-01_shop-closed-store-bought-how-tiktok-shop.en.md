In October 2023 TikTok shut its shop in Indonesia. In January 2024 it became the owner of one of Indonesia’s largest marketplaces. Less than four months passed between the two.

If a person did that, we would say they slammed the door and came back with the keys to the flat next door.

In corporate language this is called “adapting the business model to local regulation”. It sounds duller, but it is the same thing. Here is how it happened and what it teaches anyone entering South-East Asian markets.

### Why Indonesia

Indonesia is the region’s largest economy and its most populous country. According to e-Conomy SEA 2025, the annual report by Google, Temasek and Bain & Company, its digital economy approached US$100 billion in gross merchandise value (GMV) in 2025, up 14%. E-commerce is the largest part, at around US$71 billion.

Most important for our story is video. The same report says video commerce transactions in Indonesia grew 90% in a year to 2.6 billion, and the number of sellers grew 75% to about 800,000. The average order is US$4.50 to US$6. Fashion, accessories, beauty and personal care make up almost half of video commerce GMV.

A six-dollar order is not an impulse buy. It is a lifestyle. Shoppers watch live streams the way they used to watch soap operas, except the soap opera never sold them lipstick in real time.

### September 2023: no shopping on social media

By 2023 TikTok Shop had become a noticeable player in Indonesia, and not everyone was pleased. Small traders in traditional markets complained that live streams with cheap goods were taking their customers. The government acted.

On 26 September 2023 the trade ministry issued Regulation No. 31/2023. Its aim was to separate social media from e-commerce: a social network can promote products but cannot process transactions. The regulation also barred platforms from facilitating sales of imported goods worth US$100 or less per unit and barred marketplaces from selling their own products.

At 5 p.m. Jakarta time on 4 October 2023, TikTok switched off its shopping features in Indonesia.

The regulator’s message was simple: you may look, but you may not buy. For a platform where people had been buying what they watched, that is like allowing a restaurant to show the menu but not to serve food.

### January 2024: if you cannot be a social network, become a marketplace

The answer came quickly. In December 2023 TikTok agreed with Indonesia’s GoTo group to take control of Tokopedia, one of the country’s largest marketplaces. The deal closed on 31 January 2024: TikTok took 75.01% of Tokopedia and GoTo kept 24.99%. TikTok paid US$840 million for new shares, within a total investment agreement of US$1.5 billion.

TikTok Shop in Indonesia carried on together with Tokopedia, as part of a locally licensed marketplace rather than a shop inside a social network.

That is a useful lesson. The Chinese company did not argue with the rule or wait for it to be lifted. It changed shape so that the rule no longer applied to it. Not getting around the law, but moving into the part of the law where you are allowed to operate.

### The result: 2025 figures

According to Momentum Works (Ecommerce in Southeast Asia 2026, as reported by Bisnis.com), TikTok Shop and Tokopedia together held 38% of Indonesian e-commerce in 2025, up from 34% a year earlier. Shopee remained the leader with 54%, Lazada had 6% and Blibli 3%. Momentum Works puts the whole market at about US$57.7 billion GMV.

Note the arithmetic. In 2024 Tokopedia and TikTok Shop were counted separately (23% and 11%); now they are counted together. The combined share grew, but Shopee grew more. The biggest losers were neither the Chinese nor the Singaporean platform but the local second tier: Bukalapak, which had 10% a year earlier, no longer appears among the major players.

(The gap between Google’s US$71 billion and Momentum Works’ US$57.7 billion is not a mistake but a difference in methodology. Analysts argue about numbers as passionately as live-stream hosts argue about discounts.)

### October 2025: a reminder of who regulates whom

The regulatory story did not end there. On 3 October 2025 Indonesia’s Ministry of Communication and Digital Affairs (Komdigi) suspended TikTok’s registration as an electronic system operator. According to the ministry, the company had provided only partial data on live streams and their monetisation during the protests of 25–30 August 2025. The app itself remained available.

The next day, 4 October, TikTok handed over the requested data and the registration was restored.

One day is a short time. The lesson lasts longer: in a market where you depend on a licence, the regulator’s requests are not a negotiation but the terms of doing business.

### What this story teaches

**1. In South-East Asia, regulation is not background. It is a market participant.** Indonesia showed it is willing to change the rules quickly to protect local sellers. A platform whose model rests on a single licence carries more risk than it seems.

**2. Buying a local player brings status, not just share.** Tokopedia gave TikTok local infrastructure, a seller base and a legal form of operation.

**3. Content sells, but warehouses and payments deliver.** Video brings in the buyer; logistics, returns and payments keep them. That takes a marketplace, not just a live stream.

**4. Local small business is not an opponent but a political factor.** The 2023 decision grew out of complaints from traditional market traders. Any model, Chinese or otherwise, has to explain what it offers local sellers, not only buyers.

**5. A six-dollar basket is a market too.** Anyone waiting for big-ticket purchases in Indonesia will wait a long time. Those who can earn on billions of small ones already do.

### The bottom line

In Indonesia TikTok learned a lesson every platform abroad learns sooner or later: you can be the country’s most popular app and still find yourself at a locked door. What opens it is not an algorithm but a local partner, a local licence and patience.

Indonesian shoppers still watch the stream and tap “buy”. Most of them never noticed that in two years the shop closed, changed hands and reopened.

Good business is the kind whose difficulties the customer never sees.
