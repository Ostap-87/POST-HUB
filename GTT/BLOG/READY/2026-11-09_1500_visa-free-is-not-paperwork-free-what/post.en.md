Preparing for a trip to China used to start at the visa centre: forms, a photo on a white background, a letter from your employer, a queue, and a quiet hope that your passport would come back before the flight rather than after it.

China has since opened visa-free entry to a long list of countries, and many travellers now assume there is nothing left to prepare. That is almost true. On a business trip, “almost” costs exactly one ticket on the next flight.

The visa may be gone. The paperwork is not.

Below is what we at Global Tech Tour check with our expedition participants about a month before departure. One caveat, which we will repeat at the end: entry rules change, and the final word always belongs to official sources, namely China’s Ministry of Foreign Affairs, the National Immigration Administration (NIA) and the Chinese embassy or consulate in your country. Everything here reflects the situation as of October 2026, with a focus on our Russian-speaking participants. Rules for your passport may differ.

### 1. Visa-free entry: what exactly changed

China has been steadily widening unilateral and bilateral visa-free entry. For Russian citizens holding ordinary passports, a 30-day visa-free trial began on 15 September 2025. It was due to end on 14 September 2026, but on 20 May 2026 the Foreign Ministry spokesperson announced an extension until 31 December 2027.

The permitted purposes are business, tourism, visiting family and friends, exchanges and transit. A business expedition with factory visits fits that description.

Work does not. If someone on your team is going not to look but to install equipment, commission a line or train on the shop floor, that is a different conversation and, usually, a different visa. Not every border officer can tell “watching an installation” from “doing one”, but every border officer can ask. Have the answer ready.

Also note that 30 days means 30 days, not “about a month”. If the programme, follow-up meetings and a trade fair add up to five weeks, do the maths before you fly.

### 2. 240-hour transit: the side door

There is a second route: visa-free transit for up to 240 hours, or 10 days. According to the NIA, it covers citizens of 55 countries and works through 60 ports of entry in 24 provinces, autonomous regions and municipalities.

The condition is strict. You must be travelling from one country to a third country via China, with a confirmed onward ticket with a date and a seat. Moscow to Shanghai and back is not transit, however much you would like it to be. Moscow to Shanghai to Bangkok is. You also have to stay within the permitted regions.

For mixed delegations, where participants hold different passports or continue into Southeast Asia, this route is worth keeping in mind.

### 3. The arrival card now lives on your phone

Since 20 November 2025, foreigners can complete the arrival card online before arriving in China, via the NIA website, the NIA 12367 app, or mini-programs in [WeChat](/en/companies/wechat) and Alipay. Paper cards are still available at the border, but the queue at the little desk with pens on chains is longer than it looks from the plane.

Five minutes at home saves twenty in the arrivals hall. It is the best exchange rate you will get all trip.

### 4. Registration: hotels do it, apartments don’t

Foreigners in China must register their place of stay. If you are in a hotel, the hotel handles it when it takes your passport at check-in. If you stay in a private apartment, with a partner or with friends, the local police must be notified within 24 hours of arrival. That is the guest’s or the host’s responsibility, and “we didn’t know” is not one of the boxes on the form.

For business trips, hotels are simply easier. Not because of the breakfast (although sometimes), but because one task gets done without you.

### 5. Getting around: the train is faster than you think

By the end of 2025, China’s high-speed rail network passed 50,000 kilometres. In practice, between many industrial cities the train beats the plane door to door: stations are closer to the centre, security is quicker and delays are rarer.

Train tickets and domestic flights are booked against your passport. The official railway site, 12306, has an English version where foreigners register with a passport. The name on the ticket must match the passport exactly. A transliteration you typed “the way I usually spell it” may not, and then the gate will not recognise you. Gates in China rarely take anyone’s word for it.

When a programme covers three or four cities in a week, we plan transfers so they do not eat into visits. The costliest routing mistake is not a lost hour. It is a lost meeting that took six months to arrange.

### One-month checklist

1.  Passport validity and blank pages. Confirm requirements with your airline and the consulate.
2.  Your purpose of travel matches the visa-free terms. Anyone going to work needs visa advice.
3.  Length of stay: comfortably inside the limit.
4.  Arrival card completed online.
5.  Hotels booked for every night. If you stay with a partner, agree who notifies the police and when.
6.  Train and flight tickets with your name spelled exactly as in your passport.
7.  Travel insurance for the whole trip.
8.  The belief that “we’ll sort it out when we get there”. No need to check this one. Just leave it at home.

### Why a month, not a week

A week before departure, you can find out that the rules have changed. A month before, you can still do something about it. China’s entry rules have changed several times in recent years, mostly in the traveller’s favour, but “simplified” and “abolished” are different words.

So the last step on our checklist never changes: open the websites of the Chinese embassy and the National Immigration Administration and compare everything above with what they say today. We do it before every expedition, and we recommend you do the same.

A good trip does not start on the plane. It starts when, by the time you board, there is nothing left to worry about.
