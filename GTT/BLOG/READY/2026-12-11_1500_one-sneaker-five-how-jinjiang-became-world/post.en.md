Look at your trainers. Not at the brand: at the shoes themselves. With luck, there is a label on the tongue or insole saying where they were made. Without luck, there is nothing, and even then the odds that they came from one small town in Fujian province are surprisingly good.

The town is Chendai. It belongs to Jinjiang, a county-level city within Quanzhou. According to Xinhua, roughly one in every five pairs of sports shoes in the world comes from here.

One in five. If you jog in the park in the morning, at least one of the five people running past you is probably wearing Chendai. They don’t know it. Chendai doesn’t know it either, but it has its suspicions.

### Fourteen neighbours and 2,000 yuan

The story begins the way many Chinese industrial stories do: with very little money and a great deal of stubbornness.

In 1979, a villager named Lin Tuqiu and 14 neighbours each put in 2,000 yuan and opened a garment and footwear factory. For a family at the time, that was serious money. In today’s Chendai, it might buy a roadside billboard. Which, quite possibly, someone local also made.

Things moved fast. By the mid-1980s, Xinhua reports, Chendai had more than 700 township enterprises and combined industrial and agricultural output of 110.27 million yuan, making it Fujian’s first “100-million-yuan town”.

It helped that, as the local saying goes, nine households out of ten have relatives overseas. Family abroad supplied contacts, samples, first orders and first machines. Capital came not from banks but from family correspondence.

Good manufacturing does not start with a factory. It starts with a cousin in Manila who knows what people are wearing this season.

### The crisis that made the brands

For years Chendai lived on contract work, making shoes to other people’s orders under other people’s logos. The model worked as long as the customers did. In 1997 the Asian financial crisis hit exports, and many factories that worked only for foreign brands closed.

The survivors drew a conclusion business schools would later call strategy: if your fate depends on someone else’s logo, get your own. That is where the region’s sports brands came from, the ones Xinhua names as leading national players: [Anta](/en/companies/anta), Xtep and 361°. We have already written about Anta separately, so here it is just a mention; the company comes from these parts and has done more than anyone to prove a factory can become a brand.

First you make shoes for someone else’s name. Then you realise a name is also a product, just made on different machines.

### The cluster in numbers

Today Chendai has more than 7,000 shoe companies and related businesses, 421 of them above the official statistical size threshold. It turns out more than a billion pairs of sports shoes a year, with finished output worth over 50 billion yuan. All on 38.84 square kilometres, about two-thirds the land area of Manhattan.

Inside the town sits the Jinjiang International Shoe Materials City: more than a thousand component traders on over 1,000 mu (about 67 hectares), built with an investment of 8 billion yuan. This is one reason the cluster is hard to copy. A shoemaker can find a sole, mesh, glue, laces and someone who knows how to put them together more cheaply, all in one day.

Jinjiang as a whole, China Daily reports, has a footwear and apparel industry worth over 300 billion yuan, more than 330,000 registered businesses, over 100,000 private firms and 53 listed companies. In national rankings of county economies it sits in the top five: fourth for basic competitiveness, third on the comprehensive development index.

For a county, that is a lot. For the shoe capital of the world, it is about right.

### What it takes to make one pair in five

1.  Fourteen neighbours willing to take a risk.
2.  Relatives abroad willing to give advice.
3.  A financial crisis to explain why you need your own brand.
4.  A components market where you can assemble a sneaker from what is on the stalls.
5.  Forty-odd years. The scarcest component of all, and not for sale in the materials market.

### Robots on the shoe line

Footwear has long been hard to automate: soft materials, complex shapes, lots of manual assembly. That is exactly why it is usually the first industry to move to low-wage countries. Chendai has answered in two ways.

The first is automation. Xinhua says smart production lines in Chendai need about 30% fewer workers than traditional ones. Not the lights-out factory of promotional videos, but in an industry where labour cost decides almost everything, 30% is a big difference.

The second is engineering. Xtep set up its own research lab, X-lab, back in 2015. Running shoes now sell on midsole foam, weight and energy return rather than colour. This is no longer an industry where the cheapest always wins.

They used to ask how many pairs per shift. Now they ask how many grams the sole weighs. The question got lighter. The answer got more expensive.

### What it means for business

Jinjiang is a useful example of a cluster that grew from the bottom up, without a single big investor or a master plan at the start. No venture-fund city hall as in Hefei, no Singaporean textbook as in Suzhou. Just neighbours, relatives, a crisis and a great many shoes.

For companies sourcing footwear or components, or working in light industry, a few practical points follow. A cluster is not only factories but also a materials market, logistics and people, which is why its prices and lead times are hard to match elsewhere. A Chinese manufacturer that worked on contract yesterday may own a brand, and compete with its customer, tomorrow. And automation in light industry moves more slowly than in electronics, but it moves, and that changes the economics of every order.

On Global Tech Tour business expeditions, we like places like this because you can watch an industry being made. At a big plant you see the result. In a cluster you see the process: dozens of companies, each doing its part, and a market where they all meet.

Some forty years ago, fifteen people put in 2,000 yuan each. Today their town shoes one runner in five on the planet. The runners rarely think about it. They are busy running.
