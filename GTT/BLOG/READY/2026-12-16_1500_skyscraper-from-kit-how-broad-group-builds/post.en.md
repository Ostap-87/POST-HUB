Every country has its jokes about construction. The building takes ten years, opens with defects, then takes another ten years to finish. Somewhere a site manager gets a medal.

In Changsha, capital of Hunan province, one company has spent years trying to tell the opposite joke. The building goes up in days, the defects stay in the factory, and nobody seems to have got a medal. The company is Broad Group, and its story is a good guide to where construction speed actually comes from, and where it runs into people with rubber stamps.

### Air-conditioning engineers who decided to build

Broad Group is best known for absorption chillers, air-conditioning systems that run on heat (gas or waste steam) rather than electricity. Founder Zhang Yue has been in that business since the late 1980s. Construction became a second line: the company decided to make buildings in its own factories, the way it makes equipment.

The logic is an engineer’s logic. On a building site, people work in mud and rain and wait for deliveries. In a factory, they work indoors, to a schedule, with quality control. So move as much work as possible into the factory and leave only assembly for the site.

Broad could hang this on the factory wall: a building starts on an assembly line, not in a hole in the ground.

### The records: 15 days, 19 days, under 29 hours

The first big demonstration was the T30 hotel: 30 storeys in 15 days. The time-lapse went viral, and the industry started talking about “China speed” as a method rather than a fluke.

Then came Mini Sky City in Changsha: 57 storeys in 19 days, about three floors a day. According to Mingtiandi, that meant roughly 186,000 square metres, 800 apartments and office space for 4,000 people. It was originally designed for 97 storeys but cut back to meet aviation rules. Assembling it next to Broad’s own factory, the company said, saved about 15,000 truck trips.

In 2021 Broad released another video: the 10-storey Living Building, assembled in 28 hours and 45 minutes. This time the technology was different. Modules the size of a 40-foot shipping container (12.19 × 2.44 × 3 metres) arrive from the factory with wiring and finishes installed. Walls fold down into floor plates, windows and balconies fold out. The core is Broad’s patented B-Core slab: two sheets of stainless steel with hundreds of steel tubes between them. The company claims it is ten times lighter and a hundred times stronger than conventional floor slabs. That is a manufacturer’s claim and should be read as one.

In May 2026, media reported a 26-storey Broad residential building in Xiangyin county, with 208 apartments, assembled in about five days. The modules are bolted together with no on-site concrete or welding, and the building can reportedly be taken apart and moved. The company talks of a service life of over a thousand years. Nobody can check that yet, including the company.

### What happened to the 220-storey tower

Every good story has a chapter that doesn’t make the video. In 2012 Broad announced Sky City, a 220-storey modular tower in Changsha meant to be the world’s tallest building, to be assembled within months.

In 2013 the project stalled. Local officials decided it lacked the necessary approvals, and the press raised safety questions. Later reports said the foundations had been turned into a fish farm. Mini Sky City was, in a sense, the answer: if they won’t let us build 220, we’ll build 57.

The lesson is not about technology. Assembly speed is not project speed. You can put a building up in days. You cannot get it approved in days, especially if it’s billed as the tallest in the world and sits next to a watermelon field.

### Why it works

**The factory does most of the work.** Every minute on site costs more than a minute in the plant: weather, logistics, subcontractors. Modular construction moves finishing, building services and quality control indoors.

**Standardisation.** Modules of one size are easier to make, ship and install. The 40-foot container format is deliberate: the box fits on a truck, train or ship without special permits.

**Dry assembly.** With no concrete on site, there is no waiting for it to cure. Bolts are tightened straight away.

### Why it doesn’t work everywhere

Prefabrication is not new. Anyone who has lived in a post-war prefab block knows its drawbacks. China’s version is far more advanced, but the limits rhyme.

**Architecture.** The module sets the grid. Open plans, unusual facades and long spans are possible, but every departure from the standard eats into the gain.

**Logistics.** Modules have to be delivered. The further the site from the factory, the thinner the savings.

**Codes.** Building rules, fire safety and certification differ by country. A system approved in Changsha is not automatically approved elsewhere.

**The market.** Modular building pays when the order book is full. When property cools, as China’s has in recent years, the factory has fewer orders and the economics sag.

### The industry is bigger than Broad

Broad is the most famous player, not the only one. China has backed prefabricated, or “industrialised”, construction for years. According to the Ministry of Housing, 740 million square metres of prefabricated buildings were started in 2021. Industry estimates put their share of new construction at about 26% in 2022. Most of it is not stainless-steel modules but precast concrete and steel frames.

Broad is the shop window. The industry is the warehouse behind it. Records look good on video, but the economics are made by thousands of ordinary plants turning out wall panels and staircases.

### Four questions to ask at a modular plant

1.  What share of the work is done in the factory and what share on site?
2.  How many modules does the line produce per day, and how many go into one project?
3.  How far can modules travel before it stops making sense?
4.  How long do approvals take compared with assembly?

The fourth question usually gets the longest answer. And the most honest one.

### What to take home

At Global Tech Tour we run business [expeditions](/en/expeditions) to China and closed visits to companies across many industries. For builders and developers, modular production is interesting not as a “building in a day” trick but as a case study in moving work from the site to the factory. The same principle applies to furniture, shipbuilding, commercial kitchens and equipment assembly: the more you do in advance, the fewer surprises on site.

There is no neat ending. Broad proved a building can go up fast. It also showed that fast is not the same as immediately. Somewhere in Changsha lie the foundations of what might have been the world’s tallest building and became a fish pond instead. The fish, we hear, are content. They didn’t need planning permission.
