Between Singapore and Malaysia’s Johor lies a strait about a kilometre wide and one of the busiest land borders in the world. For years people crossed it one way to work and the other way to live and shop. In the last few years, data started making the trip too.

Unlike people, data does not sit in traffic on the Causeway. It got its own buildings instead. A lot of them.

This piece looks at how a border state known for oil palm plantations became one of South-East Asia’s main server rooms, what Chinese companies have to do with it, and why the next chapter will be written less by engineers than by the water utility.

### The moratorium that built the neighbour

It started with a ban. In 2019 Singapore effectively stopped approving new data centres: the island is small, land is scarce, power is expensive, and servers consume it without restraint. The pause lasted until 2022.

Demand did not pause. Cloud providers, social networks and banks still wanted capacity close to Singapore’s cables, exchange and lawyers. “Close” turned out to mean across the strait.

Nothing grows a neighbour’s economy like a ban at home. Singapore set out to save electricity and ended up creating a competitor that has plenty of it.

### The numbers: from palm oil to gigawatts

Data centres are measured in megawatts, the power they draw from the grid. According to DC Byte figures cited by DatacenterDynamics, in July 2025 Johor had 487 MW live, 324 MW under construction, 1.4 GW committed (land and power secured, construction not started) and about 3.4 GW at an early stage. That is a pipeline of just over 5 GW.

For comparison, Singapore itself had 1,072 MW live in January 2025. Its neighbour already runs at roughly half of Singapore’s live capacity, with a far larger pipeline.

Demand keeps pace. Knight Frank counted 260 MW of take-up in Johor in the first half of 2025, nearly two-thirds of it from social media companies. Vacancy stood at 1.1%. That is less a market than a waiting list.

By the end of 2024, Knight Frank estimated, Johor accounted for about 80% of Malaysia’s data centre IT capacity. And in the first half of 2026, data centre and cloud projects made up RM95.8 billion of approved investment, close to 44% of the national total, according to Malaysia’s investment agency MIDA.

When nearly half of a country’s approved investment goes into windowless buildings, it is no longer an industry. It is the landscape.

### The Chinese accent

Here is what makes Johor particularly interesting for anyone watching China.

The two largest data centre operators in Malaysia by built capacity at the end of 2024 were Bridge Data Centres and DayOne. Both have Chinese roots.

**DayOne** is the former international arm of GDS Holdings, one of China’s largest data centre operators, spun off as a separate company in January 2025. Its anchor campuses are in the Nusajaya and Kempas tech parks in Johor. In June 2025 it raised RM15 billion (about US$3.6 billion) in syndicated financing, its largest loan to date, from a group of banks including OCBC, DBS, UOB, CIMB and Maybank.

**Bridge Data Centres** is a subsidiary of ChinData, which is controlled by Bain Capital. Its MY06 campus in Sedenak Tech Park opened in 2022 with 110 MW, and its anchor tenant is [ByteDance](/en/companies/bytedance), the owner of TikTok.

In 2024 ByteDance announced RM10 billion (about US$2.1 billion) for a regional AI hub in Malaysia, of which RM1.5 billion, according to investment minister Tengku Zafrul Aziz, was earmarked for expanding data centres. Reuters also lists [Tencent](/en/companies/tencent), [Huawei](/en/companies/huawei) and Alibaba among Chinese investors in Malaysian data centres.

The geography is striking: a company with Chinese roots builds a server farm in Malaysia, financed by Singaporean banks, for a Chinese-owned app used around the world. Globalisation did not die. It moved to Johor, where land is cheaper.

### Chips, or why the server room now has passport control

AI data centres run on accelerators, and mostly American ones. That puts Johor between two large neighbours.

In June 2025 the Wall Street Journal reported that Chinese AI firms were flying hard drives full of data to Malaysia to train models on Nvidia chips in local data centres. In July 2025 Malaysia introduced permits for exports, trans-shipments and transits of US high-performance chips. As Reuters noted, the rules still allow such chips to be used inside the country, but the industry expects scrutiny to tighten.

For business, the takeaway is simple. Johor is not a loophole. It is a normal jurisdiction with a regulator, and a strategy built on a grey zone is a poor one. Servers value stability even more than people do.

### Water and power: the next chapter

A data centre needs three things: land, electricity and water for cooling. Johor has plenty of land. The other two are harder.

Government figures reported by Malay Mail put peak data centre demand in Peninsular Malaysia at 849 MW in December 2025, projected to reach 4,811 MW by 2030. Imagine plugging several large cities into the grid within five years, cities that never sleep, never go on holiday and never switch off the lights.

The authorities responded. In November 2025 Johor stopped considering applications for Tier 1 and Tier 2 data centres, which are particularly water-hungry, and shifted its focus to Tier 3 and Tier 4. New facilities that need water must use reclaimed water rather than the drinking supply. At that point the state had approved 51 projects: 17 operating, 11 under construction and 23 not yet started. Nationally, new projects must show secured power and water, and meet efficiency caps (PUE) of 1.6 for colocation and 1.4 for hyperscale facilities.

Data was supposed to be the new oil. It turns out the scarce resource is water.

### Singapore’s own Shenzhen

In January 2025 Singapore and Malaysia signed the agreement on the Johor-Singapore Special Economic Zone (JS-SEZ). Eligible companies in the zone can get a 5% corporate tax rate for up to 15 years, and lawyers list data centres among the sectors likely to benefit. The RTS Link between Johor Bahru and Singapore’s Woodlands is scheduled to open by the end of 2026: a five-minute ride instead of hours on the Causeway. Check the launch status before you travel.

The model is familiar. An expensive financial centre plus a neighbouring territory with land, power and people: that is roughly how Hong Kong and Shenzhen grew together. Here the border is a strait, not a river, and what crosses it is fibre rather than trucks full of electronics.

### What this means for business

1.  **It is a market, not one player’s construction site.** American hyperscalers, Chinese operators and Asian funds all compete in Johor. It is a good place to see how Chinese companies compete outside China.
2.  **Chinese operators are learning to be international.** DayOne and Bridge show how businesses with Chinese roots restructure ownership and financing to operate in third markets. A useful case for anyone planning their own move into South-East Asia.
3.  **Regulation is part of the product.** Water, power, chips: the rules change quickly, and the winners build with headroom on all three.
4.  **If your plan is simply “cheaper servers”, read up on reclaimed water first.** That is part of the business plan too.

### The bottom line

Johor shows how fast Asia’s map is being redrawn. Ten years ago the conversation was about plantations and cheaper housing for people working in Singapore. Today the state is full of buildings where Singapore, Beijing and California share the same rack.

Data knows no borders. It does, however, know exactly where electricity is cheaper.
