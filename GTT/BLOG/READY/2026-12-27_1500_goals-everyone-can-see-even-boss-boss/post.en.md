In most companies, senior management’s goals are classified. The boss knows them, the deputy knows them, and so does the assistant who formatted the slides. Everyone else infers them from circumstantial evidence, mainly from who got called in on Monday.

[ByteDance](/en/companies/bytedance), the company behind TikTok and Douyin, went the other way. Any employee, from intern to founder, can open any other employee’s goals. The founder’s included.

Imagine logging in on Monday morning, reading what the CEO is working on this cycle, and having questions. And discovering you are allowed to ask them.

### How the OKRs work

OKRs, objectives and key results, did not originate in China: the method grew up at Intel and became famous at Google. ByteDance took it and pushed one element to the limit: openness.

According to Chinese business media, ByteDance sets goals every two months. Teams draft their own OKRs, then align them with neighbouring departments and with leadership priorities. It is neither a pure top-down quota nor a free-for-all, more a negotiation where everyone holds the same cards face up. A new hire can look up any colleague’s goals, including those of founder Zhang Yiming. Cycle reviews feed into decisions about products and about who leads what.

Two months is not long. Quarterly goals often fade into memories by month three. Two-month goals do not have time to go stale, nor to let anyone coast.

Transparency is knowing what your boss is working on. Real transparency is your boss knowing that you know.

### “Context, not control”

The whole system rests on a principle Zhang Yiming borrowed from Netflix’s culture deck: context, not control. Give people enough information about what the company is doing and why, and they will make good decisions without three sign-offs. Withhold it, and no number of sign-offs will help.

Open OKRs are a context tool. Employees see what neighbouring teams are stuck on, what leadership wants and where the product is heading, without waylaying a manager in the corridor.

The principle shapes communication too. Zhang once said the company encourages group chats and direct contact between departments and considers one-on-one communication inefficient. In 2021, with more than 100,000 people on the payroll, he used an anniversary speech to warn against “big-company disease” and against corporate jargon.

Telling 100,000 people to “speak plainly” sounds a bit like corporate jargon itself. Still, nobody has found a better way to put it.

### Feishu: a messenger that became a management system

Context has to live somewhere. ByteDance built its own workplace suite, Feishu, sold outside China as Lark: messaging, docs, sheets, calendar, video calls and OKRs in one window.

The feature list matters less than the habits. Goals sit inside the workspace: visible from a colleague’s profile, open to comments, linkable to someone else’s goal, taggable for joint work.

Meetings deserve a mention. Feishu’s own site describes a format borrowed from Amazon: the organiser circulates a document, and for the first 15 minutes everyone reads in silence and comments in the margins. Discussion then covers only the comments. Meetings are often recorded for colleagues in other time zones.

Fifteen minutes of silence in a meeting. In many offices it would be the most productive part of the week.

Feishu is also sold to outside customers. In September 2024 its head, David Xie, said annual recurring revenue would top US$300 million, up 50% on the year before. In summer 2026, the South China Morning Post reported, ByteDance reorganised Lark, merging its product team with the group behind the Doubao chatbot and moving sales and support into its Volcano Engine cloud arm. The management tool became part of the AI bet.

### 2026: when context had to be written into the rules

The most interesting chapter came in June 2026. CEO Liang Rubo sent staff updated leadership principles, the first revision in four years. As 36Kr summarised it, the letter described a state where “everyone is busy but there is no actual output”.

“Context, not control” went from an onboarding talking point to a hard requirement: it is now a criterion for manager promotions and annual reviews, and managers who hoard context and run everything through approvals may see their authority trimmed. A new principle asks managers to “go deep into the front line”, on the logic that you cannot trust a team without understanding its work. Others urge setting ambitious goals and doing “high-level” work rather than chasing monthly numbers.

36Kr’s commentators note, fairly, that delegation often fails because managers feel safer holding the reins. The reform is a start, not a result.

Even where goals are visible to all, approvals grow back over time. Bureaucracy is like damp: it appears wherever nobody has opened a window for a while.

### Five signs you run on control, not context

1.  Only the head of the department knows the department’s goals.
2.  To learn a neighbouring team’s priorities, you have to buy them lunch.
3.  Meetings open with fifteen minutes of slides nobody read in advance.
4.  A one-day decision takes a week to approve.
5.  Checking this list requires your manager’s sign-off.

### What you can borrow without ByteDance’s scale

Copying ByteDance wholesale makes no sense: its scale, money and pace are its own. But a few things travel to almost any team.

Open up goals. Not necessarily company-wide on day one: start with managers seeing each other’s goals and teams seeing their manager’s. The hard part is not the software. It is being willing to show your goals to people who might ask questions.

Shorten the cycle. If quarterly goals are forgotten by month three, try two months.

Write before you meet. A two-page memo and fifteen minutes of silent reading discipline a meeting better than any rulebook.

Judge managers on whether they share context. ByteDance got there after more than a decade and over 100,000 employees. You can start sooner.

Practices like these are best seen in person rather than in summaries: what a working day looks like, how meetings run, what an employee sees on screen. Global Tech Tour’s corporate training and business expeditions are built around that question: not “how do they do it?” but “what would work for us?”

### The bottom line

ByteDance became one of the world’s most valuable private tech companies, and open goals played a part, if not the leading one. The deeper lesson is that transparency cannot be introduced once. It has to be reintroduced every few years, as an organisation quietly grows new walls.

Opening up your goals is easy. Not closing them again is the hard part.
