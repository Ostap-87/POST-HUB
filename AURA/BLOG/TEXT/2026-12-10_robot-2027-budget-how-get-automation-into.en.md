December is when companies decide the fate of the following year. The CFO becomes the most popular person in the building, and “it isn’t in the budget” is heard more often than “happy holidays”. A robot left out of the budget in December usually exists next year only in slide decks. Sometimes in two.

The good news: to get a robot into the budget you do not need the exact model or the final price. You need to describe the problem, the money and the timeline properly.

### The problem first, the budget line second

Budget committees do not buy robots. They buy solutions. “Buy a cobot” sounds like spending. “Close the gap of two operators on the packing line we have failed to hire for six months” sounds like a solution. It is the same cobot.

Describe the problem in terms finance understands: the operation, current headcount, the full cost of that labour including taxes, turnover and overtime, scrap and downtime, and what happens if nothing is done. That last point matters most. Not having a robot also costs money; it is just scattered across budget lines where nobody sees it.

The backdrop in Russia helps. According to figures from Kept and Innopolis University cited by Rossiyskaya Gazeta, Russian industry had about 40 robots per 10,000 workers in 2025, up 36% in a year. The 2030 target is 145, and the national project “Means of Production and Automation” plans more than 40 billion roubles for 2026–2028. Robotisation has become a national goal, which is a useful argument in any budget meeting.

### Payback: three scenarios instead of one number

A single payback figure makes CFOs suspicious. Three scenarios earn respect: **cautious** (slower ramp-up, lower utilisation, worse exchange rate), **base** (to plan) and **optimistic** (two or three shifts at full load).

An illustrative example, not taken from any real project: if the whole project costs 10 units and base-case savings are 4 units a year, payback is about 2.5 years. In the cautious case, at 2.5 units a year, it is about 4 years. If even the cautious case fits the company’s payback threshold, the budget meeting gets shorter.

Count the whole project, not just the robot: tooling, integration, shipping, duties, conformity assessment, commissioning, training and first-year spares. A robot budget without those lines is like a renovation estimate that only lists wallpaper.

### Timing: December is already late for January

The most common mistake is budgeting as if the robot can be bought off the shelf in the first week of January. From China it takes longer.

The chain looks roughly like this: model selection and spec sign-off; contract and deposit; production or assembly at the factory and pre-shipment testing of the unit; transport, which by our benchmarks takes about 15–25 days by road, 15–35 days by rail and 35–45 days by sea via Russian Far East ports; customs clearance; installation and commissioning.

An illustrative schedule: problem defined and budget approved in December, contract signed in January, Chinese New Year (which falls in early February in 2027) pushing factory work back a couple of weeks, and a realistic launch in the second quarter. That is fine, as long as it is in the plan and not discovered in March.

On Chinese New Year: factories shut for the holiday and get busier before and after it. An order that can be placed before the holiday should be.

### Currency: a risk you can describe

Buying from China almost always involves a foreign currency. A rouble budget approved in December may drift from reality by the time payment is due. The options are familiar: include a currency buffer and label it as such; split payment into stages and price each at a conservative rate; ask your bank about hedging tools. The key is not to hide the risk. A budget that names currency risk looks more honest and needs re-approval less often.

### Leasing: your choice, we model both

Aura Robotics does not lease or rent robots. If a company prefers not to pay in full upfront, it can lease the equipment through a leasing company of its choice, and we then calculate payback for both options: outright purchase and leasing. Side by side, the two calculations show the committee what matters more this year: preserving cash or lowering total cost.

Leasing does not make a robot cheaper. It makes the payments smoother. Sometimes that matters more.

### Government support: check what actually applies

Russia has support measures for robotisation, but the terms need reading carefully. In March 2026 it was reported that the Industrial Development Fund would cut loan rates in its Components, Auto Components and Labour Productivity programmes from 5% to 3% (from 3% to 1% with certain state guarantees) if at least 50% of the loan goes to Russian industrial robots or robotic systems. That incentive is tied to domestic robots.

There are regional programmes too. In 2025 the St Petersburg Industrial Development Fund launched robotisation loans of 5 to 500 million roubles at 3% for up to five years. Requirements on equipment origin should be checked before applying.

The practical conclusion: before budgeting for state support, check that the equipment you actually plan to buy qualifies and that you can apply before purchase. Programmes for 2027 may change, so rely on the terms in force when you apply, not last year’s news.

### A two-page business case

1.  The problem and the cost of doing nothing.
2.  The proposed solution (robot type, not necessarily model).
3.  Full project cost, not just the robot.
4.  Payback in three scenarios.
5.  Timeline from approval to launch.
6.  Currency risk and how it is covered.
7.  Financing options: purchase, leasing, state support.
8.  Success criteria for the pilot or first phase.

### The bottom line

A budget is not just money. It is a list of what a company believes in for the coming year. A robot that makes the list in December may be working by next December. A robot postponed “until better times” usually waits for exactly that. They just never seem to arrive.
