A freezer warehouse is the only place in a business where staff dream of a draught from the corridor. Inside it is −18 or −25 °C. People work in insulated suits, step out to warm up, then go back in. Their productivity is the sum of two numbers: how much they got done and how much they managed to thaw.

Robots don’t go out to thaw. But cold has its own effect on them too, and buyers should know it in advance. At Aura Robotics we source warehouse equipment directly from Chinese factories and match it to a specific site. Here is what sets a cold store apart.

### Why now

Russia has been short of cold storage for years. IBC Real Estate figures reported by Kommersant in January 2025 show the Moscow region added 386,000 m² of low-temperature space in 2021, 197,000 in 2022, just 15,000 in 2023 and 26,000 in 2024. Vacancy at the end of 2024 was under 1%, and average rents rose 37% in a year to 18,500 roubles per m² per year. One developer estimated equipment costs were up 32% over the same year.

When every square metre of cold gets more expensive, the logic is simple: use it more densely. And nothing stores more densely in a freezer than robots.

In China this logic is already an industry. The China Federation of Logistics and Purchasing reports 267 million m³ of cold storage capacity in 2025 (up 5.53%) and 381.4 million tonnes moved through the cold chain.

### What works in the cold

**Shuttles.** The most common choice for frozen pallet storage. A shuttle runs inside deep rack channels placing pallets one behind another, so a person or stacker only needs to bring the pallet to the channel entrance. A four-way shuttle also travels across between channels; with a lift it turns the building into automated storage with no forklift aisles. Fewer aisles means less air to chill.

**Cold-rated AMRs** move pallets and cases between zones, often across several temperature regimes and regularly from cold to warm.

**Automated stacker cranes and vertical lifts** suit tall buildings with narrow aisles.

One example from a Chinese manufacturer: in 2025 [Geek+](/en/catalog/agv/factory/geekplus) automated a cold store for logistics firm TAHUHU in Hong Kong, with three zones at 0–4 °C, −18 °C and −25 °C served by cold-store AMRs, low-temperature four-way shuttles and automated lifts. The company reports 60% less manual labour and 30% more storage capacity. These are press-release figures rather than an independent audit, but the order of magnitude is telling.

### The battery feels the cold first

A lithium-ion battery in the cold behaves like a person without a hat: it works, briefly and reluctantly. Capacity drops, internal resistance rises, and most manufacturers advise against charging lithium cells below freezing without heating, because it speeds up degradation.

Cold-store robots handle this with heated, insulated battery compartments, charging in a warm zone the robot drives to on its own, or battery swaps with charging outside the freezer. Each choice shows up in the numbers: heating uses energy, warm-zone charging costs time and route length, swapping needs extra batteries and a charging station. Ask at what temperature the datasheet runtime was measured; if it says +25 °C, recalculate. And ask for separate temperature ranges for operation, storage and charging. They are three different lines.

### Condensation comes second

A robot leaving a −25 °C chamber for a +5 °C picking area instantly frosts over and starts to drip. Moisture reaches connectors, lidars, cameras and boards, then freezes on the way back in. A hundred transitions a shift amount to a full physics course delivered to one robot.

The remedies are sealed housings with a suitable IP rating, heated sensor optics, airlocks between zones, and operating modes that keep the robot in each zone long enough to stabilise. If a manufacturer answers the condensation question with “don’t worry”, you probably should.

Frost on a lidar is the best argument for asking about small things.

### Mechanics and lubricants

Cold thickens grease in gearboxes and bearings, hardens seals and rubber wheels, and makes some plastics brittle. A freezer-rated robot is not a standard robot switched on in the cold; it is a separate variant with low-temperature lubricants, different wheel and cable materials and climate-chamber testing. So the spec should not say “for a cold store” but name a range, for example −25 to +5 °C, plus continuous runtime in the coldest zone.

### How the economics change

Three cost lines make robots more attractive in a freezer than in an ambient warehouse. Density: every cubic metre of frozen space is expensive to build and to power, and an aisle-free shuttle system holds far more pallets in the same volume. People: freezer work is hard, needs special clothing and warm-up breaks, usually pays more and is harder to staff. Doors and cold loss: the less often doors open and people and vehicles pass through, the less heat gets in.

An illustrative example: in a three-shift operation with several people permanently in a −25 °C chamber, the business case includes not just wages but clothing, warm-up time, cold lost through open doors and the floor space freed by denser storage. Each line looks modest alone. Together they often decide the matter.

### What to ask a manufacturer

1.  Temperature ranges for operation, charging and storage.
2.  Battery runtime at your temperature, not room temperature.
3.  How charging is handled: heating, warm zone or swaps.
4.  Condensation protection and IP rating of body and sensors.
5.  What differs in lubricants, wheels and cables in the cold version.
6.  Where the system already runs at a comparable temperature.

The sixth question matters most. A robot that has already survived a winter in someone else’s freezer will survive yours.
