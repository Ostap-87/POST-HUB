The most expensive part of a robot is often missing from the spec sheet. It is the rumour. It reaches the shop floor a month before the robot does and tells everyone that “half of us will be gone by spring”. The robot is still at sea, and resignation letters are already on the desk. Usually from your best people, because they find new jobs fastest.

At Aura Robotics we source robots directly from Chinese factories and calculate payback for our clients. Almost every calculation has a line nobody can quantify: how people will react. You cannot calculate it, but you cannot skip it either.

### 1. What people actually fear

Here is a number that surprises managers. On average, workers fear robots less than managers fear that workers fear robots.

In a 2017 VTsIOM poll, 85% of working Russians were not worried about losing their jobs to robots, and 74% believed no robot could replace them in the foreseeable future. In a 2021 poll, about 70% said a robot could not do their job, and only 5% thought their work could be fully automated. Yet 51% called the overall trend of replacing human labour with robots dangerous.

Notice the gap. “They will never replace me” and “this is dangerous” live comfortably in the same head. People feel safe in general and anxious in particular, at the moment a robot appears in their own workshop. An abstract robot scares nobody. The one on the delivery truck does.

The 2021 poll found the highest anxiety among the young: 59% of 18–24-year-olds feared being replaced. Keep that in mind. Experienced workers grumble and stay. Young ones quietly leave for a place without robots. For now.

### 2. Why nobody is being laid off, and how to prove it

In Russia in 2026 the labour market makes the main argument. Unemployment in May 2026 was 2.1%, according to Rosstat, close to a historic low, and the authorities expect a shortfall of 3.1 million workers by 2030.

On the shop floor that means one thing: the robot is not replacing Ivanov. It is replacing a vacancy nobody has filled for six months, a third shift nobody turns up for, or a task that sends people home with back pain.

The International Federation of Robotics said much the same in 2024: robots more often change existing jobs and create new ones than replace people outright, and the shift requires reskilling.

But “we have a labour shortage” is an argument for the board. The shop floor needs a written answer to “what happens to me?” If people move to another task, name it. If someone trains as an operator, name them. Uncertainty is scarier than any robot, because it has no serial number.

### 3. How to announce it: an order that works

There is no scientific formula here, just common sense and deployment experience.

**Team leaders first.** They hear it in person from management, not through the grapevine. The team leader translates between the office and the floor. If the team leader is against it, the robot will not work, even when it is running.

**Then the whole section, before the robot arrives.** Which task, why that task, what happens to the people doing it now. “The robot will take boxes off the conveyor and stack them on a pallet” sounds harmless. “We are implementing robotisation” sounds like a redundancy notice.

**Show a real robot or a video.** An imagined machine is always bigger, faster and meaner than the real one. A [cobot](/en/catalog/cobots) that gently places a box and stops when someone approaches answers half the questions.

**Name the people who will work with it.** Choose future operators and technicians from your own team and announce them early. A robot with “its own” person stops being a stranger.

You cannot announce automation too early. Only too late.

### 4. Reskilling: a schedule, not a slogan

Reskilling works when it has dates, hours and an outcome. Commissioning usually includes operator and technician training, but training two people is not enough. You need backups, instructions in Russian and recordings.

At scale it looks like Amazon’s Mechatronics and Robotics Apprenticeship, announced in 2021, which trains warehouse staff as robotics technicians free of charge; Amazon says pay can rise by about 40% after the first stage. There is no need to copy American figures. Copy the idea: the person working next to the robot needs a visible way up.

If the robot operator earns the same as before but carries more responsibility, in six months you will have a robot without an operator. That is one saving that eats the entire payback.

### 5. The first weeks: the most fragile period

In the first weeks the robot performs worse than in the demo. It stops, makes mistakes, needs tuning. For the shop floor this is the “told you so” moment.

**Set honest expectations.** Say in advance that the start will be bumpy and give a date for reaching the target rate.

**Keep the response loop short.** Operators must know whom to call and get a quick answer. Every unanswered stoppage is a vote against the robot.

**Give credit to people.** When the cell reaches its rate, that is the achievement of the people who tuned it. Say so publicly. The robot is not expecting a bonus.

**Do not change everything at once.** One cell, one task, a clear result. The second robot moves in far more easily once the first one has become “ours”.

### An illustrative sum: what a rumour costs

Illustrative numbers only, to show the order of magnitude. A company installs one robot. Rumours of layoffs prompt two experienced workers to leave. Recruiting and training each replacement, plus months of lower output, can cost the equivalent of several monthly salaries. Two departures can wipe out months of the robot’s payback. (Illustrative. Your HR team will calculate the real figure, and may not enjoy it.)

### The bottom line

The robot arrives in a crate, with a passport and a manual. Fear arrives unpacked, with no manual, and earlier.

Automation succeeds not when the robot starts working, but when people stop noticing it. The way you stop noticing a good colleague who simply gets on with the job.
