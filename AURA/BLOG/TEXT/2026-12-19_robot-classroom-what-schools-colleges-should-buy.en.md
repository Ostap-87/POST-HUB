There are two ways to equip a robotics classroom. One is to buy a robot that looks great on open day. The other is to buy a robot students will drop, reprogram and take apart every day. The first is cheaper to maintain. The second is the only one that teaches anything.

At Aura Robotics we source robots directly from Chinese factories, and schools and colleges keep asking us the same question: what exactly should we buy? It is the right question, because the answer depends on who is learning what. A schoolchild, a future maintenance technician and a future robotics engineer need different machines.

A quick note: this is about training arms and cells for schools and colleges. Humanoids for universities and research labs are a separate article.

### Why the question has gone mainstream

Because far more colleges now need equipment. According to Deputy Prime Minister Dmitry Chernyshenko, Russia’s federal “Professionalitet” vocational programme now has 696 clusters in 87 regions with more than two million students. Another 95 clusters are planned for 2027 (regions submitted 389 applications), 430 more colleges will join, and the target is 981 clusters by 2030.

Each cluster pairs a college with an employer and workshops meant to look like a factory, not a museum. The programme’s sectors include mechanical engineering and metallurgy, where a robot next to the machine tool is already normal. A student who has never programmed an arm will turn up at a plant where an arm is already installed. Awkward for both of them.

### Three levels of training robot

**Level one: the desktop arm.** A small robot that sits on a desk, weighs a few kilograms and plugs into a laptop. The classic example is the [Dobot](/en/catalog/cobots/factory/dobot) Magician Lite: four axes, 250 g payload, 340 mm reach, 0.2 mm repeatability, 3.4 kg. UK distributor Scan listed it at £810.98 including VAT when we checked; that is a public European retail price, not an Aura price, and is here only for scale.

It teaches coordinates, trajectories, block coding and then Python, simple grippers and suction cups, working with a conveyor and camera. It is harmless, cheap enough to drop, and can be handed to a teenager without a twenty-page safety briefing.

The limit is obvious: 250 grams is an eraser, a pencil and a cube. You cannot show a factory on it.

**Level two: the six-axis training cobot.** A small but genuine industrial robot. For example, the Dobot Magician E6: six axes, 0.75 kg payload, 450 mm reach, 0.1 mm repeatability, 7.2 kg. German distributor Unchained Robotics listed it at €5,230 excluding VAT, again a public reference rather than our offer.

Six axes bring real kinematics: tool orientation, singularities, coordinate frames, teach-pendant and offline programming, hand-guiding, speed limits near people. This is what a college student will meet at the machine.

**Level three: the robotic training cell.** An industrial cobot or robot on a table, fenced or not, with a conveyor, grippers, a camera, a PLC and stations for assembly, palletising, machine tending or welding. This is what a Professionalitet college shows its employer partner. The price is driven by the cell’s contents rather than the robot, which is exactly where mistakes are easiest.

A good training cell looks like a factory. A bad one looks like an exhibition stand nobody dares switch on.

### Who makes training robots in China

China leads for the same reason it leads in industrial robots: scale. Dobot made training arms its calling card and also sells industrial cobots, so students can move from the desktop model to a full-size one within the same programming ecosystem. [UBTech](/en/catalog/humanoid/factory/ubtech) and Makeblock have education lines, from school kits to club sets. Many industrial cobot makers offer training packages based on their production models with course materials.

For a college, that last option is often the smartest: students learn on the same model their cluster’s employer partner uses.

### What to check before you buy

**Language and course materials.** Is there a Russian or at least English interface, with courses, exercises and examples? A robot without teaching materials turns the teacher into a textbook author, and teachers are busy enough.

**Programming that scales.** Ideally one platform covers block coding for beginners, Python for advanced students and an industrial language or pendant for college work, so the robot grows with the student.

**Compatibility with industrial kit.** ROS 2 support, PLC connectivity, standard protocols. If the cell cannot talk to a controller, students learn robotics in a vacuum.

**Safety.** Simple for desktop models; for a cell with an industrial robot, a full risk assessment, guarding or scanners, and a briefing. A training cell is still a workplace, just one where everyone is doing things for the first time.

**Spares and consumables.** Students break things. That is not a flaw; it is part of the syllabus. Know in advance what grippers, suction cups, cables, motors and boards cost and how fast they arrive.

**Quantity.** One robot for a group of twenty-five is a field trip. One robot for two or three students is a lesson.

### Budgeting

For a school or college, payback is measured in practice hours, not roubles. A useful question: how many hands-on hours will each student get per year? An illustrative example: two expensive robots for a whole cohort means each student gets a few hours of hands-on time and spends the rest watching a classmate. Six desktop arms and one industrial cobot for the same budget can deliver several times more practice while still showing grown-up equipment. We tailor the mix to the curriculum and student numbers.

### The bottom line

The best training robot is not the one behind glass but the one with the paint worn off its gripper. It means someone used it. It means someone in that room made a machine do something they thought up themselves for the first time. Sometimes a career starts exactly there: with a robot that placed a cube slightly crooked, and a student who worked out why.
