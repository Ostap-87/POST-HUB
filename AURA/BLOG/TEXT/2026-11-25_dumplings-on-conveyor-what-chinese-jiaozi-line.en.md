Where dumplings come from is argued about for longer than it takes to cook them. The Urals, Siberia, China, Mongolia — every theory has its supporters, and every supporter has a grandmother. We are staying out of that one. Our question is narrower: who is best at making dumplings in large numbers, quickly and identically?

On the numbers, China. And the technology refined there on jiaozi transfers well to Russian pelmeni and vareniki — with adjustments, described below.

At Aura Robotics we source equipment directly from factories in China, including food production lines. This article explains how a dumpling line works and what to check before shipping one to your plant.

### 1. A dumpling superpower

One province shows the scale. According to industry reviews, around 70% of China’s frozen jiaozi are made in Henan. Zhengzhou is home to the two biggest players, Sanquan and Synear. Sanquan reported revenue of about RMB 7.06 billion in 2023 and production capacity of over 700,000 tonnes a year; Synear is estimated to be of similar size.

The industry keeps automating: in recent years large producers have added automatic filling-mixing systems, multihead weighers, packaging machines and [cobots](/en/catalog/cobots). For a buyer, the takeaway is that China has many factories building dumpling equipment and many plants where it runs real shifts, not just trade show demos.

The Russian market is growing too. Romir research found pelmeni sales in Russia rose 21% by volume and 34% by value between October 2023 and September 2024. Dumplings do not fear a downturn. They are a downturn food.

### 2. How the line works

A typical line has five sections.

**Dough:** mixing, often in a vacuum mixer for a denser, more elastic dough, then feeding the former as a sheet or rope.

**Filling:** mincing, mixing, pumping. The pump matters: it decides dosing accuracy and whether the line can handle fillings with pieces, not just smooth mince.

**Forming:** the heart of the line, covered below.

**Freezing:** a spiral or tunnel quick freezer.

**Packing:** weighers, bags, metal detection, cases.

A line runs at the speed of its slowest section. That is usually freezing, not forming.

### 3. Two forming principles

**Extrusion with filling inside.** The machine forms a tube of dough, pumps filling continuously into it, and a drum with cavities cuts and shapes the pieces. This is the classic industrial principle used by most dumpling machines: fast, compact, consistent.

**Imitation hand-folding.** Dough is sheeted, discs are cut, filling is dosed onto each, and a mechanism folds and seals the edges, sometimes with pleats like a handmade jiaozi. The product looks homemade, but the machine is more complex and usually slower.

Choosing the principle is choosing the product, not the machine. If your brand sells “just like Grandma’s”, an extruder will give you away at first glance.

### 4. Throughput: pieces, kilograms, shifts

Makers quote pieces per hour. High-speed automatic lines claim up to 15,000 pieces an hour at 50–120 cycles a minute. Buyers need kilograms and shifts.

An illustrative example, for arithmetic only. The line makes 10,000 dumplings an hour at 12 g each: 120 kg an hour, or about 900 kg in 7.5 hours of net running. Then subtract start-up, changeovers between products, end-of-shift washdown and stoppages when the dough misbehaves. The real figure may be a quarter lower. (Illustrative numbers. Yours will differ, which is the point of calculating.)

So compare lines on kilograms of good product per shift, on your dough and your filling, not on brochure pieces.

### 5. A Russian GOST pelmen and a Chinese machine

A Chinese machine is designed for a Chinese product. Russian pelmeni differ in shape, dough thickness, recipe and mince texture, and they have their own standard.

GOST 33394-2015 for frozen pelmeni sets, among other things, a piece weight of 3–25 g, a meat filling share of at least 50%, and a core temperature no higher than −10 °C. The pieces must not stick together or deform, the edges must be well sealed and the filling must not show.

What this means for procurement:

- moulds and drums are made to your geometry, not the catalogue’s;
- the dough-to-filling ratio is tested on your actual recipe: 50% filling is a product requirement, and the machine must hold it consistently, not just on a good minute;
- Russian dough is often stiffer, and the machine needs the force to handle it;
- vareniki with potato, cabbage or curd cheese need a pump that neither purées the filling nor clogs on the pieces.

A Chinese factory will always say the machine makes “any dumpling”. That is true in the same sense that any chef can cook any dish.

### 6. Freezing: the most underrated part

If forming is the heart of the line, freezing is its lungs. Dumplings must freeze fast so the dough does not go soggy and the pieces do not stick or lose shape. Slow freezing is how a bag of dumplings turns into one large dumpling.

Freezer capacity must match line output with a margin. Spiral freezers save floor space; tunnels are simpler. Either way, freezing time, exit temperature and how pieces sit on the belt matter. We will cover freezing technology separately.

### 7. What to check before buying

1.  Forming principle that fits your product: extrusion or hand-style folding.
2.  Throughput in kilograms of good product per shift.
3.  Trials on your recipe, with dough and filling as close to yours as possible.
4.  Moulds for your geometry, and changeover time.
5.  Filling pump for smooth mince and chunky fillings.
6.  A quick freezer balanced with the line.
7.  Hygienic design: stainless steel, tool-free disassembly, washdown.
8.  Spare parts: knives, moulds, seals — how many, and from where.
