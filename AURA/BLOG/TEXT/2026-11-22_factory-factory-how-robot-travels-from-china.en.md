The robot has passed factory acceptance, shown its serial number on camera and cleared every test. Now comes the hardest exam of its life: several thousand kilometres in a crate, two forklifts, one border and one bonded warehouse. No datasheet has a line for “logistics endurance”. It should.

At Aura Robotics we source robots directly from factories in China. This article is about what happens between two factory floors, theirs and yours. It describes common market practice. The specific route, Incoterms rule and HS code for a specific model are agreed with the forwarder and customs broker before shipment, not after.

### 1. Packing: the journey should be boring

Good robot packing looks dull. A wooden crate or frame, the robot secured in its transport position with the axes locked as the manufacturer’s manual says, not as the loader finds convenient. Inside: moisture barrier film and desiccant, because a sea container crosses several climate zones and condensation happens even to cargo that is in no hurry.

Worth writing into the contract specification:

- transport position and axis locking per the manufacturer’s instructions;
- moisture protection and desiccant for sea and multimodal routes;
- shock and tilt indicators on the crate — a cheap sticker that later settles arguments with the insurer;
- photos and video of the packed robot, serial number visible, before the lid goes on;
- ISPM 15 treatment and marking of wooden packaging.

A crate is built once. It is opened together with everyone’s expectations.

### 2. Batteries: a robot that travels as dangerous goods

Mobile robots, [humanoids](/en/catalog/humanoid) and robot dogs almost always carry lithium-ion batteries, which international transport rules classify as Class 9 dangerous goods: UN 3481 when contained in or packed with equipment, UN 3480 when shipped alone.

In practice that means a UN 38.3 test summary for the battery (no summary, no booking), correct marks and a shipper’s declaration. And for air freight, from 2026 the 30% state-of-charge limit has been extended to batteries packed with equipment, not just standalone batteries.

So ask the factory how the batteries will travel on the same day you ask about price. A robot without battery paperwork is not cargo. It is a puzzle for the forwarder.

### 3. Routes and lead times

Average China–Russia transit times, as estimated by market participants in early 2026:

| Route                                  | Time       |
|----------------------------------------|------------|
| Air                                    | 2–7 days   |
| Road                                   | 15–25 days |
| Rail                                   | 15–35 days |
| Sea via Russian Far East ports         | 35–45 days |
| Sea via St Petersburg and Novorossiysk | 45–60 days |

These are averages, not promises. Logistics firms report that border crossing times have grown several-fold since controls tightened in autumn 2025, and more cargo now goes via the Far East. Then there is the calendar: in late January and February China takes its Spring Festival holidays and factories and warehouses slow down. If you need the robot by spring, ship it before the holidays.

Lead time has three parts: the road, the border and waiting for documents. The last one is usually the longest.

### 4. Insurance: door to door, not port to port

Who insures the cargo depends on the Incoterms rule, which we covered in our article on contracts with Chinese factories. The key point: under CIF the seller only has to buy minimum cover, under CIP extended cover. For expensive equipment the difference matters.

The second trap is where cover ends. A policy that stops at the port does not cover unloading at your site, which is exactly where the robot meets the most dangerous creature in logistics: a forklift with forks that are slightly too short. Insure to the installation point, and check door widths, opening heights, floor load ratings and lifting equipment in advance. A crate that does not fit through the door is not a logistics problem. It is a measuring problem.

### 5. Customs: the code decides almost everything

Duty, documents and certification all follow from the HS code. Industrial robots “not elsewhere specified” fall under EAEU code 8479 50 000 0, with a 0% import duty. But not every robot lands there: a mobile platform, a cleaning robot, a humanoid or a line with a robot inside may be classified differently, with different rates and rules. Classify the specific product before you pay; for tricky cases, an advance classification ruling can be requested from Russian customs.

What you pay at import:

- **Duty** at the rate for your code.
- **VAT** at 22% from 1 January 2026, on customs value plus duty. VAT-registered importers reclaim it later, but pay it up front. There is a VAT exemption for technological equipment not produced in Russia, if the model is on the government list. Worth checking.
- **Customs processing fee**, raised in 2026 and scaled by customs value: for example RUB 49,240 for a shipment worth RUB 5.5–10 million and RUB 73,860 above RUB 10 million.

Customs value is not the invoice price. It is the price plus delivery to the EAEU border, so freight to Vladivostok is part of the VAT base.

Documents include the contract, invoice, packing list, transport documents, EAEU conformity documents and, if the robot uses encryption, a notification — both covered in our certification article. If any of these are prepared “in parallel with shipping”, the robot arrives before its paperwork and waits in a bonded warehouse. At a daily rate.

### 6. An illustrative calculation

For arithmetic only. Customs value of the shipment: RUB 8 million, covering robot, tooling and delivery to the border. Zero-duty code.

- Duty: 0.
- VAT at 22%: RUB 1,760,000.
- Customs fee: RUB 49,240.

About RUB 1.81 million is due at the border. The VAT comes back as a credit, but it is out of your working capital for a month or two, so plan for it. Add broker fees, storage, transport from the border and unloading. (Illustrative numbers. Yours will differ, which is the point of calculating.)

The most expensive line is usually the one missing from the spreadsheet: a week in a bonded warehouse waiting for one missing document.

### The bottom line

A robot takes longer to travel between factories than it took to build. Engineers built it; rules deliver it.

You cannot speed up the rules. You can simply be on time with the paperwork. Then the only thing the robot sees on the way is the inside of its crate. For a robot, that is the best possible view.
