Warehouse rent is charged per square metre, while goods sit in cubic ones. In a typical manual-picking warehouse, everything above two metres exists mostly in theory: slow to reach, unpleasant to stand on, awkward to count.

Hai Robotics built a business on that empty air. Its robots climb a rack to the height of a three- or four-storey building, pull out the right tote and bring it to a person, who stays on the floor where people belong.

At Aura Robotics we source warehouse equipment directly from Chinese factories. We have already covered AGV versus AMR, and [Geek+](/en/catalog/agv/factory/geekplus) and Quicktron. This is about a different class of machine and the company that now leads it.

### What an ACR is

An ACR, autonomous case-handling robot, works with totes and cases. A latent robot slides under a rack and brings the whole rack to the picker; an ACR brings only the box you need, not the furniture.

It is essentially a mobile mast with an extending gripper. It drives to the rack, raises the gripper to the right level, pulls out a tote, puts it on its own onboard shelf and moves on to the next. According to Hai Robotics, its tallest model, the HaiPick A42T, reaches 10 metres, drops to 0.28 m for the bottom level and carries up to eight totes per trip.

The full system is called HaiPick: robots, HaiStation picking stations, racking, chargers and the HaiQ management platform. In 2025 the company added HaiPick Climb, robots that climb the rack instead of extending a mast. The company says Climb stores totes up to 12 metres high, and its 2026 IPO prospectus already mentions 15. Claimed throughput is about 4,000 totes per hour on 1,000 m², with up to 30,000 storage locations in that footprint and aisles as narrow as 900 mm.

An ordinary warehouse grows outwards until it hits the fence. An ACR warehouse grows upwards until it hits the roof. Fortunately, nobody has to move the roof.

### The numbers

Hai Robotics is a private Shenzhen company. Its 2026 Hong Kong IPO filing finally made its figures public.

Revenue: 807 million yuan in 2023, 1.36 billion in 2024, 2.02 billion in 2025, and 1.12 billion in H1 2026, up 70% year on year. Gross margin rose from 16% to 31% between 2023 and 2025.

There is no profit. Net losses were 1.01 billion yuan in 2023, 1.26 billion in 2024, 0.83 billion in 2025 and 0.43 billion in H1 2026, about 3.5 billion in total since 2023. The prospectus expects another loss for 2026.

CIC data cited in the prospectus ranks Hai Robotics first in the global ACR market, with 32.8% of 2025 revenue. The company’s website mentions more than 1,100 projects worldwide. Overseas revenue rose from 24% in 2023 to half of the total in H1 2026; customers include, by the company’s account, seven of the world’s ten largest fashion companies and seven of the ten largest 3PLs.

The IPO itself is instructive. The first application, filed in February 2026, lapsed in August because no hearing took place within six months. The company refiled in September, with Goldman Sachs and CITIC Securities as joint sponsors. By the time you read this the status may have changed, so check HKEX.

For a buyer, losses are no reason to walk away. They are a reason to ask how long the maker commits to supplying parts and software updates, and to put the answer in the contract.

A robot that lifts a tote ten metres should not depend on whether the share price lifts too.

### When an ACR beats an AMR

Any numbers below are illustrative, to show the logic, not prices.

ACRs win when goods are stored in totes and cases rather than pallets; when the range is wide (thousands or tens of thousands of SKUs) and orders have several lines; when the building allows 6–12 m racking; and when space is expensive or short and moving is worse than automating. Latent AMRs and simple AGVs win when goods move as whole pallets or carts, when ceilings are 4–5 m, and when the job is transport, not storage.

An illustration. A 2,000 m² apparel warehouse with an 11 m ceiling currently uses 2.5 m shelving. Moving to ACR racking at 9–10 m, given the manufacturer’s claim of 80–400% higher storage density, could in theory fit the same stock into noticeably less space. The freed area can be sublet, used for packing, or simply not rented at the next site. But this only works if payback includes racking, stations and software integration, not just robots. ### What the building has to give back

A tall machine has tall demands.

**Floor.** A robot the height of a house hates uneven floors: a millimetre at the bottom becomes centimetres at the top. Get the flatness and load requirements from the maker early and compare them with your building. Sometimes levelling a floor is cheaper than arguing with physics.

**Height and racking.** Height drives the economics, but ACR racking is its own structure with its own tolerances. Design and installation belong in the project, not in “we’ll sort it later”.

**Totes.** The robot wants standard containers of set sizes. If you use ten kinds of box, sort out the boxes first.

**Fire safety.** High-bay storage in cartons is a question for fire codes and sprinkler design. Settle it before ordering, not after the racking arrives.

**Cold.** In unheated buildings, check operating temperatures and battery behaviour separately.

Automate a warehouse that is not in order and you get disorder, now ten metres tall.

### The bottom line

Hai Robotics does not really sell robots. It sells the air above your racking, which you have been paying for all along without using.

Warehouses, like people, tend to grow outwards with age. This is a rare chance to reverse the process.
