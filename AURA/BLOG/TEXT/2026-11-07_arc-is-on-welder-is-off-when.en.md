[Welding](/en/catalog/welding) robots are usually bought right after someone says “we can’t find welders.” In Russia that is not a figure of speech. According to hh.ru, employers posted more than 48,000 welder vacancies in January–May 2025, about 4,000 more than a year earlier, with shortages recorded in over 35 regions.

But a shortage explains why you want a robot. It says nothing about whether the robot will pay for itself. That depends on three things: how many identical welds you make, how accurately your parts are cut, and whether someone on your team knows more about welding than the robot does.

### 1. The number that matters: arc-on time

A robot does not weld faster than a good welder. Deposition rate is set by the process, not by whoever holds the torch. The robot wins because its arc is on for longer.

In manual MIG/MAG welding, the arc burns for roughly 10–20% of a shift. Industry figures compiled from ESAB, Miller and others put many shops at 8–12%, call around 15% a good result, and treat 20% as the practical ceiling. The rest of the time goes on handling, flipping, grinding, reading drawings, changing wire and waiting for the crane. That is not laziness. That is the job.

A robotic cell can keep the arc on 70–80% of the time, provided an operator loads one side of the positioner while the robot welds the other.

So the robot is not quicker. It is simply never distracted. In an eight-hour shift a welder gets an hour or an hour and a half of arc time; a robot gets 5–6. Hence the dream of “one robot for three welders”.

The dream only applies to welds the robot can actually reach.

### 2. Batch size: robots like repetition

To keep a robot welding rather than waiting, you need repeat work: trusses, frames, beams of a standard series, brackets, embedded plates, anything made in batches of dozens that comes back to the shop floor again and again.

Every new part needs a program. Offline programming and scan-based path generation cut this time considerably. But if programming takes longer than welding, the robot becomes a very expensive hobby for the engineering department.

If every structure you build is unique, manual welding is probably still the better deal.

### 3. Fit-up: the robot does not forgive gaps

A welder sees a wandering gap and adjusts by hand. A robot follows its program. Touch sensing and laser seam tracking help it find the joint, but they cannot turn a crooked part into a straight one.

So the first question is not “which robot?” but “how do you cut?” If parts come off a plasma table with a couple of millimetres of variation and are tacked together by eye, the robot will report this faster than any quality inspector. Cutting, jigs and assembly often have to change along with the welding cell.

A welding robot is the best auditor your cutting department will ever have. It costs nothing to employ, but its findings can be expensive.

### 4. Fixtures and positioners

The part has to sit in the same place every time, within the tolerance the program assumes. Jigs, clamps, a two-station positioner or a turning unit for long workpieces are not optional extras; they are half the project. On many projects tooling and integration cost about as much as the robot, sometimes more.

The two-station layout is what delivers the 70–80% arc-on time. Without it, the robot waits for the operator the same way a welder waits for the crane, only at a higher hourly rate.

### 5. A welder-operator, not an operator instead of a welder

The most common mistake is to staff the cell with someone who “knows which button to press”. The cell needs an operator who understands welding: who spots lack of fusion, adjusts parameters and knows what to do about porosity. Usually your best candidate is one of your own experienced welders.

The robot does not replace the welder. It promotes him and sends him back to school.

### 6. An illustrative calculation

Payback = total project cost / (monthly savings − monthly running costs). All figures below are illustrative.

**Scenario A: repeat production.** A plant welds frames and beams in recurring sizes. A turnkey cell (robot, power source, two-station positioner, fixtures, integration, training) costs 9 million roubles. Running two shifts, it produces what three welders per shift used to produce, six people in total. Each shift still needs one welder-operator. A welder costs the company 180,000 roubles a month fully loaded.

Savings: (6 − 2) × 180,000 = 720,000 roubles. Cell maintenance, contact tips, nozzles and spares: 40,000 a month. Payback: 9,000,000 / 680,000 ≈ 13 months, before counting less rework and a weld quality you can show to customers.

**Scenario B: project-based fabrication.** Same robot, but every structure is different. The cell welds a third of the time and spends the rest being programmed and re-fixtured. It does the work of one and a half welders, and still needs an operator. Savings are close to zero, sometimes below.

That is the answer to the headline. The same robot can pay back in a year in one shop and never in the shop next door. The difference is not the robot. It is the product mix.

### 7. What to check before you buy

Take the last 12 months of output and work out what share of welds comes from repeat products. Measure how much your fit-up gaps vary. Decide who will run the cell and who will write the first programs. Then compare a cell per shift with a crew per shift, not a robot with one welder.

### The bottom line

A welder with twenty years of frames behind him knows more about a weld than any robot. The robot knows only one thing: keep the arc on.

A good project puts the two together. Then the arc stays on, the welder does the thinking, and nobody is wasting their talent.
