Some things get cheaper so slowly nobody notices. Some get cheaper so fast buyers barely have time to celebrate: TVs, phones and, it turns out, Chinese humanoids. Two years ago a humanoid robot cost as much as an apartment. Today the cheapest one costs about as much as a decent phone. Apartments, sadly, did not follow.

Here is what got cheaper, by how much, and why. We use only public prices announced by manufacturers or reported by the media, in yuan. These are not Aura prices and not landed prices: shipping, duties, certification and commissioning come on top.

### Humanoids: from apartment to smartphone

Unitree gives the clearest picture because it publishes prices.

**Unitree H1.** The full-size humanoid that made Unitree famous. At the end of 2025, when the company opened its first store in Beijing, H1 was listed at 650,000 yuan.

**Unitree G1.** Launched in May 2024 from 99,000 yuan, a price that still applied in December 2025. By June 2026 Chinese and Taiwanese media were reporting a starting price of 85,000 yuan.

**Unitree R1.** Launched in July 2025 from 39,900 yuan, about $5,900, and instantly labelled the cheapest full-size humanoid. In June 2026 Xinhua reported the starting price cut to 29,900 yuan.

**Noetix Bumi.** In October 2025 Beijing start-up Noetix Robotics unveiled Bumi, 94 cm tall and 12 kg, for 9,998 yuan, about $1,370. It is an education and consumer model that walks, balances, dances and is programmed with drag-and-drop tools. In June 2026 it was selling for about 9,400 yuan after subsidies.

So in two years the floor price of a humanoid fell from six figures in yuan to four. But that compares big robots with small ones. Like for like, G1 fell about 14% in two years and R1 about a quarter in one.

One telling figure about Unitree itself: according to data cited by Jiemian and China Times, the company’s average selling price per humanoid fell from 593,000 yuan in 2023 to 261,000 in 2024 and 168,000 in the first three quarters of 2025. That does not mean each model became three times cheaper; the mix shifted towards cheaper models. But that is where the market went.

### Cobots: a quiet slide

Cobots got cheaper earlier and with less fanfare. According to GGII, the average price of a six-axis cobot in China in 2023 was 97,300 yuan, down 11.87% year on year, and it kept falling in 2024. GGII’s 2024 field research found six-axis 6 kg robots at just over 20,000 yuan and 5 kg cobots under 30,000, with some listings on procurement platforms below 10,000.

That last number looks like a clearance sale. A 10,000-yuan cobot and a 100,000-yuan cobot are usually different machines: different gearboxes, torque sensing, software, safety certification and service. A cheap cobot is not a discount on an expensive one. It is a different cobot.

### AMRs: prices nobody publishes

Warehouse AMRs are almost never sold off a price list. Price depends on fleet size, fleet management software, WMS integration and the project, so there is no public year-by-year series, and figures in market overviews deserve caution. The modest conclusion: competition among Chinese warehouse robot makers is intense, which helps buyers, but compare projects, not robots.

### Why prices fell

**Components.** Joints, meaning motors, gearboxes, drivers and sensors, make up much of a humanoid’s cost. In early 2024 Goldman Sachs estimated humanoid unit costs had fallen about 40% in a year, from $50,000–250,000 to $30,000–150,000, against expectations of 15–20% a year. In 2026 Morgan Stanley estimated the bill of materials for Tesla’s Optimus Gen 2 at about $46,000 with Chinese components and about $131,000 without. China’s supply chain is not just cheaper. It is nearly three times cheaper.

**Scale.** Chinese media cited estimates of about 14,400 humanoids shipped in China in 2025 and 100,000–200,000 expected in 2026. Even at the low end, unit prices cannot stand still.

**Borrowing.** Makers reuse components from neighbouring industries: batteries and electronics from EVs, cameras and chips from phones. An industry that rides on other industries’ volumes gets cheaper faster than its own volumes would allow.

**Competition and simplification.** Simpler models for specific jobs: fewer degrees of freedom, simpler hands, shorter runtime. An honest price cut, but you get less as well as pay less.

### What this means for buyers

First, the robot is no longer the biggest line in the budget. When a humanoid cost half a million yuan, integration and tooling were extras. At 30,000 they can easily cost more than the robot. An illustrative split: robot 30 units, gripper and tooling 10, integration and training 25, shipping and customs 10. The robot is less than half the project.

Second, compare like with like. Entry models often lack what real work needs: an open SDK, payload, a dexterous hand, service life. “From” prices refer to the most basic version; the research edition of the same model costs noticeably more.

Third, do not wait forever. Falling prices tempt you to wait another year. That is fine for a robot bought to experiment with, less so for a task that is costing you money now. You can wait for a TV to get cheaper. With automation that pays back, waiting is paid for in wages and scrap.

Fourth, a fast-falling market refreshes models fast. Ask how long the maker will support your version with parts and firmware.

### The bottom line

Compared first model to latest, the humanoid has become dozens of times cheaper. The work it has to do has not become cheaper by a single yuan. That is why payback maths matters more now than two years ago: a cheap robot is less forgiving of mistakes in the calculation, because the mistakes still cost what they always did.
