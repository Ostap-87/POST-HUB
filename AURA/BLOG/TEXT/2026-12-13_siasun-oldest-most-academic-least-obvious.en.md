In Chinese robotics it is fashionable to be young. A three-year-old humanoid startup raises a round, lists, and shows its robot doing a backflip. A company founded in 2000 is expected to sit in the corner at this party and reminisce.

Siasun does sit in the corner. But it has plenty to remember: it is one of China’s first robotics companies and the first to list on the Shenzhen exchange. For a buyer that is a reason not for nostalgia but for a closer look at what such a company does well and where it struggles.

At Aura Robotics we buy robots directly from Chinese factories. We have not profiled Siasun before, and people ask about it often, usually as “Who are they, exactly?”

### The pedigree

Siasun Robot & Automation was founded on 30 April 2000 in Shenyang as a spin-off from the Shenyang Institute of Automation of the Chinese Academy of Sciences. It is named after Jiang Xinsong, the institute’s former director and one of the fathers of Chinese robotics, and was founded by his student Qu Daokui.

Some context. In 1972 Jiang and colleagues wrote a memo, “On Artificial Intelligence and Robotics”, now described as China’s first policy proposal on the subject. In 1982 the institute built the SZJ-1, the first robotic arm deployed on Chinese assembly lines.

In 2009 Siasun listed on the newly opened ChiNext board under the code 300024, becoming the first robotics company on the Shenzhen exchange. Its ties to the Academy of Sciences remain.

Most Chinese robot companies have investors. Siasun has ancestors.

### What it makes

The range is as broad as you would expect from a company that has taken every order going for twenty-five years:

- **industrial robots**: six-axis models of various payloads, and cobots;
- **mobile robots and AGVs**: by the company’s account, as cited in the Chinese business press, its mobile robots hold more than 80% of the market on Chinese car assembly lines;
- **logistics and warehouse automation**: turnkey systems;
- **automated assembly and test lines**, and system integration;
- **transport automation**;
- **semiconductor equipment**, including cleanroom and vacuum robots;
- **special-purpose and medical service robots**.

The company cites more than 1,300 invention patents and a role in drafting over 80 national and industry standards. It has shipped to 32 countries and regions, with past customers including BMW, Nissan, Ford and General Motors.

A catalogue that has everything prompts one question: which of these do you do better than anyone?

### The numbers: flat revenue, growing losses

Now the awkward part. Revenue was 3.97 billion yuan in 2023, 4.14 billion in 2024 and 4.12 billion in 2025, so growth has stalled. Net profit attributable to shareholders was about 49 million yuan in 2023; then came a loss of about 194 million in 2024 and about 398 million in 2025, so the loss doubled in a year. Gross margin fell to 12.5%.

A telling detail: Siasun sold about 13,676 robots in 2025, 65% more than in 2024. The Chinese business press summed it up as “selling more, losing more”. The company blames lower margins due to competition, losses at associates and a weak dollar hurting export contracts.

By segment, only system integration and assembly lines grew in 2025 (about 1.49 billion yuan, +16%). Industrial robots brought in about 1.10 billion (−9%), logistics about 0.73 billion (−7%), semiconductor equipment about 0.53 billion (−9%). Revenue fell another 8% in Q1 2026.

Against competitors the picture is clearer still. In 2019 Siasun’s revenue was 2.7 billion yuan and [Estun](/en/catalog/industrial/factory/estun)’s 1.6 billion; by 2023 Estun had reached 4.7 billion and overtaken it. Siasun is not among China’s top ten industrial robot brands by 2024 shipments. The latecomers, Estun, Inovance and [Efort](/en/catalog/industrial/factory/efort), are running faster.

The oldest does not have to be the strongest. Still being in the race is an achievement in itself.

### Academy-backed versus startup: the buyer’s view

“State” in our working title should be read broadly: Siasun grew out of the Academy of Sciences and remains affiliated with it.

**The upside.** A company with twenty-five years of history, academic roots and public accounts will not vanish overnight. It is used to large projects: car plant lines, warehouses, public contracts. It knows system integration, building a whole cell or line rather than shipping a robot in a box. And its finances can be judged from filings, not press releases.

**The downside.** A broad range means it may trail specialists on price or specs for particular products. Losses and shrinking margins are a reason to look hard at service terms, spare parts lead times and the stability of the specific product line. Companies fighting for margin sometimes cut exactly what you will need in three years.

**Versus a startup.** A startup usually has one product, fast development and quick responses, but a short history and dependence on the next round. Siasun is the reverse: long history, broad range, big-project experience, slower processes and, on current numbers, financial strain.

Neither is better by default. The right supplier is the one whose weak spots do not coincide with your risks.

### When to consider Siasun

Put Siasun on the shortlist when you need a system rather than a single machine: mobile robots for an assembly line, in-plant logistics, special equipment, cleanroom work. For a single cobot tending a machine or a SCARA for electronics assembly, there are more specialised options.

We select robots for the task and compare manufacturers by calculation, not by founding year. We calculate payback, buy directly from the factory, show the specific unit with its serial number on video from the floor and test it on the task before shipment, with one team running the project from selection to start-up. Shenyang, incidentally, makes a good Aura Robotics Tour destination: it shows Chinese robotics that began long before humanoids were fashionable.

### The bottom line

Siasun took Chinese robotics’ first steps when robotics itself was still an idea in an academic memo. Today it has to prove that experience is worth paying for, in a market where money prefers youth.

Veterans rarely win the sprint. Yet when there is a long distance to cover, somehow they still get the call.
