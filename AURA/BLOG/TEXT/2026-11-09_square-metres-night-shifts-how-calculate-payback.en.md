[Cleaning](/en/catalog/cleaning) robot payback is usually worked out on a napkin. Divide the mall’s floor area by the brochure’s coverage rate, multiply by the robot’s price. Divide the same area by a cleaner’s norm, multiply by wages. The difference looks so good you want to sign the contract on the same napkin.

Don’t. Cleaning contractors invoice shifts, not square metres. So the robot has to be counted in shifts too: which shift it covers, which it shortens, and which it does not touch at all.

### 1. Brochure versus floor

Commercial scrubber robots are rated in square metres per hour. The Pudu CC1 is rated at 700–1,000 m²/h for full cleaning. The Gausium Scrubber 50 Pro lists a practical rate of 500–1,300 m²/h and a theoretical maximum of almost 2,000 m²/h.

Note the word “practical”. Between the theoretical and practical figures sit turns, obstacles, edges, pillars, shoppers, trolleys and the fire extinguisher stand someone placed across the walkway.

Then there are resources. The Scrubber 50 Pro scrubs for about three hours per charge and recharges in about two, with a 30-litre clean water tank. The CC1 has 15-litre tanks, runs up to eight hours depending on mode, and takes up to three hours to charge. Over a night, the robot will return to its station several times to refill, drain and recharge.

Square metres per hour is a speed. What pays for the robot is the number of hours it actually spends cleaning.

### 2. Who the robot really competes with

Robots are often compared with a cleaner holding a mop. In a large mall, the real competitor is the operator of a ride-on or walk-behind scrubber, who covers a lot of floor per shift. The robot will not necessarily beat that operator on speed.

It wins elsewhere. It needs nobody at the controls. It cleans as well in the sixth hour of the night as in the first. It follows a route, not a mood, and afterwards produces a coverage map: where it went, where it could not, and why.

A robot does not clean better than a person. It cleans the same way every time, and it can prove it.

### 3. Where the robot is useless

Corners, skirting, glass, escalators, stairs, restrooms, the food court after closing, shop fronts. Office carpets need different equipment. All of that stays with people. A well-designed robot-assisted cleaning plan gives the robot the large, open hard floors and leaves everything that needs hands and eyes to the team.

So “how many cleaners will a robot replace?” is the wrong question. The right one is: “how much of the scrubber operator’s shift can the robot take over?”

### 4. An illustrative calculation

All figures are illustrative.

A mall with 15,000 m² of hard flooring in its walkways. Every night the contractor sends two scrubber operators. One operator shift costs 5,000 roubles including the contractor’s margin: 2 × 5,000 × 30 = 300,000 roubles a month.

With robots: two scrubber robots with stations, mapping, route setup and training, around 5 million roubles. The night still needs a human: someone to clear obstacles, check the stations and clean what the robot could not reach. Count half an operator shift per night, 75,000 roubles a month. Brushes, squeegees, pads, maintenance and electricity: 30,000 a month.

Savings: 300,000 − 75,000 − 30,000 = 195,000 roubles a month. Payback: 5,000,000 / 195,000 ≈ 26 months.

Just over two years is an honest number for night cleaning alone. It gets shorter if the robot also works by day, spot-cleaning high-traffic areas: entrances on rainy days, the food court, office corridors during the morning rush. If daytime work replaces another half shift, savings rise to about 270,000 a month and payback drops to roughly 19 months.

It gets longer if the area is small, the floors are mixed and the corridors are narrow and cluttered. In a small office building with carpets and three floors and no service lift, a robot probably will not pay back. Better to say so up front.

### 5. Who buys it: the owner or the contractor

This is where engineering gives way to contracts. If the building owner owns the robot, the contractor has every reason for the robot to “struggle”: each shift it takes over shrinks the contractor’s invoice. If the contractor owns it, the robot goes into the price and the contractor keeps the saving.

What works is writing the robots into the contract and basing the contractor’s SLA on their reports: coverage maps, cleaning times, missed zones. “We cleaned everything” becomes “here is the map, here are the gaps, here is why.” That changes the relationship with the contractor more than the robot itself does.

For the first time, a facility manager gets something they never had before: proof that the floor was cleaned.

### 6. What to clarify before you calculate

Hard-floor area only, not the whole building. The night cleaning window. Whether there is a service lift and whether the robot can integrate with it (on some models, such as the Pudu CC1 Pro, lift integration is an option). Where the stations go and whether water supply and drainage are nearby. How much brushes and squeegees cost and how often they are replaced. What the report needs to look like.
