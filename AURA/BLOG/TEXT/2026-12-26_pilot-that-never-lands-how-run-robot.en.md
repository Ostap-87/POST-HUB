A pilot project has one awkward property: it is easy to start and almost impossible to finish. It starts with enthusiasm, a ribbon and a photo next to the robot. It finishes… well, it rarely does. A year later the robot is standing in a corner of the shop, everyone has got used to it like an office plant, and the answer to “how is the pilot going?” is “it’s ongoing”.

There is a name for this: pilot purgatory. Projects there do not die, but they never make it to the heaven of scale either. They wait.

At Aura Robotics we match robots to our clients’ tasks, and what we see is that pilots rarely fail because of the robot. The robot does what the spec sheet says, occasionally a little less. What fails is the agreement about what success looks like. Here is how to reach that agreement before the robot arrives.

### The numbers: everyone gets stuck, not only with robots

Pilot paralysis is well documented. In McKinsey’s 2018 Digital Manufacturing Global Expert Survey, fewer than a third of respondents, around 30%, had moved their programmes from pilot to scale.

AI repeated the story at higher speed. In July 2024 Gartner predicted that at least 30% of generative AI projects would be abandoned after proof of concept by the end of 2025, citing poor data, escalating costs and unclear business value. In August 2025 MIT’s NANDA initiative published The GenAI Divide, which found that only about 5% of enterprise pilots delivered rapid revenue growth; the rest had little measurable impact on P&L. The study drew on 150 leadership interviews, a survey of 350 employees and 300 public deployments.

These figures describe other technologies, so do not map them onto robots one to one. The pattern holds, though: a pilot without an exit criterion does not exit. A project with no end date is not a project. It is a lifestyle.

### Mistake one: a pilot without a question

The most common pilot brief is “let’s try a robot and see”. You can see for a long time. The robot drives, beeps, moves things, everyone is happy, and there is still no answer, because nobody asked a question.

A good pilot answers one specific question. Can a cobot tend this machine at a steady 90-second cycle across two shifts, with operator intervention no more than once an hour? Can an AMR move totes between picking and packing in our forklift and foot traffic without slowing the area down?

If the question does not fit in one sentence, it is not a pilot. It is a field trip.

### Mistake two: criteria invented afterwards

Write the success criteria down before launch, on paper, signed. Otherwise everyone reads the result their own way: production says “too slow”, finance says “too expensive”, IT says “not secure”, and the director says “let’s watch it a bit longer”. And the pilot is extended. Again.

We suggest three groups of criteria. **Technical:** cycle time, share of successful cycles, stops per shift, recovery time, scrap rate, each with thresholds for success, rework and failure. **Economic:** the number that makes the payback calculation work. As an illustrative example, not taken from any real project: if the model assumes the robot covers 1.5 full-time roles on a line, a pilot that shows 0.7 is not “almost a success”. It tells you the model needs rebuilding. **Organisational:** how many people it takes to keep the robot running. If one robot needs an engineer standing by, that is a key finding even when the cycle time is perfect.

Also write down what counts as failure. That is not pessimism; it is permission to stop on time. An honest negative result costs money, but less than an endless positive one.

### Mistake three: “until it becomes clear”

It never becomes clear, so set the date in advance. For standard tasks such as machine tending, tote transport or floor cleaning, one to three months of operation after commissioning is a reasonable guide: long enough to get past the learning curve, see shift and weekly variation, and gather statistics. A humanoid on a new task may need longer, but it still needs a date.

Add checkpoints: at two weeks, does the basic scenario work; at one month, what do the numbers say; at the end, decide. There are three possible decisions: scale up, rework with a new deadline (once!), or close.

The word “rework” should appear in a pilot exactly once. After that it becomes a chorus.

### Mistake four: nobody decides

A pilot needs an owner: one person with the authority to decide and the budget for the next step. Decisions that need “alignment” tend to get aligned in time for next year’s budget, and the pilot hibernates through the winter. The working group can include production, finance, IT and safety. One person decides.

### Mistake five: ideal conditions

Pilots love the easiest line, the day shift, the best operator and the supplier’s engineer standing behind them. The results are brilliant. Then the robot is rolled out to ordinary lines with ordinary people on the night shift, and the numbers drop. Test in conditions close to real operation: same product mix, same floor, same traffic, same operator. A pilot in a greenhouse proves the robot does well in greenhouses.

### Mistake six: no plan for “yes”

Many pilots stall precisely after they succeed. The robot works, everyone agrees, and nobody knows what happens next: how many units, which lines, what budget, who maintains the fleet. Sketch the scale-up before the pilot starts: if it succeeds, the next step is N robots on these lines, with indicative lead times, budget and the person writing the business case. Then a successful pilot ends with an order, not a round of applause.

### The one-page pilot

1.  One question the pilot answers.
2.  Technical criteria with success, rework and failure thresholds.
3.  The economic number that makes payback work.
4.  Staffing needed to run the robot.
5.  Timeline and checkpoints.
6.  One decision owner.
7.  Conditions close to real operation.
8.  What happens after “yes”, and after “no”.

### How we do it

Before shipment we shortlist models from partner factories (we buy directly from more than 100 Chinese manufacturers), calculate payback and agree with the client which numbers the pilot must confirm. We show the actual unit on video with its serial number and test it before shipment, so some technical questions are settled on the factory floor. On site, one team runs the project from selection to launch, so nobody has to re-explain what was agreed.

### The bottom line

A pilot is meant to be a short check before a big decision. If it has become a permanent exhibit, the decision has simply been postponed and the robot has become the excuse. The most useful outcome of a pilot is the date it ended. Even if it ended with “no”.
