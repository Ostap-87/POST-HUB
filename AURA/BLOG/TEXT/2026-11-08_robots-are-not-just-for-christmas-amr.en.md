Every November, an e-commerce warehouse turns into a railway station. Orders surge, temporary staff look for aisle B-14, permanent staff look for the temporary staff, and the site manager looks for room for one more packing bench. By mid-January it all calms down, and someone in a meeting says: “Next year, we’re getting robots.”

Good idea. The trouble is how it usually ends: the robots are sized for the peak. Here is why AMR payback is earned in the ordinary months, and why peak season is a bonus rather than the business case.

### 1. A market still growing, just more slowly

According to Data Insight, Russian online retailers and marketplaces handled 8.3 billion orders in 2025, up 24% on the previous year. That is the slowest growth in eight years, but it is still billions of parcels, and marketplaces handled 81% of them.

For a warehouse this means more order lines and smaller orders. Pickers increasingly cross the entire building for a single item.

### 2. Where a picker’s shift goes

A classic warehouse engineering textbook, Tompkins et al., “Facilities Planning”, estimates that travel accounts for about 50% of order picking time on average. Half the shift is spent walking to the goods rather than picking them.

That is exactly what AMRs address. In a “robot-to-picker” setup, pickers stay in their zone while robots carrying totes come to them. In “goods-to-person”, robots bring whole shelving units to a workstation. Either way, people walk less and pick more.

Vendor figures vary, and they deserve a sober reading. One well-documented case is the US retailer evo, which went live with Locus Robotics AMRs in 53 days in 2021. Average picking rates rose from 35 to 90 units per hour, with some staff reaching 125 or more. That is a single project with vendor-reported numbers, but it shows the order of magnitude: multiples, not percentages.

A warehouse robot does not carry anything for you. It just cancels your daily hike.

### 3. Peak and base: two different economies

A warehouse that ships N lines in a normal month may ship 1.5–2N or more at peak. There are three ways to prepare.

One: buy a fleet sized for peak. Half the robots rest by their chargers for most of the year, but everyone is busy in December.

Two: buy for base load and hire temporary staff for peak, as before.

Three: buy for base load and rent extra robots for peak. Some vendors offer this, but availability in Russia needs to be checked case by case.

The numbers make the difference clear.

### 4. An illustrative calculation

All figures are illustrative.

A warehouse handles 8,000 lines a day normally and 16,000 during two peak months. A manual picker does 60 lines an hour; with AMRs, 150. A picker hour costs 650 roubles fully loaded (for reference, SuperJob and Ozon put the median picker salary in Moscow at 80,000 roubles a month in 2025, before employer taxes).

Normal season: 133 picker hours a day without robots, 53 with them. Savings of 80 hours a day, about 1.56 million roubles a month.

Peak: 267 hours without robots, 107 with. Savings of 160 hours a day, about 3.1 million a month, if the fleet can handle the full volume.

**Sized for peak.** Sixteen robots with chargers, WMS integration, Wi-Fi upgrades and training: 26 million roubles. Maintenance: 200,000 a month. Annual savings: ten normal months at 1.56 million plus two peak months at 3.1 million, about 21.8 million. Minus 2.4 million maintenance. Payback around 16 months.

**Sized for base.** Eight robots for 14 million (half the robots, but integration and infrastructure do not halve). Maintenance: 100,000 a month. The robots cover base volume all year; staff handle the extra at peak, as before. Annual savings around 18.7 million, minus 1.2 million. Payback around 10 months.

Now look at the eight “peak” robots on their own. They cost about 12 million, save about 3.1 million a year (in the two peak months only) and need 1.2 million in maintenance. Payback: more than six years.

A peak-only robot is like a Christmas ornament: it sparkles for two months and spends the other ten in a box.

### 5. What the spreadsheet misses

The arithmetic is honest but incomplete. At peak, the expensive part is not only the labour hour but everything around it: recruiting temps, training them, first-week errors, mis-picks and returns. AMRs help here too. The robot’s screen shows what to pick and how many, so a newcomer gets productive faster. In a Lockheed Martin deployment, the project lead cited on-screen directions as one reason productivity rose.

So the sensible strategy is usually this: size the fleet for base load and let it pay back in ordinary months. At peak, it reduces the number of temps you need and makes them productive sooner. Anything beyond that should be calculated separately, without enthusiasm.

### 6. Before the robots arrive

Robots do not fix inventory records; they rely on them. You need location-based storage, accurate stock in the WMS, level floors, Wi-Fi in every aisle and space for chargers. If the system says an item is in B-14 and it is actually in B-41, the robot will take the picker straight to B-14. Quickly, politely and to the wrong place.

WMS integration deserves its own line in the project plan. Otherwise the robots arrive before anyone has given them permission to work.

### The bottom line

Peak season is what convinces you to buy robots. What pays for them is an ordinary Tuesday in March, when the warehouse runs quietly and nobody thinks about the robots at all.

If nobody thinks about them in March, the project worked.
