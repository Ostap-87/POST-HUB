Every robot has a CV. A new one has a short CV: built at the factory, tested, shipped. An ex-demo unit has a longer one: four days on a trade-show stand, waving at visitors, posing for selfies, being prodded, and on one occasion, apparently, offered a sandwich. A used robot may have a CV you would rather read before you buy it than after.

“Is there anything cheaper?” is a question we hear regularly, and it is a fair one. China’s robot market has changed so much in two years that a second-hand robot is no longer exotic. Here is where the savings are, and where it is a raffle.

### A market that grew by itself

The used industrial robot market has been around for a long time: arms retired from car plants move to smaller manufacturers, get refurbished, repainted and resold. Astute Analytica put the global used and refurbished robot market at about $1.9 billion in 2022 and projected $5.1 billion by 2031. Treat a nine-year forecast like a nine-year weather forecast, but the scale is clear: this is an industry, not a flea market.

With humanoids and robot dogs in China it happened faster. In early 2025, after Unitree robots performed at the CCTV Spring Festival Gala, renting a humanoid for an event reportedly cost up to 30,000 yuan a day. By the end of 2025 that had fallen to around 3,000 yuan, and robot dogs could be rented for as little as 339 yuan a day. In June 2026 Jiemian and Taiwan’s China Times reported that engineering prototypes pre-ordered a year earlier for close to a million yuan were being cleared on resale sites, and early research units that cost 300,000–800,000 yuan were reselling for 30,000–60,000. Current-year models were reported to resell at roughly 60–70% of the new price.

It is tempting to order a truckload. Do not rush. The price in the listing is the price of the robot. The cost of ownership is the price of its history.

### Three kinds of “not new”

**Ex-demo units.** Robots the manufacturer took to shows, demonstrated to clients or kept in a showroom. Low hours, but an unusual life: lots of power cycles, transport, demo routines and, for humanoids, falls on stage, quite literally. The upside: the manufacturer stands behind it and can provide the history and a warranty.

**Refurbished robots.** Arms or [cobots](/en/catalog/cobots) that have been serviced: new grease, belts, sometimes gearboxes and cables. Quality depends entirely on who did the work. The manufacturer or its service partner is one thing; an unknown workshop is another.

**Plain used.** Sold by the previous owner. It may be an excellent machine from a cancelled project, or a machine retired because it was tired. You cannot tell from the outside.

A used robot is like a used car, except that everyone knows how to read a car’s odometer.

### What to check

**Hours.** Industrial robot and cobot controllers usually log operating time and cycle counts. Ask for a screenshot or export from the controller itself, not the number in the ad. Gearbox and drive life is measured in hours; 30,000 hours and 3,000 hours are different purchases, however fresh the paint.

**Error log.** Emergency stops, overloads, collisions. One incident is normal. Repeated errors on the same joint deserve a question.

**Mechanics.** Joint backlash, gearbox noise, cable wear at bend points, grease leaks. Run a repeatability test; a robot that misses the point in the seller’s shop will miss it in yours.

**Battery.** For mobile robots, robot dogs and humanoids this may be the key item. Lithium batteries age with every cycle and with time on the shelf. Ask how many cycles and what capacity remains. A new battery for a humanoid can be a noticeable share of a used robot’s price.

**Software and accounts.** Whose software licence is it, does it transfer, which firmware version, and will the unit get updates? For robots bound to a cloud account or app, check the unit has been unbound from the previous owner. Otherwise you have bought a robot that still listens to someone else.

**Completeness.** Teach pendant, charging station, cables, hands or grippers, transport case, documentation. An industrial robot without its pendant turns a bargain into an escape room.

**Warranty and service.** Any warranty at all, who repairs it, and are parts still available for this version? Early humanoids are especially exposed: the maker has released two newer models, and parts for the first may run out before your enthusiasm does.

**Import paperwork.** Used equipment still has to clear customs and meet conformity requirements in the destination market. Discuss the procedure for second-hand machinery with your customs broker in advance, not at the bonded warehouse.

### When it makes sense

An ex-demo or used robot makes sense for teaching and research, where having a robot matters more than five years of service life; as a second unit for debugging programs while the main one runs the line; when you already run the same model and have an engineer who knows it; and for a simple industrial arm on an undemanding task.

It almost never makes sense for a three-shift, critical operation, when nobody can repair it, or when the saving is smaller than the cost of one serious breakdown.

A purely illustrative example: a new cobot costs 100 units, a used one 60, but after a year the used one needs a 15-unit gearbox and two weeks of downtime. The saving is no longer 40; it is under 25, minus two weeks of lost output. A used robot needs a payback calculation even more than a new one.

### A new robot should show its papers too

Almost everything above applies to new robots as well. A new machine can also turn out to be a “show unit” if nobody checks. ### The bottom line

A cheap robot can be a good deal. It can also be the opposite. The difference is not the price but how much you know about its past.

A trade-show robot has a very public past: thousands of people have seen it. The only question is whether anyone has seen its error log.
