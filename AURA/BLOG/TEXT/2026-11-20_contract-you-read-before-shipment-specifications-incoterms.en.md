A contract with a Chinese factory is usually read twice. The first time before signing, quickly, because the factory is waiting for the deposit. The second time when something has gone wrong, slowly, with a pencil and a lawyer. The second reading is always the more careful one. This article is about swapping them round.

At Aura Robotics we buy robots directly from Chinese factories and sign these contracts regularly. Here is what belongs in them, in the order people usually remember it too late.

### 1. The specification: a contract for a robot, not for “a robot”

The most important part of the contract is not about money. It is the specification annex: model and variant, configuration (hands, [gripper](/en/catalog/robotarms), compute, sensors, how many batteries), software version, scope of supply, documentation and its language, packing requirements.

If performance matters — payload, cycle time, repeatability, battery runtime — write it down as measurable values, and state how each will be tested. “High performance” is not a specification. It is a wish.

Spell out what is in the price and what is not: programming, training, remote commissioning, spare parts. Whatever is not written down, the factory will in good faith assume is excluded. And it will be right.

### 2. Incoterms: who is responsible for the cargo, where and when

Incoterms are the International Chamber of Commerce rules that define where the seller hands over goods, who pays for carriage and insurance, and when risk passes to the buyer. The current edition is Incoterms 2020: eleven rules, seven for any mode of transport and four for sea and inland waterway.

Three come up most often when buying robots from China.

**EXW (Ex Works).** The factory makes goods available at its gate; everything after that is yours, including Chinese export clearance. It sounds cheap, but handling export formalities in China is awkward for a foreign buyer. Minimum seller responsibility easily becomes maximum buyer hassle.

**FCA (Free Carrier).** The factory hands export-cleared goods to your nominated carrier. For equipment this is often the most practical option. Under the 2020 edition the parties can agree that the carrier will issue an on-board bill of lading, which helps with bank payments.

**FOB and CIF.** The classic sea terms. They are often used out of habit for container shipments, even though FCA and CIP suit containers better: under FOB, risk passes when goods are loaded on board, while your container was handed over at the terminal well before that.

One more 2020 detail: under CIP the seller must insure on extended terms (Institute Cargo Clauses A), under CIF only on minimum terms. A robot that costs as much as an apartment deserves more than minimum cover.

Finally, Incoterms do not govern transfer of title and do not replace the contract. They are not law. They are a dictionary, but one both sides use.

### 3. Payment stages: money in exchange for facts

Paying 100% up front is trust. Trust is lovely, but in a contract it is better broken into stages, each tied to a verifiable event.

A common pattern for equipment (illustrative):

- 30% deposit on signing, when the factory starts production or reserves a unit;
- 60% after the factory acceptance test (FAT), before shipment;
- 10% after site acceptance (SAT) or commissioning.

The proportions vary. What matters is that each payment is tied to an event backed by a document. “When ready” is a poor event. “On signing the factory test report under the programme in Annex 3” is a good one.

A word on how to pay. Since 2024, payments from Russia to China have become noticeably harder: Chinese banks have tightened compliance, and transfers take longer and cost more. Agree the payment route and currency before signing, put them in the contract, and build extra time into the schedule for the payment to clear. A letter of credit under UCP 600 is still an option and fits staged payments well, though not every bank or factory is willing to use one.

### 4. Factory acceptance: the exam before shipment

The Factory Acceptance Test is the buyer’s main tool. The robot is tested at the factory before shipment against an agreed programme: which operations, which parts, how many cycles, what tolerances, who signs the report.

Make the test programme an annex to the contract. If it is written after the deposit, the factory will write it.

We run these checks before shipment and show the client the actual unit with its serial number on video from the factory floor. Put the serial number in the test report too. Then the robot that arrives is the one that passed the exam, not a namesake.

### 5. Warranty, service and penalties

A warranty needs a duration, a start date and a scope. Duration: how many months. Start: shipment, commissioning or site acceptance, which can be six months apart. Scope: parts, labour, parts shipping, on-site or remote support, software updates.

Specify spare parts lead times and support response times. A robot waiting three months for an actuator is formally under warranty and effectively retired.

Late-delivery penalties are usually a percentage of the price per week with an overall cap. Without a cap the factory will not sign; without penalties it will not hurry.

### 6. Law, disputes and language

Russia and China are both parties to the 1980 UN Convention on Contracts for the International Sale of Goods (CISG). Unless the parties expressly exclude it, it applies automatically. That is no bad thing: it covers delivery, non-conformity and remedies in detail. Just know it is there, rather than discovering it in a dispute. One more detail: Russia has made a declaration requiring contracts to be in writing, so sign the contract, its amendments and any termination on paper, not by voice message.

Disputes usually go to arbitration, for example the China International Economic and Trade Arbitration Commission (CIETAC) or a tribunal in a third country. Both countries are parties to the 1958 New York Convention on the recognition and enforcement of foreign arbitral awards.

Contracts are usually bilingual. State which version prevails. Otherwise, in a dispute, you will discover you have two contracts, and both are right.

### 7. Currency control: remember your own bank

For Russian importers, a contract worth 3 million roubles or more must be registered with the authorised bank before the first payment, which assigns it a unique contract number. Between 1 and 3 million roubles, documents go to the bank without registration. The bank will want delivery deadlines in the contract, so make the delivery date realistic, not optimistic.

### The short list

1.  A specification with measurable values and how they are tested.
2.  An Incoterms 2020 rule with a named place.
3.  Payment stages tied to documented events.
4.  The factory acceptance programme as a contract annex.
5.  Warranty: duration, start date, scope, spare parts lead times.
6.  Penalties with a cap.
7.  Governing law, arbitration, prevailing language.
8.  Payment route and bank registration.

### The bottom line

A good contract with a Chinese factory is as dull as the instructions on a fire extinguisher. You read it carefully once and hope never to need it again.

Usually you won’t. Precisely because you read it before, not after.
