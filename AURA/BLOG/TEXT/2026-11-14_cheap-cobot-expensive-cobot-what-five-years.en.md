The “Chinese or European?” debate usually starts with the list price and ends there too. One side says, “It’s half the price.” The other says, “Buy cheap, buy twice.” Both are right, and neither has done the maths.

The nice thing about “buy cheap, buy twice” is that it never says what the second purchase costs. Let’s find out.

At Aura Robotics we source [cobots](/en/catalog/cobots) directly from Chinese manufacturers, so it would be easy to suspect us of bias. To make that suspicion well founded, we are going to calculate five-year cost of ownership rather than price, and show where a Chinese cobot wins and where the difference gets eaten.

### 1. The market: half the units, a third of the money

According to Interact Analysis, China has taken more than half of global cobot shipments since 2023. In 2025 its share was about 55% of units but only about 35% of revenue. The global cobot market in 2025 was roughly 57,000 units and just over US$1.2 billion.

Divide one by the other (roughly, for order of magnitude only) and average revenue per cobot comes out at around US$13,000 in China versus about US$30,000 elsewhere. An important caveat: this splits the market by where robots are sold, not by brand origin, and mixes different classes of robot. But a gap of more than two times is a property of the market, not a marketing claim.

Interact Analysis also notes that price cuts in China were significant in 2024 and almost stopped in 2025. Price wars end: manufacturers have started counting margins.

### 2. The robot is the smaller part of the project

Here is the key point. The arm is rarely the most expensive line in an automation project. Gripper, tooling, pedestal, safety sensors, vision, programming and commissioning cost roughly the same whoever made the robot in the middle of the cell.

So a 2x difference in robot price becomes perhaps a 20–30% difference in project cost. Still significant, but no longer “half the price”.

The robot sits in the middle of the cell. The money usually sits around the edges.

### 3. What goes into five years

Total cost of ownership is made of lines the catalogue never prints:

**Purchase and integration.** The robot plus everything around it.

**Software.** Base firmware is usually included. Force control, advanced safety functions, offline programming and gripper plug-ins may be paid options. Ask for the list before you buy, not at the first update.

**Training.** Who trains your operators and engineers, in which language, at what cost. An interface in a language your team reads is not a detail; it is how fast they learn.

**Spare parts.** Which parts to keep on the shelf, what they cost, and how long they take to arrive when the shelf is empty.

**Service and updates.** Who comes when the robot stops. Who updates the firmware, and whether the warranty survives it.

**Downtime.** The most expensive line, and the one nobody writes into the budget.

### 4. Downtime: the line that decides

Downtime costs whatever the stopped cell did not produce. If a cobot tends a machine that earns 50,000 roubles a day, two weeks waiting for a gearbox cost 700,000 roubles. That can exceed the entire saving on the robot.

The conclusion is unwelcome to fans of catalogue comparisons: reliable parts supply matters more than the price gap. A European cobot with no official service in your country and parts arriving via third parties can end up costing more than a Chinese one. A Chinese cobot bought through a chain of resellers with no link to the factory can too. What loses is not the country of origin but the length of the chain to the factory.

A robot waiting for a part costs more than a robot sitting in a warehouse. In the warehouse, at least, nobody is waiting for it.

### 5. Ecosystem: where Europe is still ahead

To be fair, the European market leaders have accumulated more ready-made solutions: grippers, plug-ins, application examples, experienced integrators, documentation. That saves engineering hours, and engineering hours are part of ownership cost too.

Chinese manufacturers are closing the gap: standard ISO 9409-1 flanges, common industrial protocols (Modbus, Profinet, EtherNet/IP), compatibility with popular grippers. But compatibility should be tested on your configuration, not taken from the word “compatible” in a slide deck.

### 6. Illustrative calculation

For arithmetic only. One machine-tending cell, two shifts, five years. Illustrative figures in millions of roubles.

| Line                      | Chinese cobot, direct from factory | European cobot |
|---------------------------|------------------------------------|----------------|
| Robot                     | 1.5                                | 3.0            |
| Integration and tooling   | 2.0                                | 2.0            |
| Paid software options     | 0.1                                | 0.3            |
| Training                  | 0.1                                | 0.2            |
| Spare parts kit           | 0.2                                | 0.4            |
| Maintenance over 5 years  | 0.5                                | 0.6            |
| **Total before downtime** | **4.4**                            | **6.5**        |

A gap of 2.1 million. Now add downtime. If one option has three more weeks of waiting for parts over five years than the other, at 50,000 a day that is about 1 million roubles. The gap halves. Two such episodes, and it nearly disappears.

The point is not which column wins. The point is that comparing columns without a downtime line is meaningless, and that line depends on service, not on the flag on the box. (Illustrative numbers. Yours will differ, and that is fine.)

### 7. What to ask any cobot supplier

1.  Which software options are paid, and what do updates cost?
2.  Which spare parts should we keep on site, and at what cost?
3.  How many days does an out-of-stock part take?
4.  Is remote diagnostics available?
5.  Who trains our staff, and what languages does the interface support?
6.  Is the warranty valid when we buy through you rather than directly?
7.  What service life does the manufacturer state for the main components?

The last question is rarely asked. It should be: five years is not the warranty period, it is the period in which the robot has to pay for itself.
