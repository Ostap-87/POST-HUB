In press releases, robots fry burgers, place dumplings and smile for the camera. No press release has ever featured a robot washing a crate. A pity, because a crate after a shift in a food plant is where the real work is: no audience, no applause.

[Cleaning](/en/catalog/cleaning) and sanitation are the most underrated part of food production. They add no output, so nobody likes to cost them. Yet they consume water, chemicals, heat and, above all, hours in which the line is not running. At Aura Robotics we source equipment from factories in China, and in central kitchen and food plant projects washing is often the fastest way to save money. It just never makes the headlines.

### 1. Four levers of clean

Every cleaning process rests on four factors, known in the food industry as Sinner’s circle after the chemist Herbert Sinner, who described it in the late 1950s: **time, temperature, chemistry and mechanical action**. Weaken one, and the others must work harder.

Manual washing relies on mechanics (a brush, hands, a tired shoulder) and on time. Automated washing relies on stable temperature, precise dosing and a flow of water that does not get tired by the end of the shift.

A person washes the first hundred crates well. A machine washes all of them the same.

### 2. CIP: cleaning without dismantling

Clean-in-place means cleaning pipes, tanks, kettles and heat exchangers without taking them apart: cleaning solutions run through the same circuit the product did. The Tetra Pak Dairy Processing Handbook gives a typical programme for a circuit with hot surfaces:

- warm water rinse for about 10 minutes;
- 0.5–2% alkaline detergent for about 30 minutes at 75 °C;
- intermediate rinse for about 5 minutes;
- 0.5–1.5% acid for about 20 minutes at 70 °C;
- final rinse and cooling.

“Cold” circuits get a shorter programme. Flow velocity matters too: 1.5–3 m/s in pipes keeps the flow turbulent so it scrubs like a brush. A slow flow merely introduces the detergent to the pipe.

In a central kitchen, CIP covers kettles with agitators, sauce tanks and transfer lines: everything that used to be dismantled and scrubbed by hand after each shift.

**Single-use or recovery.** In single-use systems the solution goes to drain after each cycle. In recovery systems it is collected, topped up to strength and reused until it is too dirty. The handbook also notes the catch: long pipe runs from a central station hold a lot of liquid, dilute the detergent and push up the cost with distance. That is why large plants put satellite units next to groups of equipment.

### 3. Crate washing: a tunnel instead of a sink

Crates, trays, gastronorm pans and pallets circulate every day, and there are lots of them. A tunnel washer handles them on a conveyor through a series of zones. Using one European manufacturer as an example:

- **pre-wash** at about 30–40 °C, kept low because proteins start to coagulate at about 48 °C and bake onto plastic;
- **main wash** with detergent at 50–70 °C, water filtered and recirculated;
- **rinse** at up to 80 °C;
- **blow-off** with heated air.

Large double-lane lines handle 2,240 to 5,600 crates an hour depending on version and crate type. A central kitchen for 20 outlets does not need that, but the order of magnitude is useful.

The key to tunnel economics is the direction of the water. Typically, fresh water enters only at the final rinse and then cascades back into the dirtier zones. Manual washing usually does the opposite: clean water runs everywhere, because that is easier.

### 4. Line sanitation

Conveyors, forming machines and slicers cannot be cleaned in place. They are cleaned from the outside with foam stations, low-pressure systems and sanitiser dosing. Automation is more modest here, but a central foam station with accurate dosing still beats a canister and a bucket.

And only equipment designed to be cleaned is easy to clean. Open gaps, blind cavities, threaded fasteners above the product and poorly protected electrics turn sanitation into archaeology. We cover what to check in hygienic design in a separate article on stainless steel, HACCP and food-grade lubricants.

### 5. Required, not just profitable

The Customs Union food safety regulation TR CU 021/2011 requires manufacturers to run HACCP-based procedures. The mandatory list explicitly includes keeping premises, equipment and tools in a condition that prevents contamination, choosing cleaning and disinfection methods and frequency, and keeping records. Article 15 adds that equipment in contact with food must allow cleaning and disinfection.

For Russian catering, new sanitary rules, SanPiN 2.3/2.4.4282-26, have applied since 1 September 2026. According to published reviews, they spell out, for example, how trays are handled: after every use and always at the end of the day, either in a dishwasher with a disinfection mode or by hand with disinfectants.

A machine that logs its cycles provides something a bucket never will: a record you can show an inspector. Temperature, concentration and time, none of it taken on trust.

### 6. Payback: water, chemicals, hours

The formula is simple:

**Monthly savings = (manual washing hours × hourly cost) + (water and effluent) + (chemicals) + (heating energy) + (line hours freed by CIP × margin per hour)**

An illustrative example. A central kitchen washes 3,000 crates and pans a day. By hand, that is three washers on each of two shifts: six people at a fully loaded 90,000 roubles a month each, or 540,000. With a tunnel washer, one operator per shift loads it: 180,000. Labour savings are 360,000 roubles a month. Add 20,000–30,000 saved on water, chemicals and heating, subtract 30,000 for maintenance, and net savings are about 350,000. On an 8 million rouble project including installation and connections, payback is around 23 months. (Illustrative numbers, for arithmetic only. Local water and effluent tariffs can shift the result noticeably.)

With CIP the economics differ: the main line is not water but time. If CIP cuts kettle and pipe cleaning from three hours to one, the line gains two hours. Those hours become either extra output or a shift that ends earlier.

Also ask your water utility about effluent limits. Fat and caustic in the drain often cost more than the water from the tap.

### 7. What to ask the manufacturer

1.  Fresh water, chemical and energy use per cycle or per crate: measured, not calculated?
2.  Is there heat recovery and solution recovery?
3.  How are concentration (conductivity) and temperature controlled, and is there a log?
4.  How is spray device coverage in tanks verified (a riboflavin test, for example)?
5.  Which containers is the machine set up for, and what does changeover to another cost?
6.  What steel are the tanks and pipes, what seals, what electrical ingress rating?
7.  Which spare parts (pumps, nozzles, sensors) should sit on site?

### The bottom line

Nobody writes press releases about washing, because it creates nothing new. It just puts everything back the way it was. Every single day. That is precisely its value: the most reliable automation is the kind nobody notices while it works.

Clean work is when, in the morning, nobody knows what happened overnight.
