Every food machine in a photo shines the same way. Stainless steel in a photo is like a smile at a job interview: everyone has one, and it tells you nothing about character. Character shows a year later, in brine, after the five hundredth caustic wash.

At Aura Robotics we source equipment directly from factories in China, including for food plants and central kitchens. Here is what we advise checking before payment and at acceptance. The list is long, and every line on it was earned in practice.

### 1. Steel grade: 304, 316 and 201

Stainless steel is steel with at least 10.5% chromium. Beyond that come differences you cannot see.

**304** is the workhorse food grade: 18–20% chromium, 8–10.5% nickel. Fine for most products.

**316** is the same base plus 2–3% molybdenum for better resistance to chlorides. It is the choice wherever salt is involved: meat, brines, sauces, aggressive cleaning. Chlorides attack the passive layer of 304 and cause pitting.

**201** is a chromium-manganese grade in which some expensive nickel is replaced by manganese: 16–18% chromium, 3.5–5.5% nickel, 5.5–7.5% manganese. The Australian Stainless Steel Development Association (ASSDA) states plainly that the corrosion resistance of such grades is generally inferior to similar chromium-nickel grades. For pots and bowls that are formed rather than welded, that may be acceptable. For a welded frame in a wet production area, it is a risk.

Swapping 304 for 201 is a classic way to shave a few percent off a quote. The trouble is that they look identical.

### 2. Why a magnet will not help

The favourite warehouse “test” is a magnet: if it does not stick, the stainless is good. Sadly not. Chromium-manganese grades are also non-magnetic and, as ASSDA puts it, at least initially appear to be stainless. Meanwhile 304 can become slightly magnetic after cold working. Magnetism has no relationship to corrosion resistance.

Reliable methods:

- **mill certificates** with the heat’s chemical composition (often an EN 10204 type 3.1 certificate), with heat numbers that match the actual sheets;
- **a handheld XRF analyser**, which gives the composition in seconds;
- **drop test kits** that tell chromium-manganese from chromium-nickel steel, and 304 from 316 by molybdenum.

A magnet is an excellent tool. For the fridge door.

### 3. Welds and surfaces

Good steel with a bad weld makes a bad machine. Check:

- **Surface roughness** on product contact surfaces. The European Hygienic Engineering and Design Group (EHEDG) recommends Ra 0.8 µm or smoother; US 3-A criteria are in the same range.
- **Continuous welds** without porosity, undercut or lack of fusion. Stitch [welding](/en/catalog/welding) on a frame is a row of small dirt pockets.
- **Removal of heat tint** at welds, and passivation. Dark oxide next to a weld marks a damaged passive layer and the place where corrosion starts first.
- **No crevices or blind cavities**: closed tubular sections beat angles and channels that collect whatever lands on them.
- **Contact with carbon steel.** Carbon steel fasteners or grinding sparks nearby leave rust spots on stainless. If a new machine already has “rust” that wipes off with a finger, ask where it was ground.

Only what is designed to be cleaned is easy to clean. Everything else just takes longer.

### 4. Lubricants: what drips into the product

Food machines have bearings, gearboxes, chains and guides above the product. Lubricant can reach the food through leakage or over-lubrication, so the category matters.

**NSF H1** lubricants are for incidental food contact; the registration is voluntary and checks the formulation against a list of permitted ingredients. **H3** covers cases where contact is unavoidable, such as meat hooks. **ISO 21469** goes further, certifying the whole process of lubricant design, manufacture, packaging and transport, including a hygiene risk assessment. A lubricant can hold both.

Check which lubricant the factory uses above the product zone and in gearboxes, whether it is registered, and where to buy it locally. A Chinese factory may fill a machine with food-grade grease, and a service crew a year later may top it up with ordinary grease “because that’s what we always use”.

### 5. Ingress protection: IP65 is not for washdown

If equipment is hosed down, the ingress rating of motors, sensors and control cabinets is a matter of survival. The top rating for high-pressure washdown is IP69K. The test is tough: 80 °C water at 80–100 bar, 14–16 litres a minute, the nozzle 100–150 mm from the surface, four angles for 30 seconds each. The rating originated in the German automotive standard DIN 40050-9 and was later absorbed into ISO 20653; IEC 60529 added an equivalent IPX9 test in its 2013 edition.

Not every machine needs IP69K. But if the datasheet says IP54 and the plant is cleaned with foam and hot pressurised water, a warranty dispute is guaranteed.

### 6. HACCP is not a sticker on a machine

Sometimes a supplier writes “HACCP-certified equipment”. That deserves a follow-up question. HACCP is a food safety management system run by the business that produces food. Under TR CU 021/2011 the manufacturer must develop, implement and maintain HACCP-based procedures. Equipment is a tool within that system, and Article 15 of the same regulation sets specific requirements for it:

- the design must allow washing, cleaning and disinfection;
- food contact materials must meet the requirements for materials in contact with food;
- working surfaces must be non-absorbent;
- control instruments where necessary.

So ask the factory not for a “HACCP certificate” but for documents on the materials: steel, plastics, gasket rubber, conveyor belts.

### 7. EAEU documents

Process equipment for the food industry, and for catering and institutional kitchens, is on the list of products that need a conformity document under TR CU 010/2011 on machinery safety at customs clearance: a declaration or a certificate. Electrical equipment usually also falls under the low-voltage and EMC regulations. Our article on EAEU certification covers the details, including the 2026 amendments to TR CU 010/2011. Prepare the documents before shipment, not after arrival.

### 8. Acceptance checklist

Before payment and at the factory acceptance test:

1.  Steel grade written into the contract specification, separately for product contact zones and the frame.
2.  Mill certificates with heat numbers; spot checks with XRF.
3.  Roughness of product contact surfaces and how it is measured.
4.  Welds: continuous, finished, no heat tint.
5.  No crevices, blind cavities or threads above the product.
6.  Food contact documents for plastics, rubber and belts.
7.  Lubricant: brand, H1 category, local availability.
8.  IP ratings of motors, sensors and cabinets to match your cleaning method.
9.  Tool-free disassembly for cleaning; time for a full sanitation cycle.
10. EAEU documents and a Russian-language manual.
11. A test on your product before shipment, not on water.
