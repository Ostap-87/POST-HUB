There is a short period when the robot is already yours but not yet working. It stands in its crate in the middle of the shop floor while people walk around it, take photos and ask, “So, when?” This is the most expensive stage of a robot’s life. It does nothing, and it has already been paid for.

How long this stage lasts almost never depends on the robot. It depends on what was ready when the robot arrived.

At Aura Robotics we source robots directly from Chinese factories and see a lot of start-ups. Here is how commissioning usually works in the market and where time gets lost. The specifics — who travels, for how many days, what is included in the price — go into the contract every time. If they are not in the contract, they do not exist.

### 1. Who commissions it: three scenarios

**A factory engineer comes to site.** The most reliable option and the slowest to organise: dates, flights, accommodation, an interpreter and the legal basis of the visit. Russia has extended visa-free entry for Chinese citizens until 31 December 2027, for stays of up to 30 days, for business, private and tourist trips. Work, however, is excluded. Is installing and tuning equipment at your site a business visit or work? That depends on the contract and on who pays whom. Ask a lawyer before booking flights.

**A local integrator.** A Russian company that knows the model or the equipment class. Upside: same language, same time zone, can come tomorrow. Downside: it may not have service-level access to the manufacturer’s software. Check in advance.

**Remote commissioning.** Your engineer on site, the factory engineer on video and on a remote connection to the controller. This works increasingly well for [cobots](/en/catalog/cobots), warehouse robots and standard cells. Beijing is five hours ahead of Moscow, so a morning on your floor is an afternoon at the factory: there is a shared window, but it is shorter than a working day.

Often the scenarios combine: remote preparation, a short site visit for start-up, remote support afterwards.

### 2. What the customer prepares: the main part of this article

The robot will not start working before its place is ready, and the factory does not prepare that place.

**Floor and anchoring.** For a fixed arm: a base or foundation to the manufacturer’s drawing, anchors, flatness. For a mobile robot: a floor without cracks and steps.

**Power.** Voltage, phases, rated power, connection point, earthing. Chinese industrial equipment runs at 50 Hz like Russian grids, but check the rated voltage and connectors against the datasheet. The electrician with the cable should arrive before the engineer, not after.

**Compressed air.** If the tooling uses pneumatics or vacuum: pressure, flow, air treatment.

**Network.** Wired connections, Wi-Fi for mobile robots, links to PLCs, machines or the WMS. And sign-off from your IT security team: if there will be remote access, they should hear about it from you, not from their logs.

**Safety.** Fences, scanners, light curtains, emergency stops — whatever the cell design requires.

**Parts and materials.** A real batch of your parts, cases or product for testing. Not three samples from the quality lab, but whatever arrives from the next station on an ordinary Tuesday.

**People and passes.** Who owns the start-up on your side, who will be trained, how the engineer gets through the gate. Days lost at the security desk are not unusual.

Agree a site readiness checklist with the supplier and close it before shipment. An engineer who arrives to find the robot unpowered is the most expensive tourist your plant will ever host.

### 3. Cell safety is not the robot’s job

New editions of ISO 10218-1 and ISO 10218-2 were published in 2025. Part 2 covers robot applications and cells: integration, risk assessment, verification of safety measures. The idea is simple: what is safe or unsafe is not the robot alone but the whole cell — robot, gripper, part, guarding and the people around it.

So the risk assessment is done for your cell on your site, not copied from a brochure. In the brochure the robot is always safe. It is alone there.

### 4. Training: always train two

Training usually comes in levels.

**Operator:** start, stop, change programs, understand error messages, stop safely.

**Technician or programmer:** edit paths, points and parameters, add a new part, recover after an emergency stop.

**Service engineer:** scheduled maintenance, replacing components, diagnostics, program backups.

A simple rule: train at least two people at each level. One person with the knowledge is not a capability; it is a hostage to the holiday schedule. Record the training on video, especially if it goes through an interpreter. Six months later nobody will remember exactly what the engineer meant by “always”.

A manual in Russian is not just for certification. It is what the night shift reads when there is no one to ask.

### 5. Site acceptance testing

Factory acceptance proved the robot works at the factory. Site acceptance proves it does your job at your site. Make the test program a contract appendix, as with factory acceptance.

Typical content:

- cycle time on real parts;
- continuous running for an agreed period, such as a full shift;
- defect or error rate;
- emergency stops and safety devices;
- data exchange with your systems;
- complete documentation and program backups.

If the final payment depends on acceptance, the criteria must be measurable. “The robot works well” is an impression. “At least 480 cycles per shift with no more than 0.5% defects” is a criterion.

### 6. How long it takes

Honestly: installing and tuning a standard cell or warehouse robot usually takes days, not months. The months go on everything before that: site preparation, tooling, approvals, passes and waiting for the part that is “arriving any minute now”.

Commissioning takes exactly as long as preparation did not.
