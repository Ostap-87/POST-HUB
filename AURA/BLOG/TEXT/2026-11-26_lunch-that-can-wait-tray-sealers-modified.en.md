A central kitchen has one simple job: cook today what people will eat tomorrow, the day after and, with luck, next week. Everything in that sentence is simple except “next week”.

A ready meal in an ordinary lidded container does not last long. The fridge helps, but bacteria can live in the cold too, just more slowly. Shoppers dislike preservatives and love the words “no preservatives”. Between those two wishes stands the machine this article is about: a tray sealer with gas.

At Aura Robotics we source equipment for food plants and central kitchens directly from factories in China. Here is how modified atmosphere packaging works, what a packing station consists of, and what to check before buying.

### 1. What the gas actually does

Modified atmosphere packaging (MAP) is simple in principle: air is removed from the tray and replaced with a gas mix containing almost no oxygen. Ready meals typically use carbon dioxide and nitrogen. CO₂ inhibits many bacteria and moulds; nitrogen is an inert filler that keeps the pack from collapsing.

Industry guidance for ready meals: 30–50% CO₂ and 50–70% N₂, roughly twice as much gas as product by volume, stored at 0 to +3 °C. Under those conditions shelf life can grow from 2–5 days in air to two or three weeks.

Three caveats, without which these numbers are dangerous.

**Gas does not replace cold.** MAP only works with an unbroken cold chain. A warm warehouse cancels any gas.

**Gas does not fix dirt.** If the food is contaminated after cooking, the pack preserves the problem rather than solving it. Hygiene, rapid chilling after cooking and a clean packing area matter more than the gas mix.

**Guidance figures are not your shelf life.** The manufacturer sets the shelf life and must validate it with lab testing on its own product, packaging and storage conditions. Until the tests are done, “three weeks” is a hypothesis, not a label.

Gas extends the life of food. But only food that was healthy when it went into the tray.

### 2. How a tray sealer works

The cycle: a filled tray enters the chamber, air is evacuated, gas is flushed in, film is sealed on top and trimmed to shape. All in a few seconds.

Machines come in three classes.

**Manual and tabletop** for small volumes and pilots. The operator places the tray, lowers the lid and waits.

**Semi-automatic**: trays loaded by hand, cycle automatic, several trays per chamber.

**Automatic inline**: built into a conveyor, self-feeding, multi-lane. For reference, European inline machines in this class claim up to 12 gas cycles per minute and up to 60 packs per minute. Chinese manufacturers build such machines too and show them at the leading international meat and ready-meal packaging fairs.

### 3. Gas: where it comes from and how to check it

Packaging gases are food additives (E290 for CO₂, E941 for nitrogen) and must be food grade, not borrowed from the workshop next door.

There are two usual sources: cylinders of premixed gas, or an on-site nitrogen generator plus a gas mixer. At high volumes the generator pays for itself in gas; at low volumes cylinders are simpler. Then there is mixer accuracy: if the setting says 40% CO₂ and 32% comes out, you are selling a different product from the one you tested in the lab.

Control means a gas analyser checking the mix and residual oxygen in finished packs — sampled to a schedule and logged. Without one, you do not know whether you packed a gas mix or just air with good intentions.

One more piece of physics: CO₂ dissolves into moist food. Too little gas relative to product, and a day later the pack sucks in and looks vacuum-sealed out of spite. Hence the two-to-one gas rule.

### 4. Tray and film: a marriage that has to seal

Trays and films are chosen together, or the seal fails.

**Tray material.** Polypropylene for microwave reheating. Crystallised PET (CPET) for oven reheating. Barrier laminates when long shelf life is needed.

**Barrier.** For MAP, both tray and film must keep gases in; otherwise within days the mix turns back into air. Ordinary thin plastic will not do.

**Anti-fog.** Film that does not mist up in the chiller. Shoppers want to see the food, not condensation.

**Material safety.** Food-contact packaging in the EAEU must comply with technical regulation TR CU 005/2011 on packaging safety.

The main rule: test the machine with your tray and your film. The Chinese factory will test with its own, and everything will seal perfectly. Yours will be different. The seal is where two suppliers meet, each certain the other one is to blame.

### 5. Portioning: where the time really goes

The tray sealer is rarely the bottleneck. Filling the trays is.

Central kitchen meals often have several components: a side, a protein, a sauce. Manual portioning is slow and imprecise; automatic portioning is expensive and not always feasible for chunky foods. Multihead weighers handle granular and piece goods, pump depositors handle sauces and porridges, but “cutlet plus mash plus sauce on the side” almost always stays a human job.

Hence a practical layout: people fill, the machine seals, then a checkweigher, metal detector and labeller. Keep the tray flange clean: a drop of sauce on the seal area means a leaking pack, and a leaking pack is food in air wearing a label for food in gas.

### 6. An illustrative calculation

For arithmetic only. A central kitchen produces 3,000 meals a day. A semi-automatic tray sealer runs 6 cycles a minute with 4 trays per cycle: 24 packs a minute, about 1,440 an hour. The whole volume needs just over two hours of machine time. So the thing to calculate is filling speed: four people each filling four trays a minute make 16 a minute, and the machine waits for the people.

Now the economics. Cost per meal is RUB 150. If longer shelf life cuts write-offs from 8% to 3%, the kitchen loses 90 meals a day instead of 240. That saves 150 meals, or RUB 22,500 a day — about RUB 650,000 a month over 29 working days. Against that: the machine, gas, barrier trays (dearer than ordinary ones) and shelf-life lab testing. (Illustrative numbers. Yours will differ, which is the point of calculating.)

The biggest gain is often not in write-offs, though. Longer shelf life lets you cook less often in larger batches and deliver further. That changes the logistics of the whole chain, not just the packaging line item.
