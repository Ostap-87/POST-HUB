In China, every supplier has a factory. Some own theirs. Some have a brother who owns one. Some have a “partner factory”, which roughly translates as “we know where it is.”

None of this is criminal. Trading companies are a normal part of Chinese commerce: they assemble catalogues, speak English, take payments and answer email at weekends. The trouble starts when a trading company calls itself a factory and the buyer believes it. Then the price carries an extra link, and the after-sales service an extra phone number where nobody knows the answer.

At Aura Robotics we work directly with more than a hundred Chinese manufacturers, and half the job is not finding a supplier but confirming it is who it says it is. Here is how.

### 1. Where to look

There are only a few channels, each with its own character.

Marketplaces such as Alibaba and Made-in-China are convenient, but factories and resellers sit side by side and are hard to tell apart from the storefront. A “verified supplier” badge means someone checked something, not necessarily what you need checked.

Trade shows — the World Robot Conference in Beijing, the World Artificial Intelligence Conference in Shanghai, the Canton Fair and industrial automation exhibitions — put you in front of engineers. A good sign: on a factory’s stand, engineers argue with each other about knee torque. On a reseller’s stand, a sales manager knows every model from every brand. Encyclopaedic breadth is a reason for caution.

The third channel is referrals: industry associations, other buyers, integrators. The fourth is the one we exist for: a partner whose factory relationships are already verified.

### 2. The registry: the dullest and most honest document

Every Chinese company has an 18-character Unified Social Credit Code. You can check it in the National Enterprise Credit Information Publicity System (gsxt.gov.cn), run by the market regulator. Access is free; the interface is in Chinese. Commercial aggregators such as Qichacha and Tianyancha present the same data more conveniently and add their own.

What to look at:

**Business scope.** The licence lists what the company is allowed to do. Manufacturing (制造, 生产) is a good sign. Only sales, trading, technical consulting and import-export suggests a trading company.

**Address.** A registered office in a Shenzhen tower and a production site in an industrial zone 100 km away is normal. A registered office in a tower and nothing else is worth a question.

**Founding date and capital.** A factory founded last year may be an excellent start-up. But if it talks about “twenty years of experience”, ask whose.

**Annual reports and abnormal-operation flags.** Companies that skip annual filings get flagged. Good factories do not live on that list.

A common myth, while we are here. Since 30 December 2022, China has abolished mandatory registration of foreign trade operators. So “we are licensed to export” no longer proves anything about being a factory. Nobody needs to register for that any more: not the factory, not the reseller, not the reseller’s cousin. (Licences for controlled goods still exist, but that is another story.)

### 3. Questions a middleman answers slowly

Some questions a factory engineer answers in a minute, while a middleman needs to “check with the technical department”.

What gearbox is in joint two, and who makes it? What is the actuator’s rated life and how was it tested? Which firmware versions shipped last year and what changed? How many units of this model have been built, and where did they go? Can we speak to the engineer responsible for the SDK?

A pause alone proves nothing; large factories forward questions too. But if every technical question takes a day while the discount question is answered in a minute, you know how the company is organised.

### 4. Video: not pretty, specific

A promotional video proves nothing; it was shot once for everyone. What proves something is a video shot for you: the specific unit, its serial number plate in close-up, today’s date on a sheet of paper in frame, and a factory floor around it rather than a white backdrop.

That is exactly what we do: show the client the robot with its serial number on video from the factory floor, and test it on their task before shipment. Ask any supplier for the same. A factory can film it in half an hour. A middleman will explain why now is not a good time.

A clip from the factory floor beats a deck from the office, especially if you can see the floor.

### 5. Audits and visits

When the amount is significant, an on-site check pays for itself. There are two routes.

The first is an independent audit. International [inspection](/en/catalog/inspection) firms (SGS, Bureau Veritas, TÜV and others) verify factories: does production exist, what capacity, is there a quality system, how many people work there. The cost is a small fraction of the order.

The second is to go yourself. Factories with something to show are happy to show it: assembly lines, test benches, component stores, R&D. Real production is obvious: products at different stages, benches full of cables, engineers in overalls, the smell of grease. So is a stage set: three identical robots in a showroom and a locked door behind which “they’re not working today”.

This is what our Aura Robotics Tour buying trips are for: several factories per trip, with negotiations and checks of specific units.

### 6. References: who bought before you

Ask who has already bought this model, especially in your industry and, if possible, in your region. Ask for a contact, not a logo on a slide. Logos on slides cost nothing; they can be downloaded like everything else.

A good sign is a factory volunteering where its robot struggled and what it changed afterwards. A manufacturer knows its product’s weak spots. A middleman knows only the strong ones.

### 7. When a middleman is fine

In fairness, trading companies can be useful: when you buy small quantities of varied equipment from several makers, when you need consolidated shipping and a single contract, or when the factory does not deal with foreign buyers directly. Paying for that is reasonable. Paying for a middleman while believing you are buying from the factory is not.

### A one-page checklist

1.  Company name and code in the registry match the contract and invoice.
2.  Business scope includes manufacturing.
3.  The production address exists and shows up on a map.
4.  No abnormal-operation flags or missed annual reports.
5.  Engineers answer technical questions without a day’s delay.
6.  Video of the specific unit with its serial number, from the factory floor.
7.  An audit or personal visit for significant orders.
8.  Real customer contacts, not logos.
9.  Bank details belong to the same company named in the contract.

The last point matters more than it looks. If the factory asks you to pay a different company’s account “because it’s easier”, stop and find out why.

### The bottom line

Finding a Chinese supplier is easy: they find you, the day after your first enquiry. Finding a factory is work.

But you only do that work once. After that you can work with the factory for years, and every order is easier than the last. That may be the clearest difference between a factory and an office: the factory does not move.
