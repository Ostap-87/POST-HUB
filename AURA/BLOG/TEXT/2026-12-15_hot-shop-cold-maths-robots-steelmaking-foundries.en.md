Steelmaking has jobs nobody makes adverts about. A person in an aluminised suit walks up to a bath of molten steel, dips a probe on a long lance, reads the temperature, takes a sample and steps back. Then does it again. Precise, heavy and hot in the most literal sense.

These are the tasks Chinese steelmakers hand to robots first. Not because a robot is cheaper than a steelworker, but because a steelworker, unlike a robot, cannot be replaced under warranty.

At Aura Robotics we source robots directly from Chinese factories and calculate payback for each specific task. In a hot shop that calculation works differently from a warehouse, and here is why.

### Where a robot works in the hot shop

The typical tasks are few, and they share one trait: being near them is unpleasant or dangerous for people.

**Sampling and temperature measurement.** The robot takes a probe or sampler from a magazine, dips it into the melt, withdraws it, ejects the spent cartridge and passes the sample on. It happens dozens of times a shift and demands the same immersion depth every time, which is exactly what robots are good at.

**Continuous casting.** On casters, robots fit and remove the ladle shroud, measure tundish temperature and oxygen, take samples, lance with oxygen and feed casting powder. One example is Primetals Technologies’ LiquiRob: as of March 2020 the company reported 31 systems worldwide, 21 on casters, five of them at Baosteel plants in Shanghai, Meishan and Zhanjiang, with four to five days of downtime to commission each one.

**Finishing and logistics.** At Baosteel Zhanjiang, city authorities reported in 2023 that robots handle unbinding, sleeve unloading, slag removal, grinding and labelling. Marking hot product is classic robot work: a person near hot metal spends their effort not getting burned; a robot spends it hitting the right spot.

**Foundries.** Three stories here: pouring, extracting castings, and the least loved of all, fettling and grinding. Cutting off risers, removing burrs, grinding flash means dust, noise, vibration and abrasives. A welder at least gets a mask. A fettler gets a respirator, ear defenders and the firm impression that the job was invented as a punishment.

A fettling robot never complains about vibration. It just asks for new grinding discs more often.

### How many robots do Chinese steelmakers have?

Or rather, the biggest one. According to China Daily, by the end of 2025 Baosteel (part of China Baowu) had 994 [industrial robots](/en/catalog/industrial) across its sites, or 216 per 10,000 employees. For comparison, in April 2026 the International Federation of Robotics recalculated China’s robot density using updated employment data and arrived at 166 robots per 10,000 manufacturing workers. The earlier figure of 470 had been inflated by an undercount of workers.

So even China’s steel flagship has half the robots per person of Chinese manufacturing overall. That is no surprise. A car body is thousands of identical weld spots; a melt shop is hundreds of different operations at temperatures most robots were never designed for. Steel automates slowly, but each operation it hands over tends to be one of the hardest.

### Why the payback is about safety

The usual model is simple: the robot costs X, replaces Y shifts, pays back in Z months. In a hot shop it fits poorly. There are few people at the furnace, their wages are not the main cost, and they cannot all be removed anyway.

The real value lies elsewhere. In a 2025 paper in the Journal of Development Economics, economists Wei Luo, Lixin Tang, Yaxin Yang and Xianqiang Zou studied robots and workplace injuries in China. They estimate that each additional robot per thousand workers is associated with about 0.25 fewer workplace accidents and 0.035 fewer fatalities per thousand population, and that robot adoption in 2013–2019 accounts for roughly two-thirds of the fall in accidents and over 70% of the fall in fatalities.

So an honest business case for a hot-shop robot includes fewer people in high-risk zones (and the protective equipment, medicals and hazard compensation that come with them), more consistent measurements and fewer repeat samples, continuity when it gets too hot to stand there, and the cost of a stoppage after an incident, which can exceed the price of the robot.

An illustrative example: if temperature measurement takes two people per shift across four shifts, a robot will not necessarily cut eight positions, but it can move them away from the furnace to other work. The savings look smaller than finance would like. The risk reduction is exactly what the technical director wants to see.

A furnace robot does not pay for itself in savings. It pays for itself in things that did not happen.

### What to ask the manufacturer

A metallurgical robot is usually a standard industrial arm plus whatever makes it fit for the shop, so the questions are mostly about that “plus”: heat protection (is there a foundry jacket, how is it cooled, how often is it replaced), protection against dust and metal splash and the IP rating of body and wrist, tooling and consumables (samplers, probes, grinding heads, cartridge magazines, and their annual cost), integration with the melt control system and the lab, and service: what happens if the robot stops mid-heat, and how quickly manual mode can be restored.

That last question matters more than it seems. A good hot-shop project always lets a person pick up the probe if the machine fails.

A hot shop is a place where the job has a temperature. Robots arrive slowly, one operation at a time, always the hardest one first. It is not a revolution. It is simply people standing a little further from the metal.
