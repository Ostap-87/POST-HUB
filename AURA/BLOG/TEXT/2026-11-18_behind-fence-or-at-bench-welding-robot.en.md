A welding robot and a welding cobot are related the way a tractor and a garden tiller are. Both dig. But ask a farmer which to buy and the first question will be: how much land do you have?

In our earlier article on welding we looked at when a robot pays back in a year and when it never will. Today’s question is which robot. An industrial arm in a fenced cell with a positioner and a programmer, or a collaborative arm on a welding table that the welder teaches by hand. At trade shows both weld beautifully. On the shop floor they behave very differently.

### 1. The standard has gone off the word “cobot”

Start with terminology, because it points to the main conclusion. In January 2025, new editions of ISO 10218-1 and ISO 10218-2, the industrial robot safety standard, were published. The term “collaborative robot” is gone; the standard now speaks of “collaborative applications”, and the collaborative content of ISO/TS 15066 has moved into Part 2.

The logic is simple: safety belongs to the whole task, not the arm. A cobot holding a torch, wire and an arc of several thousand degrees does not become safe because its joints are sensitive. It stops pushing on your hand. It does not stop glaring and smoking.

The cobot has good manners. The arc does not.

### 2. Safety: what the fence protects you from, and what it does not

An industrial welding cell is fenced: people stay out of the robot’s path, arc light and spatter stay inside, and fume extraction works on an enclosed volume.

A cobot sits on a welding table without a full fence. It limits force and speed; Universal Robots arms, for example, trigger a protective stop at roughly 50–100 N depending on settings and model. That helps with collisions. It does nothing for the rest. Welding equipment maker Lorch says plainly in its cobot guide that you need the same eye and face protection as for manual welding, screens for bystanders and fume extraction, and that the risk assessment covers the whole system. A torch hanging in the air during teaching burns just as well as one in a human hand.

So “no fence” does not mean “no measures”. It means different measures, and screens around the table all the same.

### 3. Batch size: how many identical parts

The main difference is economic. An industrial cell is built for large batches and long, repetitive seams. It is programmed offline or from a 3D model, and preparing a new part takes real time. After that it welds shift after shift, and with a two-station positioner the operator loads one side while the robot welds the other.

A cobot is built for small and medium batches with frequent changeovers. The welder takes the arm by the flange, traces the seam with the torch and marks points on a tablet. A new part can be programmed in hours rather than days. According to Lorch, operator training takes about two days versus weeks for a conventional cell.

The same guide gives rough limits for where a cobot fits: material roughly 1–15 mm thick (aluminium from 2 mm) and seams shorter than 1,500 mm. Long straight seams, through-arc seam tracking, external axes and positioners remain industrial robot territory.

If you weld the same frame a thousand times a month, you need a robot. If you weld a hundred different brackets ten at a time, you need a cobot. If you do both, you need both, and we mean that.

### 4. Speed: the weld is the same, everything else differs

The welding process runs at the same speed on a cobot and an industrial robot; the parameters set it, not the arm. The difference is everything around the weld. An industrial cell moves quickly between seams, has a positioner and never waits for a person. A cobot moves more slowly in collaborative mode, and a human puts the part on the table.

A cobot beats a welder on consistency, not speed: it does not tire or take breaks. A cell beats a cobot on volume per shift.

### 5. Space and installation

A classic welding robot needs a cell: a foundation or frame, a fence, a positioner, extraction, and access for a crane or forklift. The arm alone weighs around 180 kg. A welding cobot weighs about 30 kg and bolts to a table, which can move to another corner if needed.

For shops where space is short and will stay short, this matters. A cobot can go where yesterday’s manual welding booth stood. A cell goes only where you are willing to knock something down.

### 6. Price: two orders of project

According to Lorch, a cobot welding package costs around €60,000–70,000, roughly half the price of a conventional robotic cell, which usually runs to six figures in euros. These are one manufacturer’s figures for the European market; read them as a ratio, not a price list.

Chinese manufacturers have shifted this arithmetic. According to the IFR, China installed 295,000 industrial robots in 2024, 54% of the global total, and Chinese suppliers’ share of their home market passed half for the first time, reaching 57%. Chinese welding cobots are now more affordable than European ones, and Chinese-built industrial welding cells are no longer exotic.

Anywhere in the world, though, the arm is the smaller part of the budget: power source, torch, table or positioner, fixtures, extraction, screens or fencing, integration, training. For a cobot that list is shorter; for a cell it is longer.

### 7. An illustrative calculation

The numbers below are illustrative, for arithmetic only.

A steel fabrication shop has two areas. Area one makes frames in two sizes, 600 a month, with long repetitive seams. Area two makes brackets and embeds: 40 different part numbers in runs of 10–30.

Area one: an industrial cell with a two-station positioner costs 9 million roubles. Over two shifts it replaces the output of four welders per shift, with one operator per shift. Savings: (8 − 2) × 180,000 = 1.08 million roubles a month, minus 50,000 for upkeep. Payback: about 9 months.

Area two: the same cell would sit idle during changeovers. A 4-million-rouble cobot on one shift does the work of one and a half welders, while the welder at the table prepares the next part. Savings: 0.5 × 180,000 plus the extra output of a welder who no longer welds brackets by hand — call it 150,000 roubles a month. Payback: a little over two years.

The point is not the numbers. It is that “robot or cobot” is decided by the production mix, not the catalogue. One shop often needs both.

### 8. Questions to answer first

Take a year of output and split seams into repeat and one-off. Work out the average batch. Measure seam lengths and material thicknesses. Find where a cell could physically go. Decide who will program: an engineer in the office or the welder at the table. The answers usually make the choice for you.

### The bottom line

An industrial robot is for a factory that knows what it will make tomorrow. A cobot is for a factory that does not know, but is ready.

Most shops are somewhere in between. Which is why the honest answer to “robot or cobot?” is “show me your production plan.”
