Some robot makers are impossible to miss. They list on stock exchanges, release videos of dancing humanoids and hold launches with smoke machines. Others are known mostly to people who actually buy robots. Elite Robots belongs to the second group.

For a company this quiet, the name is almost a joke at its own expense. At Aura Robotics we source cobots directly from Chinese factories, and Elite turns up in our shortlists far more often than in headlines. Here is why. (We have compared Elite with [JAKA](/en/catalog/cobots/factory/jaka) and [Dobot](/en/catalog/cobots/factory/dobot) before; this is a profile of Elite on its own.)

### Who they are

The full name is Elite Intelligent Robot Co. Founded in Beijing in 2016, the company moved its headquarters to Suzhou Industrial Park in 2018. People often call it a Shanghai company; Suzhou is half an hour away by high-speed train, which to a foreigner is the same thing. To anyone from Suzhou, it very much is not.

The CEO is Cao Yunan. According to the company, around 40% of staff work in R&D, and key technology, from the controller operating system to the joint modules, is developed in-house.

Cumulative shipments exceed 20,000 robots, sold in more than 50 countries. Since 2021 Elite has built an overseas network: subsidiaries in Charleston (US), Munich and Nagoya, and offices or service points in Mexico, Australia, India and Portugal.

The customer it names most readily is BYD. According to the Chinese business press, Elite delivered about 3,000 robots to the carmaker during the EV boom.

Three thousand cobots for one customer is the kind of advertising you cannot film.

### Money: quiet, but serious

Elite is not listed and publishes no accounts. It does raise money, though: in March 2026 it closed a 600 million yuan Series D+ round, with backers including corporate funds from the data-centre supply chain, Lavender Hill Capital Partners, Dachen Ventures, Yuanhe Holdings and a Zhengzhou embodied-AI fund.

The data-centre investors are no accident. In 2026 Elite is pushing robots for assembling optical modules and AI infrastructure hardware, with an order book in that segment of around 100 million yuan, according to China Securities Journal. Two optical module makers are both its shareholders and its customers.

Its new strategy is “one brain, many forms”: cobots, mobile manipulators and general-purpose robots on one software platform, plus a “VLA+T” architecture that adds torque sensing to vision-language-action models. That is the future. A buyer today needs the present: production cobots.

### The ranges: CS and EC

**CS series**, the flagship: CS63, CS66, CS612, CS620 and CS625, with payloads from 3 to 25 kg and reach from 624 to 1,800 mm. Repeatability ranges from ±0.02 mm on small models to ±0.1 mm on the CS620. The website now also lists 30 kg models, the CS630 and CS530H. The CS line claims compliance with ISO 10218-1, ISO 13849 (PL d, Category 3) and ISO/TS 15066, CE and UL marking, and ISO Class 5 cleanroom compatibility, with more than 20 configurable safety functions. Arms are IP65, with IP68 as an option.

**EC series**, older and simpler: EC63, EC66, EC612, EC616, EC68-08 and the long-reach EC64-19 (1,900 mm, 4 kg). IP54, with CE, UL and KCs marking. It suits jobs without washdown or the full certified safety package.

Both series list an operating range of −10 to +50 °C. That sounds encouraging for unheated Russian workshops, but the datasheet describes the robot, not the grease, cables and gripper you bolt onto it. Both use a separate controller cabinet, which someone always remembers on installation day.

### Why “doesn’t make a fuss”

This is not about decibels. We found no noise figures in Elite’s documentation, so we will not invent any. It is about style: few big announcements, no humanoids on stage, and until 2026 barely any presence in English-language business press. Yet the certificates, shipments and customers are real.

For a buyer that cuts both ways. A company shipping thousands of units to one carmaker is unlikely to build for presentations, and ISO 13849 certification costs too much to do for a press release. On the other hand, a quiet company leaves less public information: no stock exchange filings, fewer independent reviews, fewer answers on integrator forums. So more of the checking falls on you: certificates for the specific model, warranty terms, spare parts lead times, documentation in your language.

A loud manufacturer tells you what the robot can do. A quiet one answers when asked. The trick is to ask.

### The competition

In China, Elite competes with JAKA, Dobot, AUBO and Han’s Robot. Dobot listed in Hong Kong in December 2024; the others vary in status and visibility. For buyers this is good news: payload ranges almost overlap, so the choice can rest on the task, delivery time and service terms rather than the catalogue.

Elite looks strong in electronics assembly, machine tending, cleanroom work and applications with demanding functional safety requirements. Compare more carefully for heavy palletizing, where rivals may have specialised models, and where a ready-made ecosystem of grippers and plug-ins matters.

### The bottom line

Elite Robots learned to build a product first, then to sell it by the thousand, and only then started talking about itself. In an industry where many do the reverse, that is almost exotic.

A quiet factory means one of two things: everything has broken, or everything works. The only way to tell is to walk in.
