Year-end reviews are traditionally written in late December, glass in hand. We are writing this one a little earlier and without the glass, and we say so upfront: it uses data available as of October 2026. China’s full-year 2026 figures will not appear until 2027, and the IFR’s report on 2026 will, on its usual schedule, come out in autumn 2027. Waiting for complete statistics would mean reviewing 2026 sometime around spring 2028.

So this is not an audit. It is a map: what we already know, what changed for buyers and what to watch in 2027.

### Industrial robots: China installs more than everyone else combined

In September 2026 the International Federation of Robotics published World Robotics 2026, covering 2025. China installed about 354,000 industrial robots, up 20% and a record. That is about 59% of global installations: almost three in every five new industrial robots in the world go into Chinese factories.

The US installed about 38,500 and moved into second place ahead of Japan (about 36,200, down 19%). South Korea installed about 30,000, Germany fewer than 25,000. Global installations exceeded 600,000, up 11%, and the operational stock reached about 5 million. For 2026 the IFR forecasts 9% growth to roughly 655,000 units.

For buyers the more telling figure is that Chinese manufacturers now supply 55% of their home market. At home, the Chinese robot is no longer the alternative. It is the majority.

Growth continued during the year: China’s National Bureau of Statistics reported industrial robot output up 34.6% year on year in August 2026.

A country that installs more robots than the rest of the world combined learns from its mistakes faster than anyone. Buyers get the machines where those mistakes have already been fixed.

### Humanoids: the year they started being counted

If 2025 was the year of humanoid videos, 2026 was the year of humanoid reports. People started counting units, and discovered they count differently.

The IFR, in its service robot release at the end of September 2026, put 2025 global sales of full-size humanoids at about 7,000 units. Chinese media cited an estimate of about 14,400 humanoids shipped in China in the same year. Nobody is necessarily wrong; they are counting different things: full-size or all sizes, sold or shipped, with or without educational models.

For the first half of 2026, data published by IT Home (as reported by TechNode) put global humanoid shipments at about 19,100, up from about 5,100 a year earlier, with Chinese makers holding more than 97%. [AgiBot](/en/catalog/humanoid/factory/agibot) led with about 8,400 units (44%), followed by Unitree with about 5,900. IDC had a different estimate, about 25,000 for the half-year. The sources agree on the main point: the market multiplied, and nearly all of it is Chinese.

Another shift: over 70% of first-half shipments went to industrial and commercial uses, up from about half a year earlier. The humanoid is slowly stepping off the stage and onto the shop floor, mostly into prepared operations and pilots for now, but the direction is clear.

Global humanoid statistics are like a forecast from three meteorologists: all promise warm weather, each with a different temperature.

### Prices: cheaper, then cheaper again

We covered prices separately, so briefly: Unitree G1, launched in 2024 from 99,000 yuan, was offered from 85,000 by June 2026. Unitree R1 fell from 39,900 to 29,900 yuan in a year. The educational Noetix Bumi costs under 10,000 yuan. Chinese media also reported collapsing humanoid rental rates and a glut of second-hand prototypes.

The takeaway is unchanged: the robot itself is getting cheaper faster than integration, training and tooling, so the share of the budget spent “around the robot” keeps growing.

### Money: robots go public

The financial event of the year was Unitree’s IPO. The company listed on the Shanghai STAR Market on 19 August 2026, raising about 6.1 billion yuan at 150.80 yuan a share and a valuation of about 61 billion yuan; the shares reportedly rose 629% on the first day. Unitree became the first general-purpose robotics company listed in mainland China.

For buyers a listed manufacturer means two things. The good: public reporting, more transparency, money for development and service. The cautious: a listed company cares about volume and revenue, so product lines will refresh quickly, which makes the question of how long older models are supported even more important.

### Standards: humanoids get rules

On 28 February 2026 in Beijing, MIIT’s national technical committee on humanoid robots and embodied intelligence (TC08) presented China’s first standards framework for the field. It covers six areas: foundational standards, “brain” and intelligent computing, limbs and components, complete systems, applications, and safety and ethics. More than 120 organisations worked on it, and the initial list contains 52 standards.

A framework is a skeleton, not a set of requirements for each model yet. But it matters: humanoids are moving from “experimental technology” to “equipment with requirements”. Within a year or two, buyers should be able to ask about compliance with specific standards rather than take things on trust.

### What changed for buyers

Four things. Choice widened: Chinese makers hold more than half their home industrial robot market and nearly all of the world’s humanoid shipments. Prices fell, but saving on the robot is not saving on the project. Models refresh faster, so ask how long your version will be supported. And rules appeared: standards and public reporting make offers easier to compare.

### What to watch in 2027

China’s full-year 2026 data (early 2027), full-year humanoid figures from analysts, the first standards from the list of 52, and how listed manufacturers report sales and margins. And prices: if the trend holds, conversations about humanoids on the shop floor will start less often with “how much?” and more often with “what will it do?”.
