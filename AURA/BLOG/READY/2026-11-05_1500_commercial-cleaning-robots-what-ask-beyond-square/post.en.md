Every commercial [cleaning](/en/catalog/cleaning) robot’s datasheet opens with square metres per hour. It is a handsome number. It tells you how much the robot would clean in an empty rectangular hall with no columns, doors or people, if nothing ever stopped it.

Shopping malls do not have halls like that. Neither do office buildings. Warehouses least of all.

At Aura Robotics we source cleaning robots directly from Chinese manufacturers, and almost every conversation starts with square metres per hour. Here are the questions to ask next. They are duller, and they decide whether the robot cleans or sits in the storeroom next to the old scrubber.

### 1. Area per hour: datasheet versus reality

Take a public spec sheet. Gausium’s Scrubber 50 has a theoretical maximum of up to 1,987 m² per hour with a disc brush. Its practical rate, according to Gausium itself, is 500 to 1,300 m² per hour, and the company says so openly. Pudu’s CC1 quotes 700–1,000 m² per hour.

Why the gap? The robot slows down at columns and shopfronts, steers around people, turns in narrow corridors and goes back to its station for water and power. The more complex the layout, the closer you get to the lower figure.

Plan with the practical number, or better, ask the manufacturer to estimate it on your floor plan.

Area per hour is speed. What you need is area per shift.

### 2. The shift versus the battery

The second number that matters is runtime. Scrubber 50 runs about 3 hours scrubbing (up to 8 hours dust mopping with a disc brush) and charges in about 2 hours. Its clean water tank holds 30 litres, the dirty tank 24.

Now some illustrative arithmetic. At a practical 800 m² per hour, three hours of scrubbing covers about 2,400 m² per charge, then it charges for two hours. If an eight-hour night shift must cover 6,000 m², one robot will not make it: it needs two full scrubbing cycles with a charge in between, plus travel and refilling. So: two robots, or part of the area cleaned by day, or night scrubbing combined with daytime dust mopping.

Count the whole shift, pauses included. A robot that scrubs for three hours and charges for two works fewer hours than the brochure suggests.

### 3. Floor types: which ones, and how much

A mall has several floors in one: porcelain tile in the galleria, ceramic tile in the food court, carpet in the cinema, rubber mats at the entrances, metal grates, escalators and travelators. A warehouse has coated concrete, joints and line markings.

Ask exactly what the model does: wet scrubbing, sweeping, vacuuming, carpet. All-in-one machines like the CC1 sweep, vacuum, scrub and clean carpets. Dedicated scrubbers cover large areas faster but should never meet a carpet.

Then list the places the robot will not go: escalators, stairs, narrow niches, corners. People still clean those. The robot takes the large, monotonous areas, not the whole job.

### 4. Lifts, doors and turnstiles

Multiple floors mean the robot needs the lift. That is solvable, but it requires integration with the lift equipment and the building manager’s approval. A public Gausium case study shows how it can work: at the FMZ Imst shopping centre in Austria, a Scrubber 50 was connected to a lift system around 15 years old and now cleans nearly 4,000 m² across three floors every night. An older lift is normal in many buildings, so ask who does the integration and how, not just whether the robot “can use lifts”.

The same goes for automatic doors, turnstiles and fire doors. Each must either know about the robot or stay open.

### 5. The station: water, drainage, charging

Without a self-service station, a scrubber needs a person to fill clean water, empty dirty water and plug it in. With an automatic station (Gausium’s WS-01, for example), the robot refills, drains and charges itself. The difference is between “the robot cleans” and “a person services a robot that cleans”.

A station needs space, a water supply, drainage and power. In a mall that is usually a utility room near the toilets. If there is not one on every floor, the robot spends time travelling. Include that in the shift calculation.

### 6. Consumables and upkeep

Brushes, pads, squeegees, filters and detergent. Ask how often they need replacing, where to get them and whether alternatives exist. Check which chemicals are compatible with the tanks and seals: not everything your cleaning contractor uses will suit the robot.

Daily upkeep usually means rinsing tanks and filters. A few minutes, but someone has to do it every day. Put that person’s name in the spec.

### 7. Reports: what the client actually pays for

This is often underestimated. Modern cleaning robots map the site and record which area was cleaned, when, and with what route coverage. For a cleaning company or mall operator, that is an argument with tenants and owners: not “cleaning was carried out” but a map, a timestamp and a coverage percentage.

Ask which reports are available, in what format, whether data can be exported and whether schedules can be set remotely.

A person says “I mopped everywhere.” A robot shows a map. The map is harder to argue with.

### 8. Day or night

At night the robot works in an empty building, faster and undisturbed. By day it cleans around visitors: slower, with detours, but right where the mess happens. Noise matters, as does how it warns people and how it behaves when a child decides to make friends.

For most commercial sites, a sensible pattern is main scrubbing at night and spot cleaning by day, robot and people together.
