It is 11:40 p.m. A guest on the seventh floor calls reception for a toothbrush. There is one person on the night desk. They can take the toothbrush up and leave reception empty, or ask the guest to wait until someone else arrives. Someone else arrives at 8 a.m.

A hotel delivery robot exists for exactly that moment. Not to impress guests, although it does, but to keep the receptionist at reception. The only question is how many such moments your hotel has in a month, and what they cost.

At Aura Robotics we source service robots directly from Chinese manufacturers and calculate payback before anyone buys anything. Here is how we do it for a hotel. We are leaving aside franchises and “hotels of the future”: this is about an ordinary hotel in Russia, with a lift, a corridor and one person on the desk at night.

### 1. What the robot actually does

A hotel delivery robot is a cabinet on wheels with lockable compartments, a lidar and a module that talks to the lift. It brings small items to rooms: towels, water, chargers, slippers, a room-service order, or a parcel a [courier](/en/catalog/courier) left at the front desk.

For scale, from public specs: Pudu FlashBot carries up to 30 kg (15 kg per tier) in 2–4 compartments, runs about 12 hours, charges in about 4.5 hours and calls the lift itself. Keenon W3 offers roughly 90 litres of space in 2–4 compartments, moves at 0.8 m/s and also connects to lifts.

What it does not do: carry suitcases, greet guests at the taxi or answer “where can I get a decent cheap dinner?”. Japan’s Henn-na Hotel, once famous for robot staff, retired more than half of its 243 robots in 2019 after complaints from guests and staff, who reportedly ended up doing extra work behind the robots. The luggage robots were among those let go.

A robot with one job works. A robot put in charge of hospitality looks lost.

### 2. China has already done the maths

In China, hotel robots stopped being a novelty a while ago. Yunji Technology, the largest player in China’s hotel robot market, listed in Hong Kong in October 2025. It reports robots in more than 34,000 hotels and a 2024 peak of 36,000 robots online on a single day.

That does not mean every hotel recovered its investment. It means the delivery robot has become ordinary equipment, like the kettle in the room. For a buyer, that is good news: these models have been run in tens of thousands of buildings, and everyone knows where they break.

### 3. Room count and floors

The first filter is simple. A robot saves time where walking is long: many floors, long corridors, reception in a separate building. In a 30-room, two-storey guesthouse the receptionist reaches any door in two minutes. The robot will get there too, just not faster.

Reasonable thresholds to start the calculation: a hundred rooms or more, five floors or more, regular late check-ins, a restaurant or room service. If none of these apply, the robot will mostly serve as a backdrop for photos in reviews. That is a job, but it does not belong in the payback model.

### 4. The lift is the real project

A delivery robot without a lift is a single-floor robot. So the key question is not “which model?” but “how does it get to the seventh floor?”

The robot calls the car through a module connected to the lift controller. That requires compatibility with your controller, consent from the lift maintenance contractor, and installation. With modern lifts it is usually a configuration task; with older ones it can be a project of its own. Find out before you buy: have the supplier and your lift company look at the control cabinet together.

Then there is traffic. At checkout time the lift is full of guests with suitcases, and the robot waits like everyone else. At night the lifts are free, which is exactly when the robot is most useful. That coincidence is the whole business case.

### 5. The night shift

Staffing is a real problem in Russian hospitality. At a Russian Union of Travel Industry press conference on 2025 results, the average staff shortage across the industry was put at around 30%. Night shifts are the hardest to fill, and the night receptionist cannot leave the desk.

Frame the task correctly. The robot does not replace the receptionist. It stops the receptionist from having to leave. These are different business cases: one counts a salary you will cut, the other a salary you will not need to add, and complaints you will not receive.

### 6. Payback with illustrative numbers

For arithmetic only. A 150-room hotel, nine floors. Around 15 room deliveries per night, each taking someone away from the desk for about 12 minutes. That is three hours a night with reception empty or a guest waiting.

Option A: add a second person on nights, at a fully loaded cost of 80,000 roubles a month.

Option B: a robot. The project, including lift integration and set-up, costs 2.5 million roubles. The robot covers night deliveries and takes some daytime runs from housekeeping and the restaurant, freeing another 40,000 roubles’ worth of hours a month. Maintenance is 10,000 a month.

Payback = 2,500,000 / (80,000 + 40,000 − 10,000) ≈ 23 months.

Now the honest adjustment. If you were never going to hire a second night person, the 80,000 line disappears and payback stretches to almost seven years (2,500,000 / 30,000 ≈ 83 months). In that case the robot pays back in reviews and a calmer night shift. That has value too, but count it separately and conservatively. (Illustrative numbers. Yours will differ, which is the point of using your own.)

A robot pays for itself not where it looks best, but where the alternative was hiring someone.

### 7. What else to check on site

Network: lidar finds its way without Wi-Fi, but tasks, the lift and reporting need stable coverage in corridors and in the car. Floors: thresholds, carpet, transitions, ramps. Doors: self-closing fire doors in corridors. Charging: where the dock sits and who can reach it. Reporting: trips, lift waits, failures.

And the doorstep scenario. The robot rings the room phone or sends a notification, the guest opens the door and takes the order from the compartment. If the guest is asleep, the robot goes back. Count those empty trips too.

### The bottom line

The guest who gets a toothbrush at 11:40 p.m. will not write in a review that your robot paid for itself in 23 months. They will write that the toothbrush arrived. The receptionist who did not have to run between floors will write nothing at all. They will simply not quit.
