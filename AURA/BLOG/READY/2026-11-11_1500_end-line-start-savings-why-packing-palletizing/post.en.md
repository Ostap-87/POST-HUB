Every plastics or household chemicals plant has a spot where automation stops. Up to that point everything runs by itself: injection moulding machines, blow moulders, filling lines, labellers, all with sensors, PLCs and touchscreens. After it stands a person, putting canisters into cardboard cases and cases onto a pallet.

That spot is called the end of the line. It is where expensive machinery meets a person whose shift is about to end.

The paradox is that the end of the line is usually automated last, even though it is one of the first projects to pay back. Here is why, and how to run the numbers.

### 1. Why payback comes fast here

End-of-line work fits a robot almost perfectly.

The task is simple and repetitive. Pick a case, place it on the pallet in a set pattern, repeat. No “we’ll figure it out on the day.”

The product is uniform. Cases of one size, bags of pellets, shrink-wrapped bundles. Even with a wide product range, the number of pack formats is usually small.

The work is continuous. Injection moulding, extrusion and filling lines often run around the clock, because stopping and restarting costs money in itself. That means the end of the line needs a person every shift, nights and weekends included. A robot’s payback depends directly on how many hours a week it works. Here, it works all of them.

And the load is heavy. Which is no longer just a question of economics.

### 2. The arithmetic of the lower back

A case of twelve one-litre bottles of washing-up liquid weighs about 12–13 kg. A bag of polymer pellets weighs 25 kg.

Russian labour regulations (Ministry of Labour Order No. 629n) set lifting limits for women: 7 kg for continuous lifting throughout a shift, 10 kg when alternating with other work (up to twice an hour), and a total of 350 kg per hour from a work surface or 175 kg from the floor.

Now take the flow. A line producing 300 cases an hour at 12 kg each moves 3.6 tonnes an hour. Under the limits for women, that flow fails on the weight of a single case and on the hourly total, even with ten people on it. The limits for men are higher, but 3.6 tonnes an hour, shift after shift, is not a job description. It is a back injury with a schedule.

In practice these stations are staffed by men, turnover is high, and sick leave and back injuries rarely appear in the project business case. They should.

A palletizer does not get tired at the end of a shift. It has no idea that shifts end.

### 3. Speed to spare

Industrial palletizing robots are fast. For reference, the ABB IRB 460 handles up to 2,190 cycles an hour with a 60 kg load. Chinese palletizers cover the same class of tasks at a noticeably lower price. With a multi-pick gripper, a single cycle can place several cases at once.

For slow lines handling a few cases a minute, a [cobot](/en/catalog/cobots) palletizer is enough. It needs less floor space and, with a proper risk assessment, may not need full fencing.

The bottleneck at the end of the line is almost never the robot. It is case infeed, pallet changeover, stretch wrapping and forklift removal, and they must be designed together with the cell.

### 4. Why the project keeps getting postponed

The reasons are usually the same.

“We’re managing.” True, at the price of turnover and overtime that never show up in the production budget, because HR pays for them.

“We have lots of SKUs.” The range may be wide, but stacking patterns usually number in the dozens, not hundreds. Modern cells switch pattern recipes from the operator panel.

“There’s no space.” A two-pallet cell does take space, but often less than today’s manual area with its aisles, temporary stacks and the supervisor’s desk.

“It’s not core equipment.” Which is why the money goes to a new blow moulder first. A year later it turns out the new machine produces more than the end of the line can pack.

A new machine without a new end of line is a six-lane motorway that ends at a garden gate.

### 5. An illustrative calculation

All figures are illustrative.

The line runs 24/7, which takes four crews. Each crew has two people at the end of the line: one packing product into cases, one stacking cases onto pallets. Eight people in total, each costing 100,000 roubles a month fully loaded.

**Option 1: palletizing only.** A robotic palletizer with gripper, infeed conveyor, two pallet stations, guarding, integration and training: around 6 million roubles. It replaces four palletizer operators, one per crew. Savings: 400,000 a month; maintenance: 30,000. Payback: 6,000,000 / 370,000 ≈ 16 months.

**Option 2: case packing plus palletizing.** Add a case erector and a packing robot, and the project grows to around 13 million. Each crew keeps one operator at half their time. Savings: 8 − 2 = 6 positions, 600,000 a month; maintenance 50,000. Payback: 13,000,000 / 550,000 ≈ 24 months.

Neither option counts the things that are hard to price in advance but easy to see afterwards. Consistent stacking means less transit damage. The line no longer stops at shift change. Fewer people go on sick leave with back problems. And the people you free up are needed elsewhere, because manufacturers are short of hands. According to Rossiyskaya Gazeta, Russian manufacturing had about 40 robots per 10,000 workers in 2025, against a 2030 target of 145. The end of the line is the shortest path from one number to the other.

Note that Option 1 pays back faster. It makes sense to start with palletizing and add case packing as a second phase.

The end of the line is the last place in the plant where products are still carried by hand. After that, the next hands they reach belong to the customer.

At the very least, the last case before the forklift should be lifted by someone whose back does not hurt.
