Mining is the one industry where “going down to work” is a job description rather than a mood. It is also the industry where the Chinese government has decided, in writing, that fewer people should be going down at all.

In 2024 seven Chinese ministries, led by the Ministry of Emergency Management, issued guidance on “intelligent” mines. The 2026 targets are refreshingly blunt: robots and intelligent equipment should take over at least 30% of hazardous and heavy jobs in coal mines and at least 20% in other mines, the underground workforce should shrink by more than a tenth, and intelligent capacity should reach 60% of coal output.

At Aura Robotics we source robots directly from Chinese factories, so we read programmes like this as buyers: what already works, what is still a demo, and what could realistically go into a Russian mine.

### A catalogue that grows faster than the tunnel

China approached mining robots the way a school approaches a curriculum: it wrote the list of subjects first. In 2019 the coal mine safety regulator published its first priority catalogue of 38 robot types in 5 groups, covering tunnelling, extraction, transport, processing, safety and rescue. By September 2025, according to Chinese business media, the updated catalogue had grown to 56 types in 7 groups.

Real-world selection ran in parallel. In April 2024 the mine safety regulator and MIIT picked 66 typical mining robot applications, 55 of them (about 83%) in coal. In May 2026 the same agencies launched a pilot covering seven scenario types, each requiring six to nine months of validation in real conditions.

Six to nine months at the coal face is an honest test. A robot that survives half a year of coal dust is no longer an exhibit. It is a colleague.

### The numbers

In November 2025 CCTV reported 1,806 intelligent longwall faces at 907 coal mines, with labour productivity up about 20% on average and more than half of coal capacity already classed as intelligent. By April 2023 the National Energy Administration had counted nearly 200 billion yuan invested in smart mining.

Shanxi, the country’s coal heartland, leads. According to China Daily (September 2025), it has 298 intelligent mines holding 65.5% of operating capacity, planned 130 more in 2025, and aims to bring all normally operating mines up to the standard by 2027.

The robot market itself is smaller than the slogans. One estimate cited by business outlet Huxiu puts 2025 Chinese demand for intelligent mining robots at 2,967 units worth 2.81 billion yuan. That is a young market, and for a buyer it means fewer standard models and fewer catalogue prices than in warehouse robotics.

### What actually works underground

**Rail-mounted inspection robots.** The most mature category. At the Tashan mine in Shanxi, run by Jinneng Holding, robots travel along overhead rails beside conveyors, recording thermal images, video and sound, sending data over 5G and docking themselves to recharge. A technician told China Daily he used to walk more than a dozen kilometres underground per shift. The robot walks them now. The mine says unmanned operations have cut 108 positions.

A conveyor several kilometres long is the perfect robot job: long, dull and dangerous. Everything people dislike, the robot does without comment.

**Driverless haulage in open pits.** At Zhunneng’s Heidaigou pit, 17 driverless trucks run on laser and 5G guidance with lidar and radar braking. Safety drivers left the cabs in May 2023, and by April 2025 the fleet had covered 710,000 km without an accident. The deputy mine director puts their productivity at about 85% of a human driver.

Eighty-five per cent is refreshingly honest. The robot is still slower than a person. It is also working through blizzards, at night, with no annual leave.

**Remote-controlled heavy equipment.** At one Shanxi pit a single operator runs two drilling rigs from six screens. The operator reports roughly 10% higher efficiency and a 60% drop in rig failures.

**Humanoids and quadrupeds.** More direction than practice so far. In 2025 Unitree signed an agreement with Shanxi Coking Coal Group and Shanxi Keda Control to develop embodied-AI mining robots. Expect the video of a [humanoid](/en/catalog/humanoid) striding down a tunnel well before the certificate allowing one into a methane mine. That is the natural order of things.

### The detail that matters most: explosion protection

Any robot heading into a coal mine first has to answer one question: will it ignite the methane?

In the Eurasian Economic Union this falls under technical regulation TR CU 012/2011 on equipment for explosive atmospheres. Equipment for underground workings with firedamp and coal dust is Group I, with its own protection levels. Chinese certification (the MA mark for coal mining plus national Ex certification) does not replace EAEU conformity assessment; the equipment is assessed again.

Three practical points follow. An explosion-proof robot is heavier, slower and dearer than its civilian sibling, so comparing their prices is pointless. Every component going underground needs checking, including the battery, charger, radio and cameras, not just the housing. And certification time belongs in the project plan from day one, not in a surprised phone call from customs.

With explosion protection, the best thing a robot can do is have nothing happen.

### What transfers to Russia

Three areas are realistic today. Open-pit mining: driverless haulage, remote drilling, conveyor inspection on the surface, where frost and dust are guaranteed and the operating temperature range belongs at the top of the spec. Surface facilities and processing plants: conveyor galleries, crushers and substations, where wheeled and rail inspection robots face conditions close to Chinese ones. And underground inspection, but only with robots that already have explosion-proof versions and can pass EAEU assessment. That road is long, and it is best started with one conveyor rather than the whole mine.

### The bottom line

For centuries mining has had one rule: come back up. China is now seriously counting how many people need not go down at all. The answer is still smaller than anyone would like. But every robot on a conveyor is a person who went to a control desk today instead of a danger zone.
