At a trade show a robot behaves like a child visiting relatives: polite, obedient, drops nothing and smiles at everyone. At home, meaning in your warehouse, it may turn out to be a different child. The buyer’s job at a show is to work out what it will be like at home.

Chinese robotics shows have become enormous. The Robotics Show (RS) at the China International Industry Fair in Shanghai in September 2025 covered about 56,000 square metres with more than 350 robotics companies, and the [humanoid](/en/catalog/humanoid) zone alone featured 27 models. CIIF 2025 as a whole covered about 300,000 square metres with nearly 3,000 exhibitors. You cannot walk a show like that in a day. Or two. In three you can walk it, but you will understand nothing.

So a trade show is not a stroll. It is an operation.

### Before you go: why are you going?

The first question is for yourself, not the manufacturer: what problem are you there to solve? “To see what’s out there” is an answer, but it brings home 200 business cards and no decisions.

Put the task in a line or two: the operation, the item, weight, cycle time, environment. Bring photos and video of the area, drawings or small sample parts, and numbers: shifts, headcount on the operation, safety requirements. A manufacturer shown a video of your line talks very differently from one told “we need something for the warehouse”.

Draw up a shortlist: five to ten must-see manufacturers and another ten if time allows. Hall maps are published in advance. Book meetings with key exhibitors before you travel; at peak hours a walk-up gets a salesperson, while a booked meeting may get an engineer.

A prepared buyer learns more in an hour on one stand than an unprepared one does in three days at the show.

### Production model or concept?

The most important question is simple: “Can I buy this?” Shows are where manufacturers display the future, and the future is not always for sale. A production model, a pre-series prototype and a concept built a month before the show for the photos all look the same.

Ask: since when has it been in series production, how many units have shipped, what is the current lead time, is it on the price list? If the answer is “soon”, write “concept” in your notebook.

### Autonomy or teleoperation?

The second question is awkward but essential: “Is the robot working on its own right now?” At Tesla’s We, Robot event in October 2024, Optimus robots served drinks and chatted with guests; Bloomberg and The Verge then reported that some functions were remotely operated by people, which a Tesla official later acknowledged.

Teleoperation is not fraud in itself; it is how training data is collected and how demos are made safe. But a buyer needs to know which of the things they saw the robot will do at their site without an operator. Look for someone nearby with a controller or a VR headset. Ask directly. Ask for the action to be repeated with a different object or in a different spot. A robot that knows one sequence will repeat it perfectly. Ask for something slightly different and you will see what it can really do.

The robot on the stand always works perfectly. The question is how many people are behind the stand.

### Questions to write down in advance

Print the list. By hour three of a show, the brain refuses to remember anything except where the coffee is.

**The task.** Have you done a similar operation for another client? Can I see video from a real site rather than a show? What part of our task can the robot not do?

**Specs.** Payload at what reach? Battery runtime in a real cycle, not on paper? Temperature range? IP rating?

**Software.** Open SDK? ROS 2 support? Interface languages? Who programs it for our task: you, us or an integrator?

**Export.** Have you shipped to our region before? Which certificates and documents do you have? EXW or FOB? Payment stages?

**Service.** Which spares should we keep on site? Lead time for parts? Remote diagnostics? How many years will you support this model?

**Factory.** Where is production? Can we visit after the show? Where is the pre-shipment test done?

That last question matters more than it seems. The stand comes down on Friday night. The factory stays.

### What to take from the stand

Not brochures; they are on the website anyway. Take the contact details of a specific engineer or export manager (ideally on WeChat, the main working channel), a photo of the spec plate, a short video of the robot working shot on your own phone rather than the promo reel. And write three lines of impressions in your phone straight away. By evening every stand merges into one big stand with blue LEDs.

### After the show: the real work starts on Monday

In the first week back, send your shortlisted manufacturers the task description and ask for a quote with a specific configuration. Compare quotes on equal terms: same payload, same delivery terms, same commissioning scope.

Then visit the factory if you can. A show proves a company can build a beautiful stand. A factory shows whether it can build robots in series.

### The bottom line

A show displays the best a manufacturer can do. That is useful, but not enough: you will be buying the robot’s ordinary working day, not its finest minute.

The trade show is the first date. The factory is meeting the parents. Decide after the second.
