A solar panel is a remarkably honest device. Whatever light falls on it, it gives back. If dust falls on it, it honestly gives back less. Dust never sends an invoice. It just quietly takes its percentage.

To get that percentage back, panels are cleaned. On a small plant a crew does it with mops and a water tanker. On a plant of several hundred megawatts that means hundreds of thousands of modules, and the crew with mops becomes a department with its own vehicle fleet. That is where robots come in.

At Aura Robotics we source robots directly from Chinese factories. In solar, China is at once the largest panel maker, the largest market and the largest test bed for the robots that look after the panels.

### A scale that needs robots

According to China’s National Energy Administration, the country installed a record 315.07 GW of solar in 2025, taking cumulative capacity to 1.20 TW, up 35.4% in a year.

A terawatt is hard to picture. Try this: at roughly 600 W per panel, that is about two billion modules (a rough count; older panels are smaller, so the real number is higher). [Cleaning](/en/catalog/cleaning) each one by hand once a month would need an army, and China has had some serious armies.

Russia’s scale is different: the Association for the Development of Renewable Energy reports 677.6 MW of new renewable capacity in 2025, including solar plants in Krasnodar, Chechnya and Dagestan. The problems are the same: dust, birds and, in most regions, snow.

### How much dust costs

In September 2025 the IEA’s photovoltaic programme (IEA PVPS) published a fact sheet on soiling. It estimates soiling accounts for 4–7% of global PV energy losses and costs the industry billions of euros a year.

It adds an important caveat: over-cleaning wastes money too, and schedules should balance recovered output against cleaning cost. Good news for anyone with a spreadsheet: a robot need not clean every day. It needs to clean when it pays.

Dust is the only member of staff who works every day for free. The robot is there to give it some competition.

### Types of cleaning robots

**Rail-mounted waterless robots, one per row.** Fixed to a row, they sweep its full length with microfibre brushes, usually at night or dawn when the plant is idle. No water, no operator, and a small onboard panel recharges them. The classic reference is Israel’s Ecoppia, which built such systems for desert plants; Chinese makers offer their own. In early 2026, Huzhou-based LEAPTING agreed to supply more than 2,000 G1 waterless robots to three Waaree Renewables plants in Rajasthan totalling about 1.2 GWp.

**Transferable robots.** One robot serves several rows, moved by hand or on a transfer cart. Cheaper per megawatt, but needs a person.

**Wet brushing machines** on tractors or self-propelled chassis. Not quite robots, but sometimes better where rows are far apart.

The right choice depends on the plant: rail robots need long, continuous rows at a uniform tilt, trackers need a compatible model, and on a patchwork site with short rows the rail-robot economics may not work.

### Robots that install panels

Cleaning is operations. But robots have reached solar construction too.

At the 440 MW Culcairn plant in Australia, developed by France’s Neoen, LEAPTING mounting robots were used. Manufacturer figures reported by pv magazine in March 2025 put the robot at about 60 modules an hour, against roughly 90–120 per eight-hour shift for a worker by hand; more than 350,000 panels had been installed by then.

In Tibet, on a 300 MW project in Chamdo at over 4,300 m, local media reported in May 2026 that four installation robots were being trialled, each fitting about 100 panels an hour, roughly twice as fast as people. Where the building season lasts about five months a year, that is more than an economic argument.

An installation robot does not mind the altitude. It just charges more slowly in the cold, and that is its only complaint.

### Inspection

The third category is inspection. Thermal drones fly the field to find hot spots: damaged cells, failed diodes, poor contacts. Ground robots check inverters and the plant substation. For a buyer this is the simplest case: payback comes from defects found and shorter inspection rounds.

### What to consider in Russia

**Snow.** Most cleaning robots are designed for dust, not snow. A brush will clear light dry snow, but not wet snow or ice, and a robot trying to drive on an icy rail risks breaking itself. Ask directly about the operating temperature range, behaviour in snow and ice, and whether there is a winter parking mode.

**Southern dust.** For steppe plants, waterless cleaning is a strong argument: water there is expensive and has to be trucked in.

**Plant layout.** Row spacing, length, tilt and mounting type. The robot is chosen for the plant, not the other way round; on an existing plant, check whether rails can be fitted without altering the structures.

**Communications.** Robots usually run over the plant’s radio network and the maker’s cloud. Decide early where data is stored and whether the system works without an external server.

### Calculating payback

For cleaning, the formula is: annual output recovered by cleaning, minus the cost of robots, maintenance and brush replacement, compared with manual cleaning including water, labour and transport. An illustrative example: if soiling costs a few per cent of annual output and manual cleaning happens less often than it should because it is expensive, the robot wins not by being cheap but by being frequent. Real numbers depend on region, tariff and plant design, and we calculate them for each project.
