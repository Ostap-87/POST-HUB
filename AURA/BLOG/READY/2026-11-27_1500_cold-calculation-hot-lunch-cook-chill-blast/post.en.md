In a central kitchen, lunch is not cooked for lunch. It is cooked for Thursday. That is the first thing to understand about cook-chill: the food does not cool down, it is chilled. The difference is roughly the one between “running late” and “arriving early”. Same clock, opposite outcome.

At Aura Robotics we source food processing and central kitchen equipment directly from manufacturers in China, and a blast chiller appears in almost every such project. It is also almost always underestimated, because it looks like a very cold fridge. It is not, and here is how to size one properly.

### 1. Two methods, one idea

**Cook-chill:** cook the dish, chill it fast to about 0–3 °C, store it cold for a few days, then regenerate (reheat) before service.

**Cook-freeze:** the same, but the product is frozen fast to −18 °C or below and stored for weeks or months.

Both separate production from consumption. Cooks work one planned shift in large batches; outlets reheat and serve. The kitchen stops living to the rhythm of the lunch queue and starts living to a production schedule.

A central kitchen without fast chilling is just a big kitchen with a very long hot-food delivery route.

### 2. Fast, not just cold

Food left to cool on a bench passes slowly through the range where bacteria multiply fastest; equipment makers put it at roughly +8 to +68 °C. Cooking does not kill everything either: spores of *Clostridium perfringens*, for example, survive heating and wait patiently for a pot to sit on a windowsill for a couple of hours.

Hence the targets. The Food Safety Authority of Ireland’s guidance on cook-chill systems sets them out clearly:

- cook to a core temperature of 75 °C (or an equivalent time/temperature combination);
- start chilling within 30 minutes of cooking;
- reach +3 °C or below at the core within a further 120 minutes, so no more than 150 minutes after cooking;
- keep portions to about 2.5 kg and no more than 100 mm deep;
- store at +3 °C or below;
- shelf life of no more than 5 days, including production day and service day;
- regenerate to at least 70 °C at the core, and only once.

That explains the figure on almost every blast chiller datasheet: “+90 °C to +3 °C at the core in 90 minutes”, and for freezing “+90 °C to −18 °C in 240 minutes”. That is a test condition, not a law of nature. More on that below.

A fridge keeps cold things cold. A blast chiller makes hot things cold. Mix them up and you are storing a problem at the correct temperature.

### 3. The Russian rulebook

Since 1 September 2026 Russian catering has operated under new sanitary rules, SanPiN 2.3/2.4.4282-26, approved by the Chief State Sanitary Doctor on 2 June 2026. They fully replace SanPiN 2.3/2.4.3590-20, run until 1 September 2032, and keep HACCP-based production control mandatory.

For food manufacturers the same obligation sits in the Customs Union technical regulation TR CU 021/2011 on food safety: the manufacturer must develop, implement and maintain procedures based on HACCP principles. Article 15 of that regulation also requires process equipment to carry control instruments where needed.

The practical upshot: a business fixes its chilling, storage and regeneration regimes and the shelf life of its dishes in its own technical documentation and production control programme, and has to be able to prove them. A chiller that chills but records nothing leaves you with good food and no evidence. Agree the exact parameters for your menu with a food technologist and a regulatory specialist before you buy, not after the first [inspection](/en/catalog/inspection).

### 4. Equipment, from cabinet to tunnel

**Blast chiller/freezer cabinets** are the workhorses of small and mid-size kitchens. Capacity is quoted in GN 1/1 or 600×400 levels and kilograms per cycle. A typical catalogue example: a 10-level cabinet chills 45 kg per cycle and freezes 28 kg. Freezing always “weighs” less than chilling.

**Roll-in blast chillers** take a whole trolley straight from the combi oven: fewer transfers, less handling, less time between cooking and chilling.

**Tunnel and spiral freezers** mean industrial scale: product rides a conveyor and comes out frozen. At this point a central kitchen starts to look like a ready-meal plant and should be costed like one.

Add cold rooms at 0–3 °C and −18 °C with spare volume, **regeneration** in combi ovens or regeneration trolleys at the outlets, and **temperature logging**: probes, loggers and data export.

### 5. Sizing chilling capacity

This is where mistakes happen. Blast chilling is sized for the busiest hour, not the daily total.

An illustrative example. A kitchen produces 1,500 portions a day at 350 g each: 525 kg. A 45 kg cabinet, allowing for loading, unloading and defrost, runs a cycle in about two hours. Eight hours of hot production give four cycles, or 180 kg. So you need three cabinets. But if 60% of output leaves the hot section in the first four hours, that peak means 315 kg in four hours, and now you need four. (Illustrative numbers, for arithmetic only.)

The second thing people forget: rated kilograms assume a certain product depth, load and room temperature. A thick sauce 100 mm deep and a stew 40 mm deep chill differently. A cabinet next to the combi ovens in a hot kitchen does not perform like one on a test bench.

There is no such thing as too much blast chilling. There is enough, or there is a queue of hot gastronorm pans.

### 6. What to ask the factory

1.  For what product, depth and room temperature is the per-cycle capacity rated?
2.  How many probes, and can different products chill on their own probes?
3.  How are cycle data recorded and exported?
4.  What refrigerant, and is the condenser built-in or remote?
5.  How does defrost work, and how much time does it take between cycles?
6.  What steel is the chamber made of, and what are the gaskets made of?
7.  Which spare parts should sit on site, and how long do the rest take?

### 7. The China angle

In March 2024 six Chinese agencies, led by the State Administration for Market Regulation, issued the first official definition of “prepared dishes”. The headline requirement: no preservatives, so shelf life has to come from process and cold chain. Central kitchen supplies for a chain’s own outlets fall outside the definition. The lesson for a buyer: Chinese factories build chilling equipment for a market where cold does the job of a preservative.

### The bottom line

Cook-chill is a strict discipline. It does not forgive “we’ll chill it later” or “let it stand a bit”. In return it gives predictability: a production plan, a steady shift and less waste.

A good central kitchen cooks today. It just thinks about Thursday.
